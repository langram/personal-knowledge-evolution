### —An "Anti-Dark-Forest" Self-Fulfilling Science Fiction Novel (First Draft Manuscript)

## Chapter 1　"Qin Jingwei" at the Gym

Spring in 2026 arrived suffocatingly humid. Dragging a half-worn carry-on suitcase behind me, I rented a short-term studio in a southern Chinese coastal university city—a modest room whose window framed a sliver of the sea.

First, a word about myself. My surname is Lang; my given name is a single character, Tu. I am in my early forties, Chinese, and have been single my entire life. My parents passed away years ago; I have no wife, no children, and haven't held a regular job in a long time. The savings sitting in my bank account wouldn't even cover the corner of a down payment on an apartment in a first-tier city, let alone raise a family. Fortunately, feeding one mouth costs next to nothing. My material desires are minimal, I have no taste for luxury, and between modest interest and frugal living, my savings stretch easily enough that I no longer have to sell the ten sharpest hours of every day to some boss.

With my time entirely my own, I do only three things: lift weights, travel, and brood over ideas alone.

Calling it "brooding" is perhaps a touch modest. Over the past few years, watching artificial intelligence surge forward day after day—large models writing code, proving math problems, generating art and video with frightening fluency—the news has turned surreal. One day a tech giant lays off thousands of white-collar employees; the next, an embodied-AI startup trains factory robots to replace blue-collar workers; meanwhile, nations bristle at one another with ever sharper hostility. Lying awake at night listening to the sea breeze, half-formed thoughts kept circling inside my head: human technology is sprinting ahead at breakneck speed, yet our social organization, power hierarchies, and distribution rules remain frozen inside centuries-old pyramids. Sooner or later, something is going to snap. Could humanity redesign society the way engineers build open-source software—no bosses, no black boxes, running on transparent public information and dynamically weighted collaboration?

The ideas stayed fragmentary, like wet clay that refused to hold a shape. I had no intention of boring anyone with them—a jobless middle-aged bachelor lecturing dinner companions on a "new civilizational system" only sounds like a crank.

Then, one afternoon at a neighborhood gym beside the university town, I met Qin Jingwei.

It was past four o'clock and the weight room was quiet, the metallic clink of iron plates ringing crisp in the air-conditioning. I had just finished four sets of deadlifts and was sitting on the edge of a bench catching my breath when a young woman walked up to the squat rack beside me.

At first glance, she looked like an Asian college student in her early twenties—not tall, but lean and compact, with crisp muscle definition across her shoulders and back, her hair pulled into a high ponytail, wearing a faded slate-blue athletic tank top. She loaded the barbell with practiced ease, braced her spine ramrod straight, and paced her breathing with a steadiness rarely seen in students who only came to snap gym selfies.

Finishing her set, she racked the bar cleanly and caught me staring blankly at her plates. One corner of her mouth curved upward. "Excuse me—mind if I borrow that pair of two-and-a-half-kilo plates by your feet?"

Her Mandarin was fluent, carrying only the faintest soft Southeast Asian lilt at the ends of her sentences, like fresh green mango sliced open on a southern summer evening.

"All yours," I said with a laugh, handing over the small iron discs. "Solid form. You've been lifting more than a couple of years, haven't you?"

"Mmm. Quite a long time, actually." She took the plates with remarkably steady wrists, and her gaze lingered on my face for two full seconds. It wasn't the polite caution of a twenty-year-old sizing up a stranger—it held an inexplicable familiarity, as though an old friend had stumbled across me on a distant street corner. "Good deadlifts yourself. You're not a local, are you? Haven't seen you here before."

"Just passing through for a quiet, warm stay."

"I'm in grad school at the university nearby," she added casually as she clipped the collar onto the bar. "Ethnic Chinese, from Thailand."

Working in on the free-weight area, one remark led to another, and conversation flowed effortlessly. She was astonishingly easy to talk to—and unusually sharp. Rather than chatting about milk tea, variety shows, or thesis advisors, she picked up a passing remark I made about gym mirrors being real-time feedback loops and spun it effortlessly into complex systems and information transparency. Privately I marveled: *Have Gen-Z grad students really gotten this wild with their reading lists?*

By six-thirty we were both starving. I suggested grabbing a bowl of Chaoshan beef rice noodles at an old corner eatery down the block, and she agreed without hesitation.

When two steaming bowls of bone-broth noodles hit the table, white vapor curling between us, I realized we hadn't even exchanged names.

"Forgot to introduce myself. I'm Lang Tu—*Lang* as in clear skies, *Tu* as in grand ambition, though neither grandness nor ambition applies to me at the moment." I stirred the chopped celery into my broth.

She took a delicate sip of Phoenix Dancong oolong from a celadon teacup and smiled faintly. "I'm Qin Jingwei."

My spoon clanked straight onto the rim of my bowl, and I nearly choked laughing.

"Wait a second… which *Qin*, and which *Jingwei*?"

"*Qin* as in the Qin dynasty, and *Jingwei* as in the mythical bird filling the sea," she replied, blinking with complete serenity.

I couldn't hold it in. Shaking my head, my middle-aged habit of running my mouth took over completely. "Young lady, forgive my bluntness, but your parents really have zero talent for picking Chinese names!"

"Oh? How so?" She rested her chin on her palm, gazing at me with amused curiosity.

"Look—they managed to pack the two most infamous names in Chinese history into a single combo!" I ticked them off on my fingers. "Ever since Wang Jingwei ruined his reputation, nobody in the Chinese-speaking world has dared name their kid *Jingwei*—just like nobody has named a child *Hui* since Qin Hui in the Song dynasty! And here you are, surnamed *Qin* and named *Jingwei*, as if someone gift-wrapped Qin Hui and Wang Jingwei together! Even setting history aside and sticking purely to mythology, wasn't Jingwei originally a little girl who drowned in the Eastern Sea? What an unlucky omen for a daughter!"

Most women in their early twenties would have rolled their eyes or turned icy at a middle-aged stranger roasting their name two hours after meeting.

Qin Jingwei didn't look even remotely offended. Instead, a strange, luminous gleam flickered deep in her eyes—like a scientist who had waited patiently beside an instrument panel until the needle finally swung onto the exact predicted mark.

"Lang Tu," she murmured, setting her teacup down with an enigmatic smile, "my parents didn't choose this name. I picked it myself. And your little rant just now… accidentally guessed half right."

"Picked it yourself?" I blinked. "Are Thai-Chinese students really into edgy historical rebellion these days?"

"The night is still young." Calmly lifting a tender slice of beef with her chopsticks, her eyes glanced playfully through the rising steam. "Once we finish these noodles, want to head back to that ocean-view studio of yours? I happen to have a bottle of homemade plum wine."

By forty-something, a man usually knows where he stands in the world. Catching my reflection in the mirror, steady training had kept the beer belly away, sure, yet the crow's-feet and scattered silver at my temples left no illusions—I was hardly some billionaire heartthrob who makes brilliant twenty-year-old grad students fall at his feet. Yet there was not a trace of cynicism, guile, or artifice about her; she felt like a clear, steady sea breeze, so natural that suspicion itself felt petty.

Later that night, ocean air drifted across the balcony as half a bottle of plum wine loosened our words and closed the distance between us. Once the lights dimmed, cautious calculations receded quietly into the background. What followed was ardent, unguarded, and uncannily harmonious—as if we were not strangers who had met beside a squat rack hours earlier, but old companions reconnecting along some unseen river of time.

Drifting into a deep sleep afterward, I dimly congratulated myself: *This southern trip paid off—running into an unburdened, no-strings-attached romance at forty-something means fate hasn't been stingy with me after all.*

I had no idea that by sunrise, what I took for a worldly fling would peel back five thousand years of human civilization right in front of my eyes.

## Chapter 2　Dawn Reveal: An Engineered "Close Encounter of the Third Kind"

I woke a little past seven. Morning breaks early by the ocean; the sheer curtains billowed softly in the breeze, throwing shimmering golden patches across the floor.

The space beside me was empty. Propping myself up on one elbow, I spotted Qin Jingwei standing barefoot by the balcony railing, wearing my oversized white cotton T-shirt and holding a ceramic mug, gazing quietly at a distant cargo ship gliding along the horizon. Morning light illuminated a flawlessly youthful profile, yet something in the stillness of her posture—watching the sea with timeless composure—sent a faint chill down my spine. She looked less like a twenty-year-old graduate student than an ancient spirit who had watched ten thousand tides rise and fall.

Hearing me stir, she turned and smiled. "Awake? I boiled water in your kettle and steeped some black tea."

I pulled on athletic shorts, stepped onto the balcony, took the mug, and sipped. "That plum wine packed a punch—I slept like a log. You're up early. Morning classes?"

"No. I passed my master's thesis defense the day before yesterday." Leaning lightly against the railing as sea wind teased loose strands of hair across her forehead, she looked steadily into my eyes. The playful spark of last night vanished, replaced by a clarity as sharp and calm as a surgical scalpel. "Lang Tu, while your mind is fresh, there's something real I need to put on the table."

My stomach gave a tiny jolt, and my middle-aged survival instincts flickered instantly toward my wallet and phone on the nightstand. "Put on the table? Don't tell me there was a hidden camera in the room last night, or you're deep in campus loan debt?"

She let out a bright laugh tinged with helpless amusement. "You 2026 Earth men—your vigilance never looks past wallets and honey traps."

"Earth… men?" I frowned and set the mug down on the small round table. "Look, it's seven in the morning. Can we skip the murder-mystery roleplay?"

"This isn't roleplay." Meeting my gaze without a flicker of theatrics, she spoke every word with crystalline calm. "Our encounter at the gym wasn't coincidence, and coming back to your room last night wasn't a spontaneous fling. It was a carefully orchestrated contact—what your sci-fi fans would call a 'Close Encounter of the Third Kind.'"

I stared at her for five full seconds before letting out a dry scoff. "So you're not a Thai-Chinese grad student—you're an alien?"

"To be precise, this body's phenotype and legal records really do belong to a Thai-Chinese graduate student, while my consciousness and civilization originate on an Earth-like planet dozens of light-years away." She tucked a strand of black hair behind her ear as casually as if reciting an ID number. "Measured in Earth's orbital years, I am over five thousand years old. Ever since bronze and early script first emerged along the Yellow River, the Nile, and Mesopotamia, I have watched nearly the entire evolution of human civilization unfold."

The morning sea breeze suddenly felt cool. Crossing my arms, I studied her clear, unblinking pupils—no manic glaze of delusion, no shifty micro-expressions of a con artist, only an almost chilling serenity.

"Alright, I'll play along." Pulling over a rattan chair and crossing one leg over the other, I leaned back like a spectator at a theater. "Elder Extraterrestrial of Five Thousand Years—you crossed dozens of light-years to live undercover on Earth. Instead of visiting the UN Secretary-General, Silicon Valley moguls, or Nobel laureates, you strolled into a modest community gym in southern China and slept with an unemployed middle-aged bachelor. Pray tell, what's in it for you?"

"Two things," Qin Jingwei answered, holding up two slender fingers with the crispness of a seminar presenter.

"First, a quick biological sample." She smiled with a candor so disarming it caught me off guard. "Our civilization mastered advanced **Horizontal Gene Transfer** long ago—not the primitive plasmid swapping Earth bacteria perform, but the deliberate uptake and integration of genetic segments from another organism to precisely remodel our phenotype, facial structure, metabolism, and apparent age, allowing us to blend seamlessly among native populations for millennia. Through last night's intimacy, I collected segments of your DNA. Once the identity of 'Qin Jingwei' graduates and closes tomorrow, I'll integrate selected markers from your genome to sculpt a fresh East Asian identity and move on somewhere else."

My mouth twitched. "So I just donated free genetic material to alien biotech? What's the second reason? Surely you didn't cross dozens of light-years just to sample my deadlift genes."

"The second reason is why I'm really here." Sitting across from me, Qin Jingwei leaned forward slightly, her gaze turning razor-sharp. "Lang Tu, over the past three years—alone in rented rooms, on overnight trains, jotting fragments into offline phone notes at three in the morning—you have obsessed over a single cluster of questions: *Why has human technology accelerated at dizzying speed in the AI era while political and economic institutions remain trapped inside feudal pyramids and rigid 'point-mass' game theory? Could humanity use an immutable content-addressed ledger like git to dismantle pointless privacy black boxes, separate basic personal sustenance from collective construction resources, replace governments and corporations with a brain-like dynamically weighted social neural network, and co-evolve a Large Civilization Model with AI?*"

A silent shockwave detonated inside my skull. Every hair on my forearms stood straight up.

The mug nearly slipped off my knee. I had never breathed a syllable of those ideas even to my oldest classmates—more than half had never touched an internet-connected file, living only inside an airplane-mode note app on an old spare phone or circling solely inside my own mind!

"You… you hacked my phone?!" My voice tightened instantly.

"Offline notes and faint cortical electromagnetic signatures are secrets to 2026 consumer hardware, yet to a civilization hundreds of millions of years past the technological singularity, they're as legible as billboards." Her tone held zero arrogance—only quiet sincerity. "We don't approach Silicon Valley billionaires or politicians because they are the chief beneficiaries of the old pyramid; their minds and incentives are welded shut. You, on the other hand—unencumbered, bossless, untamed by academia or corporate capture—stumbled independently onto the core puzzle pieces of a new civilizational order. All you lacked was the final spark to fuse scattered intuitions into a rigorous, actionable framework."

She paused, looking out over the waking southern city where glass towers and construction cranes caught the morning light.

"From the birth of the cosmos, matter has continuously emerged into greater complexity; intelligent life and civilization are cosmic inevitabilities. Because the Sun is a relatively young star, advanced civilizations had already crossed the civilizational singularity hundreds of millions of years before single-celled life even stirred on Earth. Along the road from infancy to singularity, every emerging world faces one mortal bottleneck: **when the technological singularity races far ahead of social and institutional evolution, the probability of self-destruction explodes exponentially.**"

"Earth in 2026 has one foot already on that cliff edge." Turning back, she studied me quietly. "Under the interstellar protocol for observing emerging worlds, if a planet's institutional growth keeps pace with its technology and the transition looks stable, we watch in silence and never interfere. Yet when institutional evolution stalls and self-annihilation looms near criticality, an undercover observer steps forward, finds a native already nurturing nascent thoughts close to the new architecture, lays out the complete reality, principles, and practical path of post-singularity civilization face to face, and lets that native write a theoretical treatise alongside a 'self-fulfilling science fiction novel'—using the twin levers of science and story to help the planet save itself."

Silence settled over the balcony, broken only by the hush of distant waves and the warm fragrance of steamed rice-noodle rolls drifting up from a breakfast shop below.

I stared down at my cooling black tea for a full minute. Common sense screamed that this was absurd; yet the electric chill of hearing her recite unvoiced thoughts straight out of my private mind felt undeniably real.

Exhaling slowly, I suddenly burst out laughing—the carefree laugh of a forty-something man with nothing left to lose and everything to discover. "Alright, Five-Thousand-Year-Old Alien Elder. Whether you truly hail from dozens of light-years away or happen to be the most brilliantly eccentric woman I'll ever meet, the door is open and the tea is steeped—let's go downstairs for dim sum. Tell me, piece by piece, what your post-singularity world actually looks like."

## Chapter 3　Earth Sci-Fi's "Poverty of Imagination"

The neighborhood dim-sum parlor down the alley was just swinging into morning rush, bamboo steamers exhaling clouds of fragrant vapor over shrimp dumplings, pork *siu mai*, and crispy red-rice rolls. Qin Jingwei ordered with the ease of a lifelong Cantonese local, rinsing bowls and chopsticks in boiling tea without wasting a single motion.

Dipping a shrimp dumpling in vinegar, I tossed out the blunt, everyday question nagging at me since the balcony: "Say, Qin Jingwei—mind if I keep calling you that for now? If your civilization is hundreds of millions of years ahead of ours and genuinely worried Earth might blow itself up before the singularity, why go through all this cloak-and-dagger theater? Five thousand years undercover, swapping genes, chatting up an unemployed guy in a coastal town to write a book? Why not park a massive mothership over the Pacific and broadcast a holographic seminar to all eight billion people? Wouldn't that work ten thousand times faster than anything I could write?"

Halfway through a translucent shrimp dumpling, Qin Jingwei nearly laughed out loud. Dabbing the corner of her mouth with a napkin, she shot me the fond, teasing look of an adult watching a child stack wooden blocks. "I knew you'd ask that. Lang Tu, have you ever noticed that Earth's science fiction—novels, films, comics alike—suffers from a truly astonishing **poverty of imagination** whenever it tries to picture the future or advanced extraterrestrial civilizations?"

"Hey, don't write off an entire genre," I protested on humanity's behalf. "Hollywood and sci-fi authors worldwide have been brainstorming for a century—still not creative enough for you?"

"Creative? All they do is slap a neon spacesuit onto the last two thousand years of Earth's own history!" Holding up one finger, she launched into an unapologetic roast. "Start with how Earth imagines alien contact—the exact same two or three recycled scripts every single time! Version one: a shiny flying saucer touches down, out steps a solemn 'peace envoy' in a suit or white robe trying to negotiate with Earth governments, panicked politicians and generals start shooting, and the envoy either flees in disappointment or retaliates with overwhelming violence. *The Day the Earth Stood Still*, *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*—stamped from the exact same mold."

"Version two is even blunter—*Independence Day*! Skip the talking altogether, hover a mothership over the White House, open fire with massive weaponry, and conquer humanity by brute force." She paused, tapping the table lightly as another memory surfaced. "Oh, and wasn't there a hit American sci-fi series just recently—starring Rhea Seehorn, the lead actress from *Better Call Saul*? ***Pluribus***, right? Now that one actually showed a spark of originality: instead of dropping bombs, the aliens transmit a virus-like biological signal that infects all of humanity into a single blissful collective consciousness, erasing conflict overnight."

"I caught clips of it," I nodded. "Pretty wild premise, wasn't it?"

"Clever premise, sure—yet underneath, it's the exact same arrogance and coercion!" Qin Jingwei shook her head, her eyes cooling. "It is still a **unilateral takeover that completely tramples the feelings and autonomy of individual Earthlings**! It's like an impatient adult annoyed by noisy children who injects an entire kindergarten with sedatives and calls it peace. Any genuinely advanced civilization that uses superior firepower or viral mind-control to forcibly remodel a younger species isn't just brutal—it is evolutionarily stupid. Tamed puppets lose diversity and can never generate authentic higher intelligence. The only true art is helping a native civilization grow a superior order out of its own informed, free rationality."

Taking a sip of Pu-erh tea, her critique gathered steam. "And don't even get me started on how Earth sci-fi imagines future **political and economic systems**—that part is truly tragic! One track is *Cyberpunk 2077* or *Blade Runner*: civilization can engineer cybernetic limbs and synthetic memories, yet society is somehow still ruled by mega-corporate oligarchies with obscene wealth inequality—gleaming neon skyscrapers towering over rotting slums. That's literally nineteenth-century industrial capitalism splashed with fluorescent paint."

"And the other track is even more absurd!" She spread her hands laughing. "Look at *Star Wars*, or *Dune*! Civilizations capable of faster-than-light interstellar travel, spanning thousands of star systems—and what governance are they running? Emperors, dukes, aristocratic bloodlines, feudal monarchies, or clunky ancient senatorial republics! *Dune* is literally medieval European feudalism blended with Arab desert history wearing stillsuits. A species that builds warp drives while kneeling before hereditary emperors makes as much sense as a calculus-solving woolly mammoth bashing its neighbor with a flint axe."

I burst out laughing—every jab landed straight on the nose. "Come on, plenty of sci-fi tries to go beyond lone emperors and feudal dynasties, like imagining interstellar collective minds."

"You mean the bugs in *Starship Troopers* or every generic space-opera hive mind?" Qin Jingwei shrugged with visible disdain. "That's the third form of Earthling laziness: the moment humans try to imagine intelligence beyond a single individual, all they can copy are the lowest biological collectives on Earth—ants and honeybees! One queen barking chemical orders while billions of mindless workers march to slaughter. That isn't higher collective intelligence; that's just cramming pyramidal dictatorship into insect pheromones!"

Around us the teahouse buzzed with morning chatter, retirees at the next table arguing animatedly over stock prices and geopolitics. Wisps of steam curled upward from bamboo baskets, framing the startling depth in this twenty-year-old woman's gaze.

"So a civilization that has truly crossed the singularity is neither a *Star Wars* empire, nor a *Cyberpunk* megacorp, nor a *Pluribus* or *Starship Troopers* hive mind," I said, setting down my chopsticks and looking at her squarely.

"Not remotely," Qin Jingwei replied, her voice softening with quiet conviction. "If Earth's present institutions are abacuses and steam engines, then Earth sci-fi's imagined futures merely plate the abacus beads in solid gold and paint the steam boiler laser-green. Since we have the whole day, let's not stay cooped up in this teahouse—come take a walk by the sea. Let me show you what happens when you step completely outside the old coordinate frame, and see how a civilization running smoothly for hundreds of millions of years actually treats life, privacy, and the mind."

## Chapter 4　Respec-ing Life: Why a 5,000-Year-Old Immortal Never Gets Bored

Stepping outside, the nine-o'clock sea breeze greeted us with warm, briny humidity. Along the southern edge of the university district stretched a long wooden coastal boardwalk—white surf slapping against dark volcanic boulders on one side, fiery crimson bougainvillea spilling over low walls on the other. With students in morning lectures, the promenade lay nearly empty save for a few retirees drifting slowly through Taiji forms.

Walking side by side for a few minutes, a stubbornly human question kept turning in my head until I glanced sideways at her. "Qin Jingwei, you said you're over five thousand years old. In almost every fantasy or sci-fi story I've ever read about immortality, nine out of ten immortals eventually grow sick to death of living—watching loved ones die generation after generation, repeating the same monotonous routines until numbness takes over and their one deepest wish is to die. You've lived five millennia, yet you look livelier than a forty-year-old guy like me. Doesn't endless time ever turn unbearably dull?"

Qin Jingwei stopped beside the wooden railing, tilted her head into the sea wind, and laughed so hard she nearly doubled over.

"Lang Tu, I just finished saying Earthlings lack imagination about the future, and you immediately volunteered a textbook demonstration!" Turning back with dancing eyes, she teased, "The only reason your stories paint immortality as a dreary curse that drives immortals to suicide is because you sneak an absurd hidden assumption into the premise—**you assume 'living forever' means staying trapped inside the exact same unchanging body, the exact same personality, and the exact same fixed settings, copy-pasting identical days for ten thousand years!**"

"Isn't that what staying yourself means?" I countered. "Even if you don't age, you're still *you*—once memories pile high enough, doesn't novelty inevitably run dry?"

"Who declared that 'I' must run forever on a single fixed hardware build and one static sensory profile?" Tapping a finger lightly against her forehead, then gesturing down at her lean shoulders, she smiled. "Remember the **Horizontal Gene Transfer** I mentioned this morning? In our civilization, immortality is never a petrified marble statue frozen in time—it is a **continuously editable biological journey where you can rewrite your own morphology and attributes at will**."

Gazing out across the sparkling blue water, her eyes lit up like a passionate gamer describing an all-time masterpiece. "Whenever I wish, I can exchange and assimilate genetic segments with fellow citizens—or, as I did last night, with Earthlings—integrating chosen sequences directly into my own genome to trigger horizontal genetic remodeling. I can reshape my face, apparent age, height, build, metabolic rhythm, and even tune the neurochemical gene expression that shapes temperament and emotional color!"

My jaw dropped. "Even your temperament… and biological sex?"

"Of course!" She spread her hands with breezy delight. "Tweaking facial features, height, complexion, or shifting between a twenty-year-old and a sixty-year-old physique is effortless—a few metabolic sleep cycles and you glide right into it. Switching **biological sex** involves deeper structural and endocrine reconfiguration, so it does feel rather like undergoing a 'rebirth.' Still, just imagine how exhilarating that is!"

Turning to face me, sea wind streaming through her ponytail, she practically glowed with vitality. "Across five millennia, I have lived as a lean, sharp-eyed northern youth, as a broad-shouldered seafaring merchant, as a serene middle-aged female scholar—and right now, as a twenty-something Thai-Chinese graduate student lifting weights and slurping beef noodles in southern China! Think of the massive RPG video games Earthlings love so much: inside a game, you can redesign your character's face anytime, adjust height and build, **respec** your strength, intelligence, and charisma stats, and roll a brand-new class and skill tree to experience the entire world anew. **Our immortality is an infinite open-world playthrough where you can respec your body, temperament, and way of life whenever curiosity strikes!**"

I stood frozen on the wooden boardwalk, the rhythmic crash of waves filling my ears as a lightning bolt split open a mental blind spot I hadn't even known existed.

Of course! Earth's writers portrayed immortality as torture because natural selection handed humans a non-editable, single-use character card with a hard expiration date; imagining eternity trapped inside one stiffening, unchangeable card naturally felt horrifying. Yet if continuous consciousness could ride across endlessly reconfigurable biological bodies—shifting stature, sensory acuity, sex, and emotional palettes to taste the universe from a thousand perspectives—what were five thousand years? Fifty thousand wouldn't be enough!

"Hold on," I caught myself, squinting at her with a wry grin as I sized her up from head to toe. "Going by that logic, last night—besides collecting DNA and delivering a civilizational spark—were you also casually playing a 'twenty-year-old Thai grad student has a one-night stand with a single middle-aged Chinese guy' character arc?"

Qin Jingwei blinked, neither coyly denying it nor brushing me off from on high; her frankness was as wide and bright as the morning sea. "Why—didn't you enjoy it? Being five thousand years old and still keeping an appetite for genuine human sensation and an intriguing, solitary mind beats turning into a frigid, self-righteous slab of space rock, doesn't it? For what it's worth, your genetic profile is surprisingly solid—great muscle recovery and calm stress-resilience markers. My next build will probably borrow quite a bit from you."

Her half-teasing, half-clinical compliment left me momentarily speechless before I burst out laughing. Whatever lingering awkwardness I had felt over being "sampled for alien DNA" dissolved cleanly into the salt air.

"Still, one huge piece doesn't sit right with me," I said as my laughter faded, pointing back at a phrase she had dropped earlier. "You claimed that in your civilization, nobody has any privacy at all—everything is open public knowledge. That forces me to ask a painfully blunt question that any normal Earth man in my shoes would ask right now—"

Stopping dead in my tracks, I locked eyes with her seriously. "If your civilization has zero privacy and total transparency, does that mean **every single detail of what happened in my bedroom last night is already live-streamed to a public archive where billions of aliens on your homeworld can search and watch the replay?!**"

## Chapter 5　Old Friends in Two Minutes: "Nudist Colonies," Brain-Computer Interfaces, and the Zero-Privacy Civilization

My sudden, painfully mortal question cracked Qin Jingwei up so hard she bent over the railing, laughing until tears shone at the corners of her eyes.

"Hahaha… Lang Tu, you really are a textbook 2026 Earthling!" Straightening up, she pointed toward a sleek glass-walled seaside tea lounge perched above the rocks nearby. "Come on, let's grab some air-conditioning and chilled coconuts. That's actually the single best question you could have asked—it hits the thickest psychological wall stopping Earthlings from imagining our world."

Inside the cool glass lounge, sunlight shattered like silver across the water beyond the floor-to-ceiling windows. Two chilled green coconuts arrived with straws. Taking a refreshing sip, Qin Jingwei smiled and tossed me a reassuring bone first. "First, relax: during undercover observation on Earth, we follow local cultural-adaptation etiquette so we don't traumatize uninitiated natives—so no, last night wasn't broadcast live onto a planetary jumbotron. Still, once my mission concludes and my full experiential memory syncs back home, anyone on my planet who cares to browse it certainly can. And I promise you, not a single soul will react with Earth-style voyeuristic leering or moral shaming."

"Yeah, easy for you to say!" I gripped my coconut, furrowing my brow deep enough to trap a fly. "Put yourself in an Earthling's shoes: every stupid remark you've ever made since childhood, every embarrassing mistake, every midnight web search, your bank balance, even every intimate moment in bed—dumped into a planetary database anyone can query at will? That sounds like George Orwell's *1984* on steroids! Just picturing it makes my skin crawl. How could anyone live like that without losing their mind?"

"You only get goosebumps because you've lived inside a clothed society for thousands of years and just heard about people walking around unclothed," Qin Jingwei answered calmly, dropping an analogy that stopped me cold. "You've heard of **nudist colonies** or **naturist villages** on Earth, haven't you?"

"Sure—places in Europe and North America where everyone lives naked."

"Think about it," she said, leaning slightly forward with crystal-clear eyes. "When an ordinary clothed person tries to picture life inside a naturist village, their head floods with embarrassment, shame, discomfort, or voyeurism—*how on earth can you face strangers naked every single day?* Yet look at the actual residents living there: facing unclothed neighbors day in and day out, they feel neither curiosity, nor shock, nor scandal—they simply treat it as the most natural, unremarkable fact of daily life. **In my homeworld civilization, living without privacy is simply a civilization-wide 'naturist village of the mind.'**"

Her voice grew firmer, locking straight onto the theoretical knot I had wrestled with for years. Even so, one rock-hard wall still stands straight in front of me—and in front of hundreds of millions of ordinary Earthlings who can never just shrug it off."

## Chapter 9　The "Chemistry" of Blood Feuds, the "Anti-Dark-Forest" Civilization, and the Taiji Yin-Yang

At four in the morning, a faint porcelain-blue band appeared where dark ocean met dark sky. The long night was finally beginning to ebb.

Pouring the last half-cup of warm tea for me, Qin Jingwei spoke more softly than before, yet every word carried a quiet, piercing weight. "Hold your hardest question for thirty seconds. Before you throw it at me, look at one more extraordinary thread woven through Qin Hui and Wang Jingwei's stories—one that reveals **what actually happens to human nature when sworn enemies finally break information isolation and truly understand each other**."

She tapped the wooden table lightly. "Check the historical record and you'll notice something striking: neither Qin Hui nor Wang Jingwei began life as a soft compromiser—quite the opposite! **Both started out as fierce hardliners, radicals, and hawks!** Before the fall of the Northern Song, Qin Hui passionately opposed ceding territory or installing puppet rulers—so fiercely that the Jurchen invaders took him north as a captive. And Wang Jingwei was among the most fiery radicals of the Tongmenghui revolutionary alliance, even risking his own neck to **assassinate the Manchu Prince Regent Zaifeng**—the exact arrest that inspired *'one swift stroke of the blade brings joy, worthy of a young man's head'!*"

"Then how on earth did two of the fiercest hardliners undergo such a dramatic reversal?" I leaned forward instinctively.

"Because **information isolation shattered—and they actually sat down face to face with their mortal enemies!**" Qin Jingwei's eyes shone like the morning star. "During his captivity in Jin territory, Qin Hui engaged in prolonged, candid exchanges with the Jin peace-faction statesman **Wanyan Chang**; after Wang Jingwei's arrest, he spent long nights in prison debating and conversing deeply with the Qing imperial statesman **Prince Su Shanqi**, whom he had once despised as a 'feudal relic blocking the revolution.' From sharp verbal sparring they moved into genuine mutual respect and kinship—Shanqi even fought to spare Wang's life. Once face-to-face communication tore down the caricatures and both sides saw another flesh-and-blood human being carrying real fears, burdens, and conscience, extreme tribal demonization melted into **the shared empathy of a single human species**! It was that cross-camp human empathy that turned both former hawks into moderates who came to believe **cooperation beats confrontation**."

She sighed softly, adding a cool institutional diagnosis: "Those ancient attempts still ended in tearing tragedy not because empathy and communication were wrong, but because the ancient world lacked universal transparent information channels and equal rules constraining unilateral aggression—genuine mutual understanding occurred only by fluke inside a prison cell or an enemy camp among a handful of elites, while millions outside stayed locked inside information silos of blood hatred. Yet even that tiny sliver of real dialogue was enough to melt the hardest ideological ice!"

Drawing a deep breath of cool pre-dawn sea air, I finally threw down the heaviest, rawest question burning in the hearts of millions of ordinary people and hardliners alike:

"Qin Jingwei, I hear the theory. Yet ordinary human beings—especially those scarred by loss—always slam straight into one brutal reality: **the murder of a father, the violation of a wife, the ruin of a homeland and family! These aren't petty misunderstandings over tea—how could anyone possibly brush off blood feuds like that and simply 'reconcile'?!**"

Silence lingered as surf hissed against the distant shore.

Qin Jingwei did not flinch, nor did she offer hollow saintly platitudes about turning the other cheek. Meeting my eyes directly, her expression turned deeply earnest, compassionate, and razor-sharp.

"Lang Tu, I would never feed you empty moralizing. **Even the reconciliations of Qin Hui and Wang Jingwei with their adversaries rested on a strict objective precondition: both sides carried—overtly or latently, in innate biological constitution and acquired cognitive outlook—the capacity for empathy, perspective-taking, and gentle tit-for-tat.** As your own proverb says, **it takes two hands to clap**. Just like a chemical reaction, peaceful synthesis requires compatible properties on both sides."

"As for the blood feuds you named—the murder of parents, shattered families, conquered homelands—you must trace them back to their **origin**." She laid it out with clinical precision. "How do catastrophic blood feuds actually start? In the vast majority of cases, they begin as manageable disputes between rational actors whose intense emotions, fed by **hardline tit-for-tat strategies**, escalate iteration by iteration—one punch answered by a knife, one raid answered by a massacre—until blood spills and neither side can step back. A smaller share comes from individuals burdened by innate biological deficits compounded by violent childhood environments, warping into antisocial personalities driven by pathological logic—which, strictly speaking, is a psychiatric disorder."

Setting down her empty teacup, she delivered a natural-science analogy that made my breath catch:

"Look closely: whether an encounter ends in swords beaten into plowshares—like those reconciliations—or spirals from minor friction into **'a commoner's wrath spilling blood five paces; an emperor's wrath littering the land with corpses,'** the underlying mechanism is identical! It is generated by the interaction of participants' **biological genes, psychological imprints, and social game-theoretic traits**—just as some chemical substances blend gently while others detonate with terrifying force. Strip away the mythology, and whether two human beings make peace or war comes down to **action and reaction** between divergent cognitive structures forged across innate nature and lived environment—no different from how the infinite variety of chemical reactions flows strictly from **different atoms' electron-shell configurations and outer valence electrons**!"

Holding my breath, I stared out at the lightening horizon as a blinding clarity broke over me.

"Then what?" I asked softly. "If certain atoms naturally react explosively, what is humanity supposed to do?"

"Does a chemist stand in the lab screaming that sodium is 'inherently evil and eternally damned' because it explodes on contact with water—or try to purge every sodium atom from the universe?" Qin Jingwei smiled warmly, eyes shining. "Of course not! Chemists study atomic electron shells carefully, master the scientific laws governing their reactivity, and **use those laws to engineer stable, safe, life-serving molecular compounds!**"

"Human civilization's future depends on cooperation, not confrontation!" Her voice rang luminous and unshakeable in the dawn air. "Yet a cooperative civilization is built *upon* recognizing human innate and acquired relativity—**never by trying to erase that relativity**, because action and reaction are inseparable twins, just like atomic properties in chemistry. The ultimate task of social science is to acquire real scientific knowledge of human cognition and game dynamics, and on that foundation **engineer stable cooperative civilizational structures**—propelling science and culture forward, crossing the singularity safely, and ushering in genuine *Datong*, the Great Harmony!"

Then her gaze sharpened like tempered steel, drawing an unmistakable moral and mathematical line:

"And remember one iron rule: **a genuinely sound cooperative game system must ensure that every participant's cooperation stems from full access to transparent information, independent rational reasoning, and completely spontaneous, voluntary, free choice!** Never confuse true cooperation with forced 'peace' imposed by an external authoritarian power, an empire, or a mind-control virus—because **control versus counter-control, and enslavement versus counter-enslavement, are themselves confrontation, never cooperation!**"

A quiet smile crossed her face as rosy dawn clouds ignited across the eastern sky. Suddenly, she circled all the way back to yesterday morning's dim-sum roast:

"By the way, Lang Tu—remember how we roasted Hollywood and Western sci-fi over dim sum yesterday? I deliberately saved the grandest, most influential monument of Chinese science fiction until right now: ***The Three-Body Problem***."

My heart gave a sudden thump. "Liu Cixin's *Three-Body*? You read that too?"

"Not only read it—having lived on Earth five thousand years, I read the entire trilogy carefully three times when it came out. The literary sweep and cosmic scale are genuinely staggering." Offering sincere admiration first, she then sliced straight to the core. "Yet examine the foundational worldview emblazoned right on the title of its second volume: ***The Dark Forest***."

"The Dark Forest theory…" I breathed softly.

"At bottom, **the Dark Forest theory is simply Earth's traditional law of the jungle extrapolated to its bleakest extreme at interstellar scale!**" Standing up into the first golden rays breaking over the ocean, Qin Jingwei's voice resonated like a clear bronze bell. "It assumes the entire cosmos is an utterly sealed, secretive **'universe of privacy'** where every species hides its coordinates and conceals its intentions! And inside that universe of privacy, the famous **'Chain of Suspicion'** that forces civilizations to exterminate one another on sight is—translated directly into the mathematical framework of your treatise—**nothing other than the 'negative chain reaction' triggered by information isolation and maximal privacy!**"

A chill of pure recognition shot down my spine as the final piece of the entire cosmic puzzle locked cleanly into place.

Of course! The axioms of *The Dark Forest*—cosmic sociology plus technological explosion plus the Chain of Suspicion—were literally **"a privacy universe + negative chain reaction"** pushed to cosmological infinity!

"And the interstellar network I hail from," Qin Jingwei turned back to me, sea wind lifting her dark hair as sunrise gilded her silhouette in pale gold, smiling brightly, "is literally an **'Anti-Dark-Forest' civilization**! If the universe were truly a merciless dark forest where exposure meant instant death, I wouldn't have spent five thousand quiet years watching Earth, nor spent this entire night drinking tea and handing you a spark—the moment humans invented radio, we would have casually tossed a photoid or a dual-vector foil your way! The cosmos isn't a dead graveyard precisely because intelligent species that actually survive the technological singularity eventually discover that **dismantling walls of privacy, severing the Chain of Suspicion with transparent public knowledge, and flipping the negative chain reaction into a positive cooperative chain reaction** is the only durable attractor across the stars."

"Lang Tu," she stepped closer and stopped right before me, five millennia of quiet urgency gathered in her gaze. "Think back over everything we talked through tonight. **If even Qin Hui and Wang Jingwei—figures condemned by Earth's history as its greatest traitors—could move toward choosing cooperation over confrontation once genuine mutual understanding broke through, why on earth can't modern humanity, armed with global networks and artificial intelligence, do the same?** If nations, corporations, and factions keep clinging to Dark Forest paranoia and ranking confrontation above cooperation—**now that AI and nuclear weapons have placed planetary annihilation in human hands, what ending awaits Earth's civilization?!**"

The question struck like a thunderbolt, leaving my chest tight and breathless.

Outside, a blazing crimson sun climbed clear of the sea, spilling molten gold across the dark swells.

Gazing out at the rising sun, Qin Jingwei's expression softened into deep tenderness, as though gently steadying this stubborn, brilliant young blue planet.

"Actually, the key to walking out of the Dark Forest has already been written inside humanity's oldest wisdom and boldest science. **From ancient China's intuitive philosophy of Taiji to Einstein's modern scientific theory of relativity**, humanity has repeatedly glimpsed **the cosmic universality of relativity**. Once you finally find the courage to embrace relativity inside social science itself—no longer trying to annihilate difference in pursuit of brittle uniformity, but letting human contrasts interlock and transform like your ancestors' **Taiji Yin-Yang fish, Yin nestled within Yang and Yang nestled within Yin**—you will step out of the Dark Forest, cross the singularity intact, and build a far more harmonious, radiant civilization with your own hands."

## Chapter 10　A Recursive Farewell: "Once Upon a Planet…"

Morning flooded the streets in bright golden light. After sharing two steaming bowls of freshly poached fish-slice congee at a corner stall, I caught a cab to drop Qin Jingwei off at the south gate of the university town.

Past nine o'clock, camphor trees shaded the campus boulevards as groups of chattering undergraduates streamed past with backpacks slung over their shoulders. Nobody glancing at this slender young woman in a white T-shirt, jeans, and canvas tote would ever guess she was older than all of China's recorded dynasties combined.

Stopping beneath the dappled shade outside the subway entrance, Qin Jingwei turned with a bright, unhurried smile. "This is far enough. Oh—before I go, I still owe you the final piece of the puzzle: who actually invented that legendary 'Protocol Two' singularity rescue method in the first place?"

"I was just about to ask," I chuckled, hands tucked in my pockets. "Since the very first 'Progenitor Singularity Civilization' had no older aliens ahead of them, when technology outran their institutions and threatened to blow their world apart, they couldn't exactly conjure an extraterrestrial out of thin air to teach them!"

"Right on target!" A spark of mischievous delight and deep admiration danced in her eyes. "In truth, the entire protocol I walked through with you—undercover contact, sharing the theory, prompting a native to write a theoretical treatise paired with a self-fulfilling sci-fi novel—**was originally invented by a lone genius scientist inside the cosmos's very first Progenitor Singularity Civilization!**"

I blinked in astonishment. "He invented it himself?"

"Yes!" she laughed softly. "When that progenitor world hurtled toward its own singularity, it faced the exact same lethal gap between runaway technology and stalled institutions. That genius scientist was the first being in the cosmos to uncover the mathematical and game-theoretic principles of the new civilizational order. He realized immediately that publishing a dry academic treatise alone would never persuade billions of citizens locked inside legacy habits and vested interests in time. So he hit upon an audacious stroke of genius—**he invented 'self-fulfilling science fiction'!** Alongside his treatise, he wrote a novel imagining an older, already-perfected alien civilization sending an undercover envoy to meet an ordinary native, lay out the blueprint, and save a planet on the brink of self-destruction."

"Powered by that twin engine of scientific treatise plus self-fulfilling sci-fi story, the people of the progenitor planet grasped, embraced, and fell in love with the new architecture—and genuinely crossed the civilizational singularity peacefully within mere decades!"

Winking playfully, Qin Jingwei spoke as brightly as if reciting a billion-year-old galactic nursery rhyme: "Once the progenitor species ventured out among the stars, they carried that legendary novel across the galaxy. Later post-singularity civilizations read it, slapped their knees in delight, and turned the originally fictional plot into an actual interstellar observation and aid protocol—whenever they spotted a young world like Earth about to capsize at the technological singularity, they literally sent an undercover envoy down to find a native incubating nascent ideas, tell the story all over again, and prompt a fresh treatise and companion sci-fi novel…"

Goosebumps raced up both my arms, and I burst out laughing aloud. "Hold on—in computer programming, isn't that an **infinite recursion**?! That's literally the Chinese childhood joke every kid grows up hearing: *'Once upon a time there was a mountain, on the mountain stood a temple, in the temple an old monk told a young monk a story—and what was the story? Once upon a time there was a mountain…'*"

"Spot on!" Qin Jingwei laughed wholeheartedly and recited its interstellar sci-fi edition:

> **"Once upon a time there was a planet, and on that planet an alien told a native the story of crossing the civilizational singularity. And what was the story? The story went: 'Once upon a time there was a planet, and on that planet an alien told a native the story of crossing the civilizational singularity…'"**

Morning sunlight danced through camphor leaves across her smiling eyes.

As her laughter faded, she dropped effortlessly back into the breezy, everyday cadence of a graduating college student and gave me a bright wave:

"Alright, Lang Tu. My master's thesis defense passed, I've officially graduated, and my flight back to Thailand leaves early tomorrow morning."

Both of us knew silently what tomorrow actually meant. Once the flight departed, the Thai-Chinese graduate student "Qin Jingwei" would vanish cleanly from human archives. Carrying the DNA markers gathered from me two nights ago, she would gently reshape her morphology through horizontal gene transfer—stepping into a brand-new face, a brand-new age, perhaps even a brand-new sex—and slip back into Earth's quiet corners or the next star system beyond.

And every detail of her five-thousand-year watch—our entire night of laughter, fury, awe, and quiet tenderness—would stream back intact to every citizen of a fractured oceanic world dozens of light-years away.

"Safe travels, Qin Jingwei." I didn't ask her to stay, nor press sentimentally about whether we would ever meet again; I simply raised a hand and smiled. "Send my regards to all those old friends dozens of light-years away on your archipelago world."

"Consider it done." Giving me one last playful wink, she turned and stepped lightly into the bright sunlight at the subway entrance, her silhouette dissolving smoothly into the vibrant, bustling 2026 morning crowd of southern China.

I lingered beneath the camphor trees long after she disappeared from sight.

Warm southern wind brushed my face as every word she had spoken across the teahouse, seawall, and dark balcony echoed quietly back—*Qin Jingwei*: carrying both the heavy interrogation of five thousand years of continental history and the stubborn, tender courage of a tiny bird filling a boundless sea pebble by pebble.

Taking a deep breath, I turned and walked back to my little ocean-view studio.

Drawing the sheer curtains halfway, I steeped a fresh mug of hot tea, sat down at the desk, opened my half-worn laptop, and created a blank document. A single cursor blinked steadily against the white page—like a tiny newly lit star in an immense night sky.

Resting my fingers on the keyboard, I began setting down, word by word in my own understanding, everything she had told me—first writing the theoretical treatise on the new civilizational order, and then recording these thirty-six extraordinary hours inside Earth's own "self-fulfilling science fiction novel."

Yet as both the first-person narrator of these pages and the writer of its companion theoretical treatise, I want to leave one plain, unembellished confession here—**whether you believe it or not, that spring 2026 encounter in a coastal southern Chinese city really happened.**
