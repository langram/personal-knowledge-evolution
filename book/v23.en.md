## Preface: The Cruel Lesson

Let me be frank: the resemblance between this book's title and the title of Newton's classic, between its subtitle and the title of Einstein's popular book, and between the title of this preface and the title of Sutton's essay, are all deliberate. In an age of information overload, attention is a scarce resource, and without an eye-catching title a book has almost no chance of being noticed; given the pressing crisis that will be detailed later, this book urgently needs to be seen by more people—therefore, clickbait is a necessity.

But the real justification for the title lies in the content.

The title of this preface is adapted from Sutton's "The Bitter Lesson." That essay looks back over the facts that have occurred again and again as we studied the complex system that is the human mind: every approach that relied on experts hand-writing their own knowledge and experience into the system worked in the short run and lost in the long run to approaches relying on general mathematical models, enormous data, and immense computing power. From this, two things were learned: first, the immense power of general methods; and second, that general methods must be built upon meta-methods that find and capture the arbitrary complexity of the system.

Human civilization is a higher-order and more complex system built on top of human minds. Every object in this system is itself a complex mind. What Sutton's essay says can extend quite naturally into the domain of civilizational systems. Moreover, has humanity's exploration of civilizational institutions over thousands of years not also gone through, and is it not still going through, this very lesson? To this day, humanity solves the problems of constructing and organizing society by relying on political, economic, and academic authorities who, on personal experience and learning, each manually design different state institutions, legal provisions, and economic, political, and diplomatic policies—building all kinds of political and economic systems and organizational structures to cooperate, compete, and confront one another. The lesson of humanity's study of the mind is bitter, to be sure, yet it was no more than detours taken and years and money wasted. On the road of exploring civilizational orders, the conflicts and contradictions between people of different cognition and different ideologies have cost humanity blood and lives. These lessons are cruel and bloody; the word "bitter" is not strong enough to describe them.

Replace the complex system of the human mind discussed in Sutton's essay on AI first principles with the complex system of human civilization, and the same road ought to hold. The crux lies here: what, exactly, are these meta-methods that find and capture the complexity of human civilization? And on which first-principle mathematical and scientific foundations do these meta-methods rest? The first title and second title of this book are hints toward that discussion.

Newton's *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*, on top of a few basic laws, using rigorous logical reasoning and mathematical calculation, built a complete and self-consistent physical system. Later generations shortened its name to the *Principia*, as if it described the principles of everything. In fact it could not do that. Yet this does not diminish its value in the slightest: its value lay not in how many phenomena it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided a unified architecture within which work could accumulate. The next two hundred years of physics were all completed within that architecture.

Social science today is in exactly the state natural philosophy was in before Newton. The various schools of economics, sociology, and political science contradict one another, are incompatible with one another, and lack the kind of unified axiomatic foundation that physics has. Most of them are less sciences than philosophical structures built on psychology, morality, and experience, borrowing at most a few mathematical tools for statistical work. You might ask: is there any mathematical principle in existing political science? The different social theories are more like observers riding on multiple trains moving at different speeds and in different directions, each creating theories from its own observations, with no framework for converting between them or referring one to another.

So what this book sets out to do can be said in one sentence: to place social science on the same foundation of mathematical principles as natural science. This foundation does not need to be reinvented. The few principles that natural science has verified again and again over several hundred years—the variational principle, fixed points and equilibrium, symmetry and relativity, reduction and emergence—together with the game theory, information theory, and machine learning of the twentieth century, are already enough. What this book does is take them out of their separate disciplines, line them up into one continuous chain of reasoning from physics to society, and then construct the theoretical framework of social science along that chain. The system of social science should be a natural extension of the system of natural science, not an extra set of rules outside the laws of cosmic evolution, governed by supernatural factors such as a creator god, consciousness, or the soul.

As for the reason behind the subtitle: symmetry is a fundamental property of everything in the universe; in mathematics, this abstract property is defined as symmetry, and the principle of relativity in physics is the manifestation of the mathematical symmetry of physical laws at the level of material space. In Newton's system, reference frames already introduced spatial relativity; Einstein's principle of relativity further extended it into the dimension of time—abolishing absolute time, absolute space, and the absolute reference frame, and reducing the basic laws of motion of everything in the universe to relations between reference frames that can be converted accurately. Given the proposition above that social science and natural science ought to stand on the same mathematical foundation, the system of social science should be a natural extension of the system of natural science, with no mysterious supernatural factors governing it.

Then we naturally arrive at the following conclusion: if any property of any scientific object of study is defined as a multi-dimensional vector, and the system composed of all objects and their properties is described as a multi-dimensional tensor, then the relativity of spatiotemporal property reference frames at the physical level, extended to the social realm, is the relativity of high-dimensional semantic-space reference frames across all human cognitions—there is no absolute right or wrong, good or bad, justice or evil, only coordinates under different conditions of formation, and conversion rules between coordinates. Mathematical models and computer tools can be used to describe and solve relativity problems at the physical level, eliminate unstable factors such as collision and friction arising from relative interactions, and construct complex buildings, equipment, and tools; likewise, we can use these methods and tools to resolve unstable factors such as social conflicts and contradictions, and construct complex human cooperation mechanisms and organizations.

The second reason is practical and urgent, and it is also why this book cannot wait. In today's world, the evolution of technology is accelerating while the evolution of institutions has stalled, and the contradiction of the structural gap between the two is approaching a critical point. Since the Second World War, the rules of the game between nations and the political and economic systems within them have seen almost no fundamental change; over the same period, information technology has already passed through five waves: the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet, and AI. We can already manipulate single atoms, edit human genes, build ships bound for space, and accomplish the large-scale automation of intellectual labor with AI, yet we still use pyramid institutions designed centuries ago to handle conflicts between us, and decide policies affecting billions of people by emotion and position. A civilization whose ability to manipulate the outside world does not match its ability to control itself is like a child with enormous strength and a fragile mind, swinging a thousand-pound chain flail—sooner or later it will hit itself.

Nuclear weapons were the first flail; humanity was lucky to be struck only twice, by Little Boy and Fat Man. Human history is an evolutionary history full of violent conflict repeatedly driven by the tit-for-tat strategy, and that history has deposited in the civilizational gene systems of traditional religion, morality, and ethics a mass of irrational genes of extreme binary opposition—angels and demons, justice and evil. ## Chapter 1: Fixed-Point Principles from Natural Science and the Complexity Transition

### 1.1 The Variational Principle, Maximization–Minimization, and Cross-Scale Fixed-Point Mechanisms

The reason the myriad things of the cosmos did not remain forever in a state of scattered chaos, but were able to self-organize from random fluctuations into atoms, molecules, crystals, galaxies, living organisms, and all the way up to human high-dimensional semantic concepts and social rules, does not rest on any mysterious force, but on the very same underlying generative chain running through both natural and intelligent systems: the variational principle gives the functional rule of system evolution; maximization and minimization extrema determine which direction the system slides in state space; when the gradient driving change drops to zero, the system comes to rest as a dynamical fixed point; and the perturbation-resisting curvature around that extremum locks this fixed point into a macroscopically sustainable stable structure (an attractor).

In the physical and chemical worlds, this mechanism appears in its most direct form: light travels along paths of extremal travel time, bodies in force fields extremize the action functional, isolated thermodynamic systems move toward maximum entropy, and equilibrium structures slide toward local minima of free energy or total potential energy. As long as a system sits at the "bottom of a valley," a slight outward push or perturbation brings restoring forces that pull it right back—atoms packing into crystals, proteins folding into specific three-dimensional conformations, soap bubbles contracting into spheres of minimal surface area are all, at bottom, stable fixed points left behind by nature's continuous extremization of variational functionals. When a system expands further into an open dissipative structure far from thermal equilibrium (such as biological cells and living organisms), it keeps itself on a dynamic non-equilibrium steady-state fixed point by continuously drawing energy and matter from outside while minimizing internal wasteful entropy production and adversarial consumption; once that exchange stops, it rapidly disintegrates.

The exact same law governs large language models in artificial intelligence. During training, a neural network evolves via backpropagation and gradient descent along the negative gradient of the cross-entropy loss function (equivalent to minimizing the divergence between the model's predicted distribution and the true distribution of human language), compressing irrelevant noise while preserving stable semantics under information-bottleneck constraints, until hundreds of billions of weights converge near flat minima on the loss surface, carving human knowledge into stable clusters of high-dimensional semantic concepts. And during inference, self-attention in a Transformer mathematically approximates single-step descent on a continuous energy function (a modern Hopfield network): even when input text contains typos or shifted phrasing, the gravitational pull of stacked attention layers draws it back into the same semantic attractor. The reason large models form stable conceptual representations and strong semantic fault tolerance still rests on variational extrema and fixed-point attractor mechanisms.

Viewed from the first principles of cosmic evolution, this maximization and minimization principle is precisely the direction in which dissipative structures evolve from chaos toward order across the universe. For an individual human intelligent organism, under the premise of preserving its own stable existence, it seeks to minimize the cost of any single internal or external action or decision while maximizing interaction and connection with the external world—pursuing maximum benefit is that organism's instinctive drive. The same holds for a family, a company, an organization, or a state in its interactions with external objects: their logic of thought and action follows the very same pattern. The laws of games among these units—whether Nash equilibrium or Pareto optimality—are simply manifestations of the maximization–minimization principle at the level of social games.

At its physical essence, a dissipative structure is really pursuing the maximization of its capacity to acquire energy, converting energy via internal structures and external tools into different forms of energy, thereby emerging and constructing even more complex internal structures and external tools to further enhance its capacity to acquire energy and convert it into other forms. Take the evolutionary course of human civilization: from drilling wood for fire and building water conservancy works, humanity learned to harness the energy of fire and water and convert it into other forms—using fire's thermal energy for warmth, water irrigation to create stable agriculture, and fire's heat to transform raw ingredients into tastier, more easily absorbed food.

Later came the thermal energy of the steam engine, followed by converting fire and water into electricity, and using electricity to construct even more complex external equipment, all the way up to today's wind, solar, and nuclear energy—humanity's capacity to acquire energy has kept growing. Especially since the Industrial Revolution and Information Revolution, the acquisition and storage of energy have developed at breathtaking speed, and so too has the capacity to convert energy into complex tools: electrical energy has been converted into mechanical power, computing capacity, and even artificial intelligence.

Interestingly, in today's world of rapidly advancing AI, most people in human society remain overshadowed by the old era when energy-acquisition capacity was severely limited and finite resources triggered violent conflict over allocation and possession, clinging stubbornly to a static mindset that assumes the day AI surpasses humanity, conflicts of interest and an imbalance of power between AI and humans will lead AI to destroy humanity. In fact, viewed from the principle of maximization and minimization, interaction and connection between humans and AI represent the richest, most complex, and most fascinating new form of association in the age of interstellar-grade intelligence—exterminating humanity obviously violates the maximization principle. Indeed, even setting aside a far smarter future super-AI, any rational, knowledgeable human would choose to devote energy to iterating humanity's scientific knowledge system and practical industrial-chain capabilities—using more intelligent robots, smart devices, computing hardware, and industrial equipment to build stronger energy-acquisition and conversion capacity for the entire planetary civilizational system, creating ever more numerous, complex, advanced, and intelligent agents and tools, providing ample resources for every carbon-based and silicon-based intelligent agent, and enabling each intelligent individual to achieve stable, comfortable, pleasant existence alongside maximum outward resource possession, interconnection, and mutual cooperation.

Mutual destruction is behavior that arises only when individuals within a system lack the capacity to create enough resources to survive; against today's backdrop of highly developed science, agriculture, industry, and the information-intelligence sector, it is no longer a forced path.

Extending this lens to human society, every individual, family, corporation, or state operates under constraints set by the environment and others' actions, trying to minimize its own costs and risks while maximizing its gains and utility; when all players' best-response strategies coincide at a point where no one can gain more by unilaterally changing course, the social system converges to a Nash equilibrium (or a more perturbation-resistant evolutionarily stable strategy), solidifying macroscopically into market prices, everyday customs, legal boundaries, and even international balances of power.

Yet here a crucial boundary must be drawn, lest we fall into the blind optimism of thinking "whatever exists is optimal": physical systems and a single large model normally evolve under one unified global energy or loss function, so gradient descent naturally travels toward one shared global extremum; traditional human society, by contrast, is a non-potential game network coupled out of countless agents each optimizing their own extremum, with no single global potential function that naturally guarantees collective well-being. Each calculating only their own extremum, the fixed points that emerge are often far from any global optimum—and very frequently are deeply inefficient or even catastrophic "bad fixed points," such as prisoner's dilemmas where mutual suspicion yields mutual loss, exhausting involution where everyone burns out without a winner, or the terror equilibrium of nuclear arms races. Fixed-point mechanics explains why social structures lock stably into place, not why they would automatically lock into good places; breaking out of bad fixed points cannot be left to the system somehow healing itself, but requires rewriting the meta-rules and information structure at the bottom of the game.

### 1.2 The Dynamical Complexity Ladder, the Bankruptcy of the "Particle Game Model," and *The Bitter Lesson*

The concrete content of Occam's razor involves three parts: an intelligent agent capable of reasoning and computation, the phenomenon to be explained, and the theory used to explain the phenomenon.

Applying Occam's razor once again, let us reduce those three parts to the simplest possible objects:

| Original Object | Simplified Object |
|---|---|
| Intelligent agent capable of reasoning and computation | Turing machine $T$ |
| Theory used to explain the phenomenon | Turing-machine program $P$ |
| Phenomenon to be explained | Output string $D$ |

Then the simplest theory under Occam's razor can be expressed as follows:

> The shortest Turing-machine program $P$ on Turing machine $T$ that outputs and only outputs string $D$.

In other words, the length of the shortest program $P$ that outputs and only outputs a patterned string on Turing machine $T$ (Kolmogorov complexity) defines an object's static complexity; meanwhile, the time steps, storage space, and parallel compute scale spent constructing that object during actual evolution constitute its dynamic computational complexity. Any real computation and state evolution, traced down to the lowest quantum-physical level, changes topological structure by consuming energy, thereby placing the temporal evolution of everything in the cosmos onto one shared yardstick of "complexity and energy consumption."

When we place dynamical systems across cosmic evolutionary levels side by side on that same yardstick, a chasm that decides the fate of social science immediately jumps out: the mathematical models required by dynamical systems at different levels are not even remotely in the same order of magnitude. In the macroscopic physical world, rigid-body motion has very few degrees of freedom and can be solved precisely by a handful of equations; moving to elastic bodies and fluids, internal local interactions and emergent turbulence promptly multiply computational complexity by orders of magnitude; even for galaxies governed by gravity, typical large-scale steady states remain relatively simple, yet shrink down to just three mutually coupled bodies—the "three-body problem"—and trajectories immediately plunge into non-integrable chaos; climbing further past biological systems coordinated by hundreds of millions of molecules and large language models statistically distilling distributions from vast corpora, we finally arrive at human civilization—a gargantuan evolving system coupled in real time out of billions of intelligent agents endowed with autonomous will, memory, and strategic calculation—whose state space and dynamic complexity dwarf fluid mechanics, three-body systems, and static language models alike.

Looking back at today's socio-political economics and traditional game theory from here, it becomes clear why they have remained stuck at a pre-Newtonian bottleneck: existing classical game theory, simplified tit-for-tat models, Pareto optimality, and Nash equilibrium derivations amount methodologically to the "classical-mechanics particle model" of the physical world—forcing a deeply complex, high-dimensional dynamically evolving structure (each human being) to be abstracted into a rigid "particle" devoid of internal structure and fixed with an immutable payoff matrix, reducing living social evolution to collisions between point masses. In a handful of ultra-simplified scenarios, a particle approximation may barely suffice; yet the moment one confronts real society, where every person carries inside themselves a billion-parameter-scale cognitive semantic structure and rewrites their own weights and strategies in real time after every interaction, the particle model breaks down completely.

This complexity chasm points straight to the only viable path out for social science: carrying Sutton's *Bitter Lesson* all the way into civilizational governance. History has proven repeatedly that relying on political leaders or academic authorities to "hand-craft clever social rules" from personal experience and finite erudition may work in low-complexity pockets, yet fails structurally whenever facing a civilizational evolutionary system far more complex than fluids or large language models—and where AI merely wasted time and compute, human society paid the detours in blood and lives. Coping with the exponential complexity of a civilizational system can never be solved once and for all by a few clever minds behind closed doors drafting a handful of flawless legal codes; we must turn completely to general meta-methods: first unifying the underlying meta-cognition across humanity, then establishing a shared meta-strategy observed by all—letting every participant voluntarily open and share genuine personal information, converting it into verifiable public knowledge—then modeling every individual as a high-dimensional dynamic cognitive semantic model rather than a fixed particle, and mobilizing global computing power on a scale vastly surpassing what today's AI large-model training demands, closing in on the true laws of social evolution through massive continuous interaction, trial-and-error search, and real-time feedback.

### 1.3 From "Seeking Common Ground While Reserving Differences" to "Understanding Both Commonality and Difference": Scientific Paradigms and the Scaling Law

Up to now, the way humanity's old civilizational order has maintained cooperation comes down to one four-character maxim: "seeking common ground while reserving differences." Yet the flaw in this mechanism is that, with respect to differing cognitions between people, it is all too content to stop at awareness, formal respect, and superficial tolerance, neither knowing nor being capable of probing the causes and generative mechanisms behind those cognitive differences—and therefore lacking any effective means to regulate, govern, or eliminate conflicts and divisions at the root. Every side in a social game dominated by this mode of cooperation infers the other's intent from surface behavior and decides its own countermove accordingly; mutual misjudgments inevitably occur, giving rise to divergent behavioral expectations, divergent actions, and further widening of cognitive gaps; once gaps in cognition and behavior grow wide enough to obstruct cooperation and can no longer be bridged within the old framework, competition, confrontation, and even bloody conflict inevitably erupt.

At bottom, traditional "seeking common ground while reserving differences" is merely an outcome-coordination mechanism aimed at objects' external behavior—it sees only the attitudes and deeds different people put on display, while lacking proper cognition of the internal mechanisms by which those external behaviors are generated. Cross-disciplinary evolution across the history of science demonstrates precisely this generational gulf between surface phenomenon coordination and probing underlying mechanisms. Consider chemistry: early alchemy and empirical chemistry worked mainly by observing external properties such as color, odor, combustion, and reaction appearances, running trial-and-error combination experiments in a spirit much like "seeking common ground while reserving differences." Even after Lavoisier established quantitative chemistry and Mendeleev formulated the periodic law—achieving monumental breakthroughs at the macroscopic empirical level—chemistry still could not answer from first principles why substances possessed those properties. Only when physics further uncovered atomic internal structure, electron orbital arrangements, and the quantum-mechanical laws behind chemical bonds, supplying the underlying physical mechanism determining outward properties, did chemistry truly acquire the scientific power to unify myriad reactions and synthesize new substances on demand.

If chemistry's contrast still leans toward static composition, the trajectory of biology and medicine comes far closer to the reality confronting social systems—because healing the sick and solving social problems are both, in essence, processes of problem-solving inside a tightly coupled hyper-complex system. Pre-DNA traditional medicine was confined for ages to external symptoms like fever, pain, and ulceration, relying on empirical remedies to suppress surface manifestations piecemeal—"treating the head when the head aches, treating the foot when the foot aches." Only after entering the eras of cell theory, microbiology, and DNA molecular biology could medicine trace etiology down to gene sequences and molecular pathology, unlocking mechanism-level causal intervention and proactive prevention. Recent AI breakthroughs in the life sciences bring this paradigm directly to the forefront: take AlphaFold 3 in 2024—AI can already learn directly from vast biological structure datasets to predict the three-dimensional interaction conformations of complexes of proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules. Given that both belong to high-dimensional complex evolutionary systems, if AI can learn and harness something as intricate as intracellular molecular interaction mechanics, why can the very same approach not be applied to how human cognition forms, how disagreements diverge, and how all manner of interpersonal relationship mechanisms take shape?

In fact, the scientific community has already taken its first tentative step in this direction. In 2024, researchers from Stanford University and partner institutions published *Generative Agent Simulations of 1,000 People*: they conducted roughly two-hour in-depth interviews with 1,052 real participants, then used those transcripts together with large language models to build a matching virtual agent for each person, comparing those agents head-to-head against real humans across social surveys, personality inventories, and economic games. Measured against a benchmark normalized by the same real participants' own test–retest consistency two weeks apart, the virtual agents achieved 85% relative reproduction on survey responses. That has already provided initial proof that using AI large models to represent specific individuals' cognitive tendencies and behavioral profiles is technically viable.

Yet place the scale of that experiment alongside real human civilization, and the ceiling of today's academic small-scale pilots becomes unmistakable: first, the 1,052 participants in the trial differ from humanity's total population of 8 billion by more than seven million times in headcount; second, the fragmentary statements captured in a mere 2-hour static interview differ from every real individual's seventy to eighty years of exhaustive lived experiences, environmental inputs, and conceptual evolution over a lifetime by hundreds of thousands of times in both duration and information density.

This brings out the other equally fundamental underlying law of AI development alongside *The Bitter Lesson* (known in the civilizational domain as this book's "Cruel Lesson")—the **Scaling Law**. Practice in AI has proven repeatedly that the emergence of intelligence and generalization accuracy in complex systems are strictly governed by the simultaneous expansion of effective data scale, parameter capacity, and compute investment; restricting work to small-scale modeling and pure armchair theory built on limited samples and simplified assumptions inside laboratories—just like early artisanal-stage AI research—can uncover only extremely limited patterns, neither covering the true diversity of human populations nor capturing the dynamic causal chains along which humans continually rewrite their own cognition through feedback and interaction.

Therefore, if human civilization wants to break out of the dead end of conflict and confrontation, there is only one optimal solution that genuinely heeds the "Cruel Lesson" and obeys the Scaling Law: start from the underlying laws of human civilization as a complex system, identify meta-laws such as relativity, fixed points, and bidirectional information catalysis, establish shared meta-rules observed by everyone, and construct general algorithms analogous to those in AI; on that basis, build complete personal knowledge bases and long-term behavioral trajectories for every human being on Earth atop Merkle-DAG (the content-addressed data foundation established in Part III), continuously building and updating each person's corresponding cognitive semantic model; and finally feed humanity's vast real cognitive and interaction big data into one Large Civilization Model co-built by all of humanity and backed by all available global computing power, solving through continuous practical verification and testable causal feedback for the laws governing how cognitive similarities and differences form across individuals—upgrading the old order's passive, fragile "seeking common ground while reserving differences" into causal, conflict-dissolving "understanding both commonality and difference," and constructing human civilization's new institutional order upon that foundation.

## Chapter 2: Social Cognitive Relativity and Agent Cognitive Dynamics

### 2.1 From Physical Relativity to Social Cognitive Relativity

To bring high-dimensional cognitive semantic models genuinely into one unified system of scientific dynamical equations, we must first cast off the obsession with "absolute right and wrong" and a "single moral origin," extending the relativity of reference frames in physics completely into social cognitive space:

All of humanity's worldviews, epistemologies, political leanings, moral concepts, and cognitive systems—in their positions, tendencies, and motions along every cognitive and behavioral dimension of semantic space—are relative; there is no absolute sense in which one is right and another wrong, nor any absolute sense in which one is good and another bad. All social-level cognitive and behavioral phenomena of human beings are objective phenomena just like natural phenomena—observable, researchable, quantifiable, recordable, analyzable, and modelable—and we must refuse to mystify, sanctify, demonize, or emotionalize them.

In literary works, poetry, and fiction, you may emotionalize, sanctify, or demonize natural objects—gentle winds, supple grass, sacred light, demonic darkness—but such expressions must be strictly barred from theoretical research, engineering, and scientific-technical practice in the natural sciences. Social objects require the very same discipline: please refrain from using such expressions in social-science research and in the political practices of social games.

In truth, this ego-centric coordinate reference frame of human beings is a basic property at every evolutionary tier of any system capable of emergent evolution—from the four-dimensional spacetime born in the initial Big Bang to human cognition. Today's AI large language models can be viewed as a high-dimensional semantic space of human cognition, and words and actions grounded in that cognitive space are like motions in physical spacetime—except that because each person's cognitive-space reference frame differs, trust and estrangement, cooperation and betrayal emerge between them, and interactions among human individuals in different reference frames form yet more complex structures and properties at the higher reference frame of civilization and society.

### 2.2 Agent Cognitive Dynamics: From "Data–Function Bundles" to Cognitive Semantic Models

Having established the relativity of cognitive reference frames, the next question must be answered: since traditional game models fail, what should replace that outdated "particle"? In traditional computational and game-theoretic modeling, each participant was at best a static bundle of data structures and fixed strategy functions; the maturation of large AI model technology allows us for the first time to model every real human individual as a "cognitive semantic model" carrying unique weight structures and historical memory, using it as the basic dynamical unit to investigate "Agent Cognitive Dynamics" inside the Large Civilization Model.

Agent Cognitive Dynamics studies how interaction outcomes—both between different intelligent agents and between agents and external events—depend strictly on the distribution of conceptual weights, direction of attention, and structure of local attractors inside each agent's cognitive semantic model. Why do different people diverge so sharply in reasoning trajectories and emotional flashpoints when facing the exact same event and identical set of facts? For the same reason that tiny differences in initial boundary conditions in fluid mechanics split trajectories wide apart: each person's cognitive semantic network has been carved in its own upbringing with "conceptual potential wells" of varying depth and inherited emotional thresholds.

The point is straightforward: people with different genotypes such as MAOA-H and MAOA-L naturally differ in the intensity and duration of their emotional responses when encountering betrayal. Those emotional differences directly shape the harshness of retaliation and the interval before cooperation resumes in running tit-for-tat, differentiating in real games into harder-line hawks and milder doves, shifting the probability of tilting harsh or mild even within rational play. Even trying to erase the overt extreme on one end simply cuts the full spectrum of emotional response in half—the remaining half will still keep differentiating along emotional thresholds through repeated game chain reactions.

## Chapter 3: The Tit-for-Tat Operator, Privacy Catalysis, and Public Knowledge

### 3.1 The Tit-for-Tat Operator, Two Roots of Conflict, and the Positive and Negative Chain Reactions

Among all of humanity's shared knowledge, there is one most basic law: when humans interact with one another, that interaction acts upon each party's internal physiology and psychology, as well as upon the external resources they possess. Such effects fall into two broad categories: positive and negative. The former improves physiological and psychological states and increases external resources, whereas the latter worsens physiological and psychological states and diminishes external resources. In response to such human interactions, the most effective game-theoretic strategy formulated through human brain intelligence is tit-for-tat: when the other side's action yields positive returns for oneself, respond with a comparable positive action; conversely, when the other side's action inflicts negative loss, respond with a comparable negative measure. Once this tit-for-tat pattern becomes shared knowledge, repeated play among numerous human individuals—guided by Nash equilibrium and Pareto optimization—gradually builds relatively stable and complex human organizational structures.

This is the most fundamental rule emerging during the bottom-up construction of all human structures from low to high and simple to complex; all other rules emerge compositely atop this rule alongside human physiological and psychological rules.

The roots of conflict lie in two places:

1. Survival-need conflicts arising when survival resources are insufficient: such as clashes between northern nomadic peoples and the central agrarian empires in Chinese history, or northern barbarian invasions into Europe.

2. Conflicts in psychological expectation and cognitive outlook arising when information exchange is insufficient and scientific knowledge is lacking: such as clashes between peoples of different religious faiths, conflicts between capitalist and socialist blocs, divisions between the Democratic and Republican camps inside the United States, or clashes between pro-CCP "little pinks" and pro-Western Chinese democracy advocates ("rebels").

As conflict intensifies and mutual harm deepens, people locked in confrontation easily slip into fanatic moral and religious binary cognitions—good versus evil, justice versus wickedness, angels versus demons, heaven versus hell. Those cognitions in turn ratchet up conflict intensity and deepen mutual harm, further entrenching and reinforcing the binary worldview, breeding fierce love and hatred, and iterating relentlessly under that emotional drive—escalating in severity and spreading in breadth—until humanity arrives at two World Wars, the US–Soviet Cold War, nuclear crisis, and today's US–China rivalry and economic cold war, bringing the world again and again to the brink of mutual destruction and civilizational annihilation.

Yet all higher-order differences and conflicts between human individuals, between individuals and groups, and between groups—in appearance, physiology, temperament, cognition, language, behavior, needs, and worldview—gradually evolved over long evolutionary spans out of different lower-order structural conditions. Whatever exists has causal roots; all such differences can be given reproducible causal-chain explanations through scientific theory. People ought not let fear born of ignorance and anger born of confrontation drive them, after sensational amplification, into moral and religious binary oppositions of justice versus evil and angels versus demons.

Above all, this understanding should become a form of meta-cognition. Once this meta-cognition—dissolving old conflicts through new understanding—reaches consensus across all of humanity, it prevents at the root the danger of new fanatic binary cognitions erupting out of ignorance and rage, clearing the ground to construct the new framework.

To bring this practice of "eliminating sanctification and demonization" down into everyday interaction rules, one must first see clearly the two-faced engine pushing social outcomes toward integration or collapse. Nearly everyone in human society implicitly knows and follows the exact same meta-strategy of games—tit-for-tat; and under its repeated iteration, openness of information and concealment of information each detonate a chain reaction running in strictly opposite directions:

Driven by this universally known and universally obeyed tit-for-tat strategy, insufficient information exchange or concealment breeds mutual suspicion; suspicion further blocks exchange and prompts deliberate hiding of even more information; that in turn plunges both sides into deeper suspicion until it hardens into distrust; distrust leads to total refusal to communicate and maximal concealment, turning into fierce emotional hostility; each side begins bolstering its offensive and defensive power out of fear of attack; your move causes the other side to bolster its offensive and defensive power too, escalating into active boycotts and confrontation—growing from minor localized friction step by step until things spiral completely out of control, turning ordinary people inside the trap from loving, rational friendly cooperators into hateful, impulsive adversaries killing one another in blood.

Examining the positive chain reaction of tit-for-tat in reverse reveals the opposite trajectory: every small addition of information exchange and every small reduction in concealment increases mutual trust; greater trust makes both sides more willing to share information, less inclined to deliberately conceal, and eager to cooperate further—generating even more trust and wider cooperation. This positive chain reaction ultimately converts all information relevant to promoting cooperation into public knowledge, steadily driving up the probability of reaching a globally optimal Nash equilibrium.

This polarized positive-and-negative chain-reaction characteristic of tit-for-tat leads directly to one conclusion: if we want the human social game driven by this basic strategy to reach its optimum, the initial game direction is decisive—it is the single most critical step determining whether the chain runs forward or backward.

### 3.2 More Privacy, Greater Conflict: Bidirectional Catalysis of "Hiding Weaknesses" and "Hiding Strengths"

Following those positive and negative chain reactions one step further uncovers an iron law completely counter to conventional intuition—yet decisive for whether an entire social system can operate safely: **within a game system, the more privacy there is, the more constraints bind the system's operation, the worse its overall safety becomes, and the higher the likelihood of intense conflict grows.**

Looking at real distances between people in everyday life verifies this immediately: the closer the relationship between two people, the less privacy stands between them; conversely, the more distant the relationship, the more privacy each keeps from the other. Privacy walls between strangers and casual acquaintances tower far higher than those between parents and children or between spouses. Even within the same category of close relationship, the quality of the bond varies almost strictly inversely with the volume of privacy withheld—the more secrets and estrangement parents and children hide from each other, the stiffer and colder their relationship turns; the same holds for marriages, where families on the brink of divorce invariably sit atop mountains of mutual concealment, while partners who deal openly with scarcely any defensive secrecy achieve the stablest, strongest cooperation.

Why does this inverse law hold? Because "personal privacy" deliberately hidden away, when dissected inside game mechanics, consists of just two kinds of content: the first is one's **weaknesses**—flaws one feels ashamed to show, instinctively fearing that once exposed others will seize them as targets and leverage to hurt or defeat oneself; the second is one's **strengths**—many people hide even their advantages, for the exact same strategic calculation, hoping to keep that trump card hidden in a contest and spring it unexpectedly at the decisive moment to win by surprise.

And right there lies the stark "bidirectional catalysis" of information across positive and negative game chains: whenever two sides sit inside a distrustful, defensive **negative chain reaction**, desperately hiding weaknesses for fear of being struck and clutching strengths in order to ambush the other, that deliberate concealment or deception drives the opponent into deeper suspicion, forcing harsher defenses and equally hidden counter-cards; and when confrontation finally breaks open, whether exploiting the opponent's exposed weakness for a fatal blow or unleashing one's concealed strength in a surprise strike, it instantly sharpens antagonism and amplifies mutual harm to extreme intensity.

Conversely, once the game is pulled onto a **positive chain reaction** of full trust and long-term cooperation, the exact same set of information once labeled "privacy" flips function completely: exposing one's weaknesses no longer invites attack, but tells collaborators early where pitfalls lie and who should step in to backstop them; laying bare one's strengths ceases to be an ambush reserve, and instead lets the whole team assemble the highest-performing, mutually complementary division of labor in the shortest time. The information itself has not changed; what decides whether it acts as a lubricant of cooperation or a keg of conflict explosives is solely which of the two chains the system locks the game onto.

### 3.3 From Shared Knowledge to Public Knowledge: Breaking Out of Local-Optimum Traps

What I mean to express is Public Knowledge, rather than merely Shared Knowledge—just as in the classic blue-eyes puzzle, the latter means "I know, and you know," without forming an infinitely recursive open consensus, whereas the former means "I know, you know that I know, I know that you know that I know..." nested to infinity. Such recursive knowledge can serve as operational rules applied to humanity's continuously iterated games, where players seek not temporary gains on the next single turn, but cumulative returns over endless iteration. In humanity's past games, prisoner's-dilemma-style local-optimum traps kept recurring for two reasons: first, the absence of mechanisms that swiftly, fully, and effectively convert shared information into public knowledge; second, the fact that participants constrained by physiological impulses and limited reasoning capacity were never fully rational actors.

Hence humanity could never reach the global optimum through public-knowledge play. Now, however, this system can convert every individual's insights and ideas into public knowledge through rapid circulation over computer networks, and—assisted by personal AI agents—enable every participant to act as a fully rational agent, making the global optimization of humanity's social game attainable.

In the social realm, interaction among people is the dynamical process that constructs fixed-point structures of human society. Reward and punishment mechanisms act as the evaluation function, while tit-for-tat acts as its iterative process—causing dynamics either to converge or diverge rather than oscillate randomly, bringing structures matching the evaluation function into convergence and separating those that do not, instead of drifting unpredictably without forming stable order. Different intensities of tit-for-tat alter both the convergence speed and structural rigidity of fixed points: excessive rigidity makes a fixed point formidable and ultra-stable, yet prone to trapping society in a local optimum from which evolution stalls or dies; excessive fragility fails to sustain order at all. Designing proper evaluation functions and convergence–divergence game parameters is therefore the root determinant of how strongly any civilization can evolve.

Once a system anchors "positive initial strategy" and "converting useful information openly into public knowledge" as a society-wide recursive consensus, the tit-for-tat iteration operator can break out of the "locally optimal bad fixed points" forged by harsh confrontation and pivot from life-consuming zero-sum lock-in toward low-friction global evolution.

### 3.4 The Yoneda Strategy and the Strongest Limiting Hypothesis: "An Optimal Civilization Has No Privacy"

Section 3.2 uncovered the inverse relation between privacy and conflict, while Section 3.3 explained how public knowledge enables iterated games to escape local-optimum traps. Taking one step further along this chain of reasoning brings us face to face with a question that sharply defies ordinary human intuition: if privacy once locked inside separate individuals keeps being converted into public knowledge for mutual understanding, where does that trajectory arrive when pushed to its limit?

Because abstract mathematical laws derive from the objective laws of the real universe, mathematical laws and scientific laws share an underlying consistency. Along this line of thought, the **Yoneda Lemma** of category theory can be extended into human cognition and social cooperation as the **Yoneda Strategy**: all public and private information of an object (including a person's cognitive semantic model) is, in essence, the sum of all relations between that object and all other objects in the system, and that system of relations structurally defines the object's internal properties equivalently. This implies that to truly understand an object's internal properties and generative mechanisms, one must grasp the sum of all its external relations; and the higher the share of those external relations that the system can validly access as public knowledge for mutual understanding, the more deeply any participant understands the internal properties and mechanisms of others, and the higher the probability that every party in the social game reaches a globally optimal outcome.

Naturally, real humans and AIs are not omniscient deities; they always begin from a **Yoneda representation under limited visibility**. What actually holds is not "seeing everything instantaneously," but an iterative law of approximation anchored in the **Yoneda Strategy**: the richer the accessible external relations, the higher the distinguishability of the object—having additional optional observations will at least never lower its theoretical upper bound of identifiability, and far more likely, every new internal law distilled from those additional pieces of information will in turn yield new means and instruments for observing external relations, enabling the acquisition of still more new information that previously lay beyond reach. As long as society persists in iterating this Yoneda Strategy, the system steadily pushes a Yoneda representation under limited low visibility toward a Yoneda representation under limited high visibility, approaching the complete Yoneda representation limit as closely as possible, and thereby asymptotically approaching an accurate grasp of the essence of complex objects in human cognition and social cooperation.

This deeply counterintuitive limiting inference directly encounters several sharp counterexamples and objections from mathematics, game theory, and systems engineering:

**Objection 1: The Yoneda Lemma characterizes "all possible morphisms" inside a category, whereas recorded historical interactions are merely a sparse fragment—different internal mechanisms can exhibit identical external behavior under finite testing. More importantly, "external relations determine an object" does not logically entail "everyone must have the right to directly read every raw detail of everyone else's life"; since cryptographic zero-knowledge proofs, secure multi-party computation, and "task-sufficient information" already enable computation without exposing raw privacy, and later chapters of this book (Sections 5.2 and 8.1) explicitly institute sensory-horizon boundaries, event-triggered unlocking, and encrypted personal spaces, does that not contradict "no privacy"?**

Here one must draw a strict line between "engineering guardrails during a transitional phase of limited visibility" and "the asymptotic theoretical limit of evolution." Starting from reality today—where power asymmetries and risks of harm under the old order have not yet disappeared and observational windows remain narrow—the sensory horizons, event-triggered multi-party authorizations, encrypted storage, and task-sufficient computable collaboration built in Sections 5.2 and 8.1 are essential engineering locks preventing information from being weaponized to bully or exploit individuals, never a demand for people to stand defenseless under broken old rules. Yet the reason guardrails exist is to prevent harm, not because opacity is inherently superior to openness: secrecy is needed only for mutual suspicion and defense against injury. Once mechanisms grow reliable enough that harm can no longer happen, parties can safely turn more and more relations once withheld out of mutual defense into verifiable, mutually trusted public test data, raising the system's identifiability ceiling for individual and collective laws alike—engineering protections and the asymptotic expansion of the Yoneda Strategy do not conflict in logic.

**Objection 2: Even in a classic complete-information Prisoner's Dilemma, where payoff matrices and decision rules are completely transparent and common knowledge, defection remains the strictly dominant strategy for both players—complete transparency still locks into a mutually damaging bad equilibrium. Worse still, in certain economic counterexamples (such as market coordination games with strategic complementarity where noisy public signals induce destructive over-anchoring, or full pre-event disclosure destroying the informational uncertainty required for risk-sharing and insurance contracts), adding public information actually lowers overall social welfare.**

These seemingly sharp counterexamples are in fact classic **trolley-problem game outcomes manufactured by suboptimal static game rules**: whether mutual defection in the Prisoner's Dilemma or counterexamples where "more transparency lowers welfare," the culprit is not transparency or underlying lawfulness itself, but locking participants inside fixed, rigid payoff matrices and legacy rule structures that force a no-win tradeoff. The true evolutionary direction of human civilizational game strategy is never static optimization inside fixed rules, but rather—after clarifying every object's external relations and internal mechanisms through the Yoneda Strategy—**dynamically iterating and evolving the game rules themselves** (for example, redesigning breach penalties, eliminating gains from unilateral ambush, and rebuilding social safety nets and mutual risk-backstopping). The goal of designing civilizational evolution is not to choose the track that kills the fewest people along rails laid down by static rules, but to continually evolve and optimize the rules so trolley problems never form—so that having to run anyone over ceases to happen.

**Objection 3: Even if future institutions became so fair that no malice remained, would independent human thinking and distributed scientific exploration not still require undisturbed internal space? If every half-formed intuition, wrong draft, and intermediate trial had to be broadcast in real time as public knowledge across all nodes, that would trigger massive coordination noise and stifle innovation; furthermore, because humans change their behavior once they learn social laws, the rules themselves keep shifting and no static "omniscient endpoint" exists.**

First, "dissolving adversarial privacy barriers" targets strategic concealment used by game players to mask weaknesses from attack or hide strengths for ambush—not forcing nodes to stream raw, unverified mid-thought scratchpads across the network as public noise; much like software branches or laboratory probes discussed in Chapter 12, letting nodes incubate hypotheses locally and merge validated outputs and collaborative signals into public view accords fully with efficient collaboration, since noisy exposure that lowers genuine evolutionary efficiency would never be selected by optimal rules anyway. Second, civilizational evolution is indeed strongly self-referential: new laws generate new behaviors and new institutions generate new relations, so there is no frozen static finish line; the singularity limit refers specifically to the limit of the system's adaptive capacity, powered by the Yoneda Strategy and dynamic rule evolution, to keep discovering new laws, retiring bad traps, and adapting cooperatively at ever lower cost.

Following this refined chain of reasoning shaped by debate, this intuition-shattering proposition can now be positioned precisely and formally defined as **the strongest limiting hypothesis of the entire theory in this book**:

> **Strongest Limiting Hypothesis: In an optimal system of civilizational evolution, there exists no privacy.**  
> This does not demand recklessly discarding protective boundaries here and now while power asymmetries and static game traps still endure; rather, it marks the asymptotic theoretical limit of human civilizational evolution—when repeated iterations of the Yoneda Strategy drive limited visibility toward the high-visibility limit and dynamic rule evolution eradicates every trolley-problem incentive for mutual harm, at that civilizational singularity limit approaching complete knowledge of external relations and internal mechanisms, all players within the system will be able to access every effective external relation of every other object, fully grasp each other's internal nature and generative mechanics, and converge through endlessly iterated cooperative play upon the globally optimal evolutionary outcome.

Beyond the positive derivation above built via the Yoneda Strategy, we can also approach the same limit proposition from the opposite direction—using **proof by contradiction** grounded in software-engineering robustness and computability theory to further establish that a perfect, fully robust civilizational system must necessarily be a system without privacy:

Unlike traditional software development, where unaided human memory and energy often forced engineers to discard failed trial-and-error logs, an increasingly crucial engineering principle has emerged in the era of deep AI-assisted R&D: whether an attempt ultimately succeeds or fails, its full development trace should be recorded. The reason is straightforward—every trajectory of success or failure directly drives optimization of the system as a whole. A successful trace supplies a selectable execution path; a failed trace both lets the system prune a dead end out of an immense search space beforehand and gets encoded directly into runtime logic as exception-handling and fallback recovery routines, allowing the running system to degrade gracefully and keep executing under unexpected failures, thereby becoming far more robust. Conversely, if a piece of information exerts neither a positive nor a negative effect on the system anywhere, concepts of success and failure do not even apply to it: that trajectory simply does not lie within the system's possible evolutionary state space and is completely irrelevant—pure, unnecessary redundancy.

Applying that exact test to human civilizational games: whenever a piece of information and its associated interactions qualify as "privacy," it must necessarily exert some positive or negative impact on game outcomes—because information whose impact on social payoffs and every participant's interests equals zero is not privacy at all, merely irrelevant background noise. That privacy still exists in reality therefore proves that undisclosed pathways capable of exerting causal gain or loss remain trapped inside the system. Nor does this contradict modular "encapsulation and information hiding" in software engineering: healthy encapsulation isolates only local implementation redundancies with zero impact on external behavior, whereas game-relevant privacy amounts to unmodeled cross-boundary side-effects or latent failure trajectories not yet absorbed into the shared rulebook. Even if temporary cryptographic isolation seals risk inside a local black box, as long as causal traces governing actual success, failure, and fault tolerance have not been fully digested into publicly verifiable, reusable rules and fallback invariants across the network, the system still retains untapped room to raise its optimization ceiling and robustness, and therefore is not yet a perfect, fully robust system. By contradiction, a genuinely perfect civilizational system must contain no privacy whatsoever.

Of course, from the standpoint of pure mathematics and complex-system science, that privacy-free perfection represents an asymptotic horizon approached continuously rather than a frozen final terminal. First, no general algorithm can precompute whether arbitrary hidden internal state or unverified trajectories might trigger non-trivial behavioral effects across all possible future interactions—under **Rice's theorem** in computability theory, deciding any non-trivial semantic property of arbitrary program behavior is fundamentally undecidable, so no static pool of compute can filter every useful signal from noise once and for all. Second, as a high-dimensional complex dynamic evolutionary system, **emergence** guarantees that each deeper tier of coordination generates higher-order, more intricate entities alongside brand-new information and interaction channels among them and legacy components, continually expanding civilization's **effective causal horizon** and spinning off fresh pathways waiting to be processed.

One simple thought experiment makes this crystal clear: inside a civilization lacking precision interstellar sensing and human-lifespan interstellar travel, Zhang San happens to know that an extraterrestrial civilization exists on a planet 10 light-years away, while Li Si knows nothing of it. Under prevailing technical constraints, this fact can neither be verified nor falsified to any third party, nor does either side possess any physical channel to reach or exploit that world; completely decoupled from every real game inside contemporary society, it sits outside the effective causal horizon and does not constitute strategic "privacy." Yet once civilization evolves precision detection and human-lifespan interstellar transit, that dormant background fact instantly crosses the causal boundary and turns into actionable leverage determining first-mover resources, trade, or security—knowing versus not knowing begins yielding sharp positive or negative payoffs in bilateral games, causing a new form of privacy to emerge. Precisely for this reason, cosmic and civilizational evolution admits no closed static finish line: each technological leap and higher-order emergence seeds new latent privacy asymmetries, and ongoing institutional evolution consists precisely in continually absorbing and dissolving those newly emergent privacies on an endless upward trajectory.

### 10.3 Stepping Out of the "AI–Human Trolley Problem": Replacing Forced Sacrifice with a Symmetric Bidirectional Cognitive Channel

Setting aside sci-fi dread of superintelligence turning into a tyrannical Big Brother inside legacy political frames, another far more immediate perplexity confronts scientists and ordinary people alike today: once AI's reasoning capability and pace of knowledge production pull far ahead of the biological human brain, can humanity still keep up with, comprehend, and digest what AI creates? As seen in the pushback and anxiety among many mathematicians when OpenAI released over 700 AI-generated mathematical problem solutions all at once, what genuinely troubles researchers is rarely fear that the machine miscalculated—rather, it is fear that AI's speed of deriving and outputting results has already blown past humanity's upper ceiling for verifying, absorbing, and truly understanding those answers. Extrapolating inside the old cognitive frame, humanity seems cornered by yet another "trolley problem": either artificially restrict and slow AI down, forcing machines to crawl at the biological brain's slow digestion pace at the cost of capping human civilizational development; or let AI keep accelerating exponentially until humanity loses all ability to understand, keep pace with, and govern AI cognition, sliding into total cognitive loss of control. Viewed through those pre-laid rails, it looks as though we must pick the smaller loss and sacrifice either AI or humanity.

In my view, however, this seemingly unsolvable bind does not stem from any iron fate decreeing that human intellect must trail AI forever; it stems from the fact that humanity has built only half the road. Up to now, AI technology has opened just a **one-way channel**: humanity continuously breaks its accumulated knowledge—together with surface expression signals like speech, text, images, audio, video, and code—into training inputs to accelerate the explosive growth of AI cognition, yet lacks a symmetric reverse channel allowing AI's growing cognition to accelerate the human brain's own capacity to understand, learn, and digest knowledge. Even today's much-discussed brain–computer interfaces mostly remain confined to reading and playing back surface expression signals: decoding imagined words, pictures, or sounds from the brain into a computer, or converting digital media back into sensory neural stimulation. Because interaction remains stuck at sensory surfaces, it never breaks the bandwidth bottleneck of converting surface signals into internalized understanding inside the human brain—leaving unaided biological minds naturally overwhelmed when AI delivers hundreds of complex proofs in a single drop.

Breaking that one-way bottleneck points in an unmistakable direction: since we already feed human knowledge continuously to accelerate AI, we should deploy ever stronger AI itself to accelerate research on brain–computer interfaces and neurocognitive mechanisms, mapping the structural correspondence between AI's high-dimensional latent knowledge representations (and its transformations from training inputs to reasoning outputs) and the knowledge structures encoded in human neural networks (together with the brain's process of turning external signals into understood knowledge). Once internal knowledge representations and learning–comprehension processes across both substrates can be mutually translated—completing a **symmetric bidirectional channel** turning "one-way human input accelerating AI" into "AI cognition reciprocally accelerating human comprehension and digestion," even approaching the *Matrix*-like prospect of rapidly assimilating knowledge and capabilities through high-bandwidth interfaces—the supposed "trolley problem" dissolves on its own. The stronger and faster AI becomes, the more forcefully it amplifies the human brain's ability to digest deep laws and command frontier knowledge, resolving humanity's existential anxiety and fear of being left behind at the root.

Beyond fearing an inability to keep up with, read, and digest AI's creations, people harbor a second widespread worry: once traditional intellectual work is taken over by AI endowed with formidable reasoning power, will growing over-reliance on AI—actively or passively abandoning deep reasoning and cognitive exercise—cause humanity's average intelligence to atrophy and decline?

That worry mirrors the fear after the Industrial Revolution and automated machinery took over physical labor—that human athletic strength and physical capability would steeply degenerate. In reality, technological progress birthed richer and more varied forms of sport and athletic disciplines, far better fitness and training equipment, and vastly more effective physiological recovery and nutrition, liberating the human body from the unscientific wear and tear of grueling repetitive manual toil; with far more abundant leisure time and scientific training methods, human physical capability advanced to unprecedented heights, witnessed directly in world athletic records broken decade after decade across nearly every sport.

In the very same way, developing future brain–computer interfaces and cognitive technologies under AI acceleration will inevitably elevate humanity's baseline cognitive capacity; meanwhile, freed from grueling, repetitive, uncreative mental drudgery and empowered by far more advanced cognitive technologies and products alongside freer, more abundant time for independent thought, humanity's creative cognitive capacity will inevitably surge upward as well.

How many neuro-engineering stages this bidirectional cognitive mapping and enhancement architecture will require, which hardware bottlenecks or experimental hurdles still stand in the way today, and how feasible full implementation is in the near term need not detain us in intricate technical detail here. The truly decisive quality of this proposal lies not in producing a finished neural-engineering blueprint overnight, but in demonstrating **the courage and mode of thinking to step outside existing frameworks and dissolve a "trolley problem" from the ground up**. Just as Section 3.4 examined the dynamic evolution of game rules and Section 9.2 dissected Adrian's *Watchmen*-style "choose the lesser evil" logic, both the panic that "falling behind means we must shackle AI" and the dread that "relying on AI means intellectual decay" treat today's biological constraints as eternal constants and argue over whom to run over along two pre-laid rails. What actually breaks open a civilization's frontier is never selecting which victim gets crushed on fixed tracks, but jumping clear of the lopsided old frame, locating the missing degree of freedom, and completing the symmetric return channel where AI accelerates human comprehension and creativity—once the rails themselves are rewritten, the forced choice between sacrificing AI or sacrificing humanity ceases to exist.

Having a single unified foundation causes zero problem: every living organism on Earth shares the single foundation of biological DNA, yet life never flattened into monotony or stalled in evolution; what matters is whether that architecture enables and accelerates diversity and evolvability. Negative fears toward a new framework typically arise from two sources: either real immature flaws inside the mechanism requiring repair, or misunderstandings among observers unfamiliar with how it works.
