# Nova Principia: Mathematical Principles of Social Science

## Relativity, From Physical to Social Theory

**新原理：社会科学的数学原理**

**从物理相对论到社会相对论**

Author: Tangram Liu (langram)

## Preface: The Cruel Lesson

Let me be frank: the resemblance between this book's title and the title of Newton's classic, between its subtitle and the title of Einstein's popular book, and between the title of this preface and the title of Sutton's essay, are all deliberate. In an age of information overload, attention is a scarce resource, and without an eye-catching title a book has almost no chance of being noticed. Clickbait is a necessity.

But the real justification for the title lies in the content.

The title of this preface is adapted from Sutton's "The Bitter Lesson". That essay looks back over seventy years of AI research, which is the history of humanity's attempt to reproduce in machines the complex system that is the mind, and finds the same thing happening again and again: every approach that relied on experts hand-writing their own knowledge and experience into the system worked in the short run and lost in the long run to approaches that relied on general mathematical models, enormous data and immense computing power. Sutton draws two conclusions. First, general methods have enormous power, and he names two of them: search and learning. Second, the contents of minds are too complex to be simplified, and we should not write our own understanding of them into the system; the only things that should be built in are the meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity.

Human civilization is a higher-order and more complex system built on top of human minds, and each of its units is itself a complex mind. If a single mind is too complex to be hand-written into a system, a civilization made of billions of minds is all the more so. So Sutton's conclusion extends quite naturally to civilization. In fact, has humanity's exploration of civilizational institutions over thousands of years not been living through this very lesson all along? To this day humanity solves the problems of constructing and organizing society by relying on political, economic and academic authorities who, on their personal experience and learning, each design by hand the institutions of states, the articles of law and the policies of economics, politics and diplomacy, and through these cooperate, compete and confront one another. A hand-designed institution has written into it the right and wrong of its designer's own reference frame; with no way of converting between frames, disagreements can only be settled by confrontation. The lesson of humanity's study of the mind is bitter, to be sure, but its cost was no more than detours taken and years and money wasted. On the road of exploring civilizational orders, the conflicts between people of different cognition and different ideologies have cost humanity blood and lives. This lesson is cruel and bloody; "bitter" is not a strong enough word for it.

Replace the complex system of the mind in Sutton's essay with the complex system of human civilization, and the conclusion should hold just the same. So there are two key questions: which are the meta-methods that can find and capture the complexity of human civilization? And on which most basic principles of mathematics and science do these meta-methods rest? To the first question this book's answer is: search is institutional experiment that forks, runs in parallel and is compared on a unified base; learning is dynamic weights, updated again and again by the feedback of real-world results. To the second, the main title and the subtitle of this book are the hint.

On top of a few basic laws, using rigorous logical reasoning and mathematical calculation, Newton's *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* built a complete and self-consistent physical system. Later generations shortened its name to the *Principia*, as if it had described the principles of everything. In fact it could not do that. But this does not diminish its value in the slightest. Its value lay not in how many phenomena it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided a unified architecture within which work could be accumulated. The next two hundred years of physics were all carried out inside that architecture.

Social science today is in exactly the state natural philosophy was in before Newton. The various schools of economics, sociology and political science contradict one another, are incompatible with one another, and lack the kind of unified axiomatic foundation that physics has. Most of them are less sciences than philosophical structures built on psychology, morality and experience, borrowing at most a few mathematical tools for statistical work. You might ask: is there any mathematical principle in existing political science? The different social theories are more like observers riding on multiple trains, each moving at a different speed and in a different direction, each creating a theory from its own observations, with no framework for converting between them or referring one to another.

So what this book sets out to do can be said in one sentence: to place social science on the same foundation of mathematical principles as natural science. This foundation does not need to be reinvented. The few principles that natural science has verified again and again over several hundred years, the variational principle, fixed points and equilibrium, symmetry and relativity, reduction and emergence, together with the game theory, information theory and machine learning of the twentieth century, are already enough. What this book does is take them out of their separate disciplines, line them up into one continuous chain of reasoning from physics to society, and then construct the theoretical framework of social science along that chain. The system of social science should be a natural extension of the system of natural science, not some additional set of rules outside the laws of cosmic evolution, governed by supernatural factors such as a creator god, consciousness or the soul.

The subtitle has two reasons.

The first reason is in the content. Symmetry is a basic feature of everything in the universe, and what it means mathematically is this: remaining unchanged under some transformation. The principle of relativity in physics is one such symmetry: the laws of physics remain unchanged under a change of reference frame. In Newtonian mechanics position and velocity were already relative, while time was still absolute; Einstein extended the principle of relativity to all the laws of physics, and time became relative too. Relativity abolished absolute time, absolute space and the absolute reference frame, and reduced the measures of time and space to relations between reference frames that can be converted exactly, while the laws themselves are the same in every frame. Since social science stands on the same foundation of mathematical principles as natural science, there is no reason for this symmetry to fail in the social domain. Each person's cognition is a reference frame formed by his genes, experience and environment, and none of them is privileged. So the relativity of the properties of space and time at the physical level, extended into the social domain, is the relativity of cognitive reference frames: there is no absolute right and wrong, good and bad, justice and evil, only coordinates formed under different conditions, and rules for converting between them; what does not vary is the laws that hold in every frame and the facts that can be checked. What the observers on those trains lack is exactly a Lorentz transformation. This is also what the lesson means here: no reference frame is privileged, so no one's reference frame should be hand-written into institutions.

With the mathematical models and computational tools of mechanics, humanity analyzes the interactions between objects, brings unstable factors such as collision and friction under control, and has built complex buildings, equipment and tools; the same methods and tools can also be used to analyze the interactions between people of different cognition, to resolve conflicts and contradictions, and to construct complex mechanisms and organizations of human cooperation.

The second reason is practical, and urgent, and it is why this book cannot wait: humanity needs relativity to dissolve and remake the extreme binary oppositions in its own civilizational genes, and there is not much time left to do it.

In today's world the evolution of technology is accelerating while the evolution of institutions has stalled, and the structural gap between the two is approaching a critical point. Since the Second World War, the rules of the game between nations and the political and economic systems within them have seen almost no fundamental change; over the same period information technology has passed through five waves: the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet and AI. We can already manipulate single atoms, edit the human genome and build ships bound for space, and we have begun to automate intellectual labor on a large scale with AI, yet we still settle our conflicts with pyramid institutions designed centuries ago, and decide policies that touch billions of people by emotion and position. A civilization whose power over the outside world outruns its control of itself is a child of enormous strength and fragile mind, swinging a chain flail that weighs a ton: sooner or later it hits itself. Nuclear weapons were the first flail; it came down twice, on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and in the decades since, humanity has been lucky enough not to let it fall again. AI is the second, a revolutionary technology that frightens and excites the whole of humanity at once, and double-edged like nuclear technology.

But the deeper crisis is in the humans who wield these technologies. Human history is an evolutionary record full of violent conflict, driven over and over by the tit-for-tat strategy, and that history has deposited in traditional religion and in morality and ethics a mass of emotional genes of extreme binary opposition, such as angels and demons, justice and evil. The conflicts that play out again and again between individuals, between groups and between nations are all these genes acting as catalyst and accelerator: define the other side as evil, and then every violent means becomes justice. In the nuclear age humanity was lucky enough to escape all-out nuclear war. In this age empowered by AI, the lethality of the state's machinery of violence will be multiplied many times over. How likely is it that we get lucky twice? The framing of relativity is precisely the dissolution and remaking of this civilizational gene: it reduces "justice and evil" to "differences between reference frames that have conditions of formation", and reduces moral judgment to facts that can be checked and rules that can be changed. Once that remaking is done, the same tit-for-tat strategy turns from the catalyst of violent conflict into the catalyst and accelerator of positive cooperation under a new civilizational order. So what the subtitle describes is both the foundational thesis of this book's whole theory and the means by which humanity can dissolve and remake this civilizational gene.

The rapid development of AI in the last few years has given everything said above the objective conditions for becoming real:

First, large AI language models have given the human space of consciousness a mathematical model for the first time. The whole of human knowledge has been integrated into a high-dimensional semantic space of billions of parameters: any property of an object can be represented as a multi-dimensional vector, and the system formed by all objects and their properties can be described as a multi-dimensional tensor, which can be measured, recorded and processed, and on which higher-order social models can be designed, constructed and evolved. A Cartesian coordinate system and a Newtonian reference frame for social science have become technically possible for the first time.

Second, silicon-based computing devices can now possess human intelligence, and this fact provides the basis for a consensus: problems in the domain of human society can also be solved by mathematical and scientific models, just like problems in the natural domain. Every cognition and behavior of a person at the social level is an objective phenomenon that, like a natural phenomenon, can be observed, studied, quantified, recorded, analyzed and modeled.

The book has four parts. Part I is the principles: the minimization, maximization and fixed points that natural science has already given us, the extension of relativity from physical reference frames to cognitive reference frames, tit-for-tat as the iteration operator that constructs social structure, and common knowledge and the two chain reactions. Part II is the construction: two kinds of resources and equality in the sense of the rules, the social neural network with dynamic weights, artificial intelligence as public infrastructure, and the Large Civilization Model in which training and inference are one. Part III is the foundation: a carrier of common knowledge in which content is identity, and every person's digital space and data sovereignty. Part IV is the path: the gap between technology and institutions, alignment and the fork in the road, peaceful evolution starting from one person's cloud storage, and science, culture and shared creation.

On method, I want to make three things clear first.

First, I have had no professional training in mathematics, and rigorous formal proofs are beyond my ability. The path this book takes has two stretches: the first sets existing scientific and mathematical knowledge that commands broad consensus side by side, to bring out its common structure; the second starts from that structure and deduces the construction of society step by step. In the second I have tried to make the premises reliable enough and the reasoning simple and intuitive enough that every step can be followed by a reader with a university education, and every step can also be refuted by the reader at a specific link.

Second, a word of warning in advance, with an example from chess. The evolution of a complex system often gets stuck in a locally optimal state of play, like a chess position in which both sides' pieces are tangled together, neither willing to give anything up, as if whoever wins this skirmish wins the whole game. A true master has a higher intuition, and the courage to sacrifice a piece and enter a temporarily worse position in order to reach the global optimum. The proposal in this book is counterintuitive, and as you read on you will be unable to resist denouncing it as the reckless audacity of an outsider, like a child casually sending the queen into the enemy's line of fire. Please have a little more patience. Once the first move of the sacrifice is played, the position often suddenly opens up. When trying to reform a civilizational order that has fallen far behind technological evolution, one must have the courage to break every stale habit of thought and step outside the existing framework. Do not let the concepts of nation, regime and corporation, those traditional pyramid structures, confine your imagination of the future. In the evolving system that is the universe, every entity, structure and evolving object, along with its rules, has a beginning, and will have its moment of extinction or transformation.

Third, the parts of this book differ in how reliable they are, and I keep them apart. The foundation in mathematics and science uses theories that are already settled; the generalizations and institutional designs I build on it are inferences that can be questioned and ought to be tested; the part on information systems is an engineering proposal premised on current and foreseeable computing and AI capabilities. The appendix at the end of the book spells out item by item which parts are grounds, which are inferences, and which could be started on today. As for the hypotheses I have thought about for a long time but that still lack broad consensus, category theory and the dualoid, the mathematical roots of consciousness, and many more distant ideas in engineering and civilization, I have put them separately into a document, "Extensions: Experimental Hypotheses and Ideas", and they are not premises of the argument in this book.

Last, let me stress that I have not discovered and defined the specific rules and structures of a new human civilizational order. What this book proposes is a foundation of mathematical principles for social science, extended naturally from the mathematical foundation of natural science, together with some meta-methods, meta-strategies and a plan for getting started on building a new civilizational order. They are like the mathematical principles of the early days of large language models: those theories and designs were a compass, a telescope, a chart, but not the destination; the day they were written down did not mean that a powerful AI model at once appeared in perfect form. In the same way, these theoretical ideas of mine are only a torch held up to scout the road toward the singularity of civilization. The traditional strategies by which human civilization evolved over the past several thousand years have achieved a great deal, to be sure, but they were also full of setbacks and failures, and were paid for with countless blood and lives. I hope that on the new road people can travel a little more smoothly and a little faster, and need no longer pay for their lessons in blood and lives.

Good. Now let us begin.

# Part I: Principles

## Chapter 1: Minimization, Maximization and Fixed Points

### The foundation is already there

This book does not start from a new mathematical object. It starts from the three most reliable things in natural science: the variational principle, fixed points, emergence. Each of the three is textbook material, and each has been tested for several hundred years. What I do is set them side by side, bring out one and the same structure, and then carry that structure all the way into society.

### Minimization is everywhere in natural science

From the microscopic to the macroscopic, from physics to chemistry to biology, the principle of minimization is visible everywhere.

At the level of elementary particles, the motion of all particles and fields obeys the principle of least action; the paths they choose make the action stationary, usually a minimum. At the chemical level, electrons outside the nucleus preferentially occupy the lowest-energy orbitals; at constant temperature and pressure, reactions always proceed spontaneously in the direction of decreasing Gibbs free energy; molecules always adjust their spatial structure to minimize total potential energy, which is why the water molecule takes its V shape with an angle of 104.5 degrees. At the biological level, the folding of an amino acid chain into its native structure is a search for the lowest point of free energy; phospholipid molecules spontaneously form bilayer membranes in water in order to minimize the free energy of the system; metabolic pathways and neuronal connections are all optimized under evolutionary pressure toward states that consume less matter and energy. At the level of gravitation, a celestial body made of free-flowing fluid spontaneously gathers toward its center, so that its total gravitational potential energy reaches a minimum, which is why large bodies collapse into spheres. General relativity interprets gravity as geometry: an object acted on by no external force moves along a geodesic, the path between two points in spacetime along which proper time is stationary; making the Einstein-Hilbert action stationary yields the field equations.

### Every minimization is a maximization

Nearly all of these minimization principles can, mathematically, be seen equivalently as the maximization of some corresponding physical quantity.

In relativity, minimizing the action is equivalent to maximizing proper time: a freely falling object's own clock runs fastest. In thermodynamics, minimizing Gibbs free energy at constant temperature and pressure is maximizing the total entropy of system plus environment: phospholipids packing tightly into a membrane is a local decrease in entropy, and what it buys is the complete liberation of the surrounding water molecules. In chemistry, minimizing the total potential energy of a system is equivalent to maximizing bond energy: the more energy released, the more firmly the atoms are bound. In optics, Fermat's principle says light takes the path of least time, and from the path-integral viewpoint this means that only near this path do the phases add constructively, while the other paths interfere destructively.

Minimization and maximization are two faces of the variational principle. This is not my discovery, and strictly speaking what these principles require is a stationary value, a minimum being only the most common case. What I have done is set them side by side to bring out the common structure, and then take it out of physics.

### Fixed points: the mathematical name for stable structure

A state of a system that no longer changes under the repeated action of some rule of change is called, in mathematics, a fixed point. The equilibrium states of physics and chemistry are fixed points. The attractors of dynamical systems are fixed points and their generalization: nearby states are all pulled toward them. The Nash equilibrium of game theory is also a fixed point: each person's strategy is the best response to the strategies of the others, and no one can gain by changing unilaterally; what Nash used to prove its existence was precisely a fixed point theorem.

There is another class of especially important stable structures that are not in equilibrium. A flame, a cell, a living organism, a city all maintain themselves by continuously consuming energy, and fall apart the moment the flow of energy is cut. Prigogine called them dissipative structures. When this book later speaks of people, organizations, nations and civilizations, it treats each of them as a dissipative structure: a stable structure that acquires and converts energy through its internal structure and external tools, and by doing so maintains and develops itself.

So "things that stably exist" have the same mathematical name at every level. The atom is a fixed point under the electromagnetic interaction, the molecule is a fixed point of chemical bonding, the species is a fixed point of the evolutionary game, the institution is a fixed point of the social game.

### Emergence: more is different

The fixed point describes existence; emergence describes change. Philip Anderson made this clear in "More Is Different": basic objects at a lower level, through combination, form stable structures at a higher level, and the higher level has laws of its own that cannot be simply derived from the laws of the lower level. Chemistry is not applied physics, and biology is not applied chemistry. But the existence of the higher-level structure still obeys every constraint of the lower level.

Put emergence and fixed points together: the fixed points of each level are the basic building blocks from which the next level is constructed. Atoms make up molecules, molecules make up cells, cells make up living organisms, people make up society. No link in this chain needs an extra, supernatural rule.

### A working principle that runs through every level

Put the three things above together and you get the working principle of this book:

**For a stable structure, the internal cost of maintaining its own existence tends toward minimization, while the paths by which it establishes connections outward with other objects tend toward maximization. The maximization of connections among objects at a lower level is, seen from the level above, the minimization of the internal cost of the newly emerged structure. The two processes complement each other and advance in alternation.**

The first half is the common form that the variational principle and thermodynamics take at every level; the second half is a summary of dissipative structures and of the whole history of evolution. At the physical level it has an exact mathematical form; at the biological and social levels it is a generalization made by this book, an organizing framework, not yet a theorem. Whether it has value depends on whether it can yield operational definitions and testable inferences at the social level. Chapter 3 gives the first one.

Scientific research itself is an example of this principle: the minimization of basic theory, Newtonian mechanics relative to natural philosophy, gave people a far stronger ability to transform the external world and construct things that did not previously exist; and new tools in turn promote progress in basic science.

### From the theory of evolution to society

Darwin's survival of the fittest, described with this principle, says: in the functions required to maintain their own existence, living things always tend to evolve toward forms that consume less energy; in connecting with the outside world, they always tend to evolve in the direction of being able to relate to more objects. An example of the former is the increase in the efficiency of digestion and metabolism; examples of the latter are the passage from single-celled to multicellular organisms, and from solitary living to social cooperation.

Human society is the same. The individual's instinct to maintain its own survival, the instinct to develop social relationships and economic interests, Nash equilibrium, Pareto optimality, the cost-cutting, efficiency-raising and profit-maximizing of firms: these phenomena of minimization and maximization are everywhere. Even the demands made at the level of politics can be interpreted by this principle: the right to life corresponds to a stable structure's instinct to maintain itself; freedom corresponds to the maximization of relations with other objects; democracy corresponds to the maximization of the emergent higher-order group organization; and equality is the precondition that truly allows individual interest and group interest to be maximized at the same time. Eliminating the inequality of power is the best way to eliminate the waste of resources in the whole system: it minimizes the internal friction of the whole system while maximizing the free connections among the individuals within it. Marx's sentence, "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all", is, I believe, saying exactly this.

### A ruler: the energy-efficiency ratio

This principle gives the book a ruler that it uses from beginning to end. When comparing two complex systems, if you compare their advanced features one by one, you will very likely find that each has its own strengths and the argument never ends. The other way is to start from the bottom: have two systems with the same function complete the same task, compare the output first, then compare the energy consumed in the process, and get the energy-efficiency ratio. From here on, whenever this book compares two strategies, two institutions or two architectures, it uses this ruler.

Theories can be measured with it too. Occam's razor says: among the theories that explain the same phenomena, the shortest is the best. In the language of the theory of computation, a theory is a program, the phenomena are its output, and the length of the shortest program is called the Kolmogorov complexity. This optimum is itself uncomputable, but it can be approached step by step. So the evaluation function for a good theory is the construction of the greatest external connectivity at the least internal cost. Chapter 14 will use it again when discussing how humans and AI do research together.

### Evaluation is not a judgment on the right to exist

This ruler has a limit that must be stated clearly. The evaluation function is a descriptive tool, not a judgment on the legitimacy of existence. We can judge that the hunting ability of felines far exceeds that of rodents, but we cannot say the existence of felines has higher value. Everything that exists is a fixed point produced by some converging minimization process; the value of every direction of evolution lies in enriching the space of evolution and preparing for the next emergence.

So later in this book I will compare institutions and strategies at great length, better and worse, higher and lower, but please remember: what is being compared is the energy-efficiency ratio and the capacity to evolve, not the right to exist. Having a better option does not mean the old option must be eliminated. Maintaining diversity is the safer and faster way to achieve evolution. This point will come up again in Chapter 3 when discussing lenient and strict game strategies.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: In physics, "action" and "free energy" have precise mathematical definitions and measurable values. In social science, "least cost" and "internal friction of the system" do not. Without a precise definition, it is not a falsifiable proposition but a rhetorical frame that can be explained any way you like after the fact.**

A: The value of Newton's framework in 1687 lay not in how much it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided an architecture within which work could accumulate; at that time no one could write a mathematical description of how steam acts after water boils, and that does not show the framework was wrong. But I also accept the reasonable part of the objection: Newton in 1687 did give operational definitions of mass, force and acceleration, and that is the minimum bar for a theory in its infancy. So the demand this book places on itself is this: every time this principle is applied to a specific field, it must come down to an operational definition. Its first operational definition in the social domain is the strictness of Chapter 3: the intensity of punishment and the interval before forgiveness in the tit-for-tat strategy, as the control parameter for the convergence speed and stability of social structure.

**Q: This merely reorganizes what is already known. Does that count as something new?**

A: Reorganizing language to express the same thing is not a meaningless act. It makes previously invisible relationships visible, lets bridges be built between previously independent fields, and lets later generations do work in the new language that could not be done in the old one. The entire wager of this book lies here: whether it lets people, for the first time, ask questions that could not be asked in the old language of social science. When you have finished Part I, you can judge for yourself.

## Chapter 2: Relativity: From Physical Reference Frames to Cognitive Reference Frames

### Reference frames from physics to cognition

A reference frame is first of all a mathematical concept: a set of coordinate axes, an origin, a metric. Physics tells us that all reference frames are relative, and no reference frame is entitled to call itself absolutely at rest. Galilean relativity says the laws of mechanics are the same in all inertial frames; Einstein extended it to all physical laws and pointed out that simultaneity itself depends on the reference frame. The principle of relativity is a symmetry: the form of the laws stays unchanged under a transformation of reference frame. Symmetry is not decoration in physics: Noether proved that every continuous symmetry corresponds to a conserved quantity.

This concept can be extended all the way down the line. In chemistry, the same reaction has a different equilibrium position at different temperatures and pressures. In biology, the environment perceived by every individual is relative to its own receptors and metabolic state: the bat's world is a world of echoes, the dog's world a world of smells. In these fields relativity holds just the same: the result of observation depends on the state of the observer, but between different observers there exist laws of mutual conversion.

So why is it that when we reach the social domain, especially the domains of morality, politics and ideology, everyone becomes self-centered, takes their own cognitive coordinate system as the standard of absolute correctness, and treats everything inconsistent with themselves as wrong?

Because taking oneself as the origin is a basic property of any stable structure: every fixed point described in the previous chapter relates to the outside world starting from itself. The problem is not that people have their own coordinates, but that they treat their own coordinate origin as the center of the universe. Today, large AI language models have for the first time made human cognition into a measurable high-dimensional semantic space. Each person's cognition is a position and a direction in this space; language and action based on this cognitive space are like motion in physical spacetime. Each person's reference frame is different, and this is why there is trust and estrangement, cooperation and betrayal between people.

### A social science version of the principle of relativity

So, after minimization and maximization and fixed points, the principles part of this book adds one more: the principle of relativity.

Among all human worldviews, epistemologies, political leanings, moral conceptions and cognitive systems, the differences in position, inclination and motion along every cognitive and behavioral dimension of semantic space are relative. There is no absolute right and wrong, no absolute good and bad. When one person thinks another is "wrong", it is like a passenger on one train thinking that another train is moving backward: he is describing the relative motion between two reference frames, not an absolute property of either side.

This principle does not abolish facts. The relative velocity of two trains is an objective, measurable quantity, and so is the difference between two people's cognitive coordinates. What it abolishes is the entitlement to treat one's own coordinate origin as the center of the universe. The goal of social science is to find the laws of conversion between different cognitive reference frames, just as the Lorentz transformation connects the measurements of different inertial frames. The reason today's various social theories are incompatible with one another is precisely that each sits on its own train and this transformation does not yet exist.

Symmetry also has a direct counterpart in the social domain: equality. A rule is equal for two people, A and B, only if its content stays unchanged when the two of them are swapped within it. Symmetry breaking, in turn, is the starting point of division of labor, differentiation and hierarchy. Chapter 5 will turn this into equality in the sense of the rules.

### Refusing mystification, sanctification and demonization

In poetry and fiction you may emotionalize, sanctify and demonize the objects of nature: the gentle wind, the holy light, the demonic darkness. But such expressions must be kept out of theoretical research and technical practice in natural science. The objects of human society need to be treated the same way. Every cognitive and behavioral phenomenon at the social level of humanity is, like a natural phenomenon, an objective phenomenon that can be observed, studied, quantified, recorded, analyzed and modeled.

This leads to something I call meta-cognition, which is the psychological premise for everything argued in the rest of this book:

All the differences and conflicts among humans, between individual and individual, individual and group, group and group, in appearance, physiology, personality, cognition, language, behavior and needs, these differences at the higher level, are structural differences at various lower levels that have gradually formed over the long course of evolution and in the game of human society. Every one of these differences can be given a reproducible causal-chain explanation built from scientific theory. People should not be driven by emotions such as the fear produced by ignorance and the anger produced by confrontation. The so-called fierce binary oppositions of angels and demons, justice and evil, are in fact nothing more than extreme irrational emotions, in the manner of moral and religious judgment, produced when the forward and reverse chain reactions of the tit-for-tat strategy in a long social game catalyze and amplify them.

By "lower-order structural differences" I do not mean attributing a higher-order conflict to some single clean lower-order source. People often say that red is joyful and blue is melancholy, but that does not mean a light wave in some range of wavelengths has the property of joy. A property like violence does not exist at the biological level; it is the product of many factors compounded together, genetic inheritance, living environment and so on. The properties of any lower-order object are not good or bad, only different in character; once we understand the mechanism, we can combine these factors correctly. Reducing plague to pathogens, reducing epilepsy to neuronal discharges: reductions of this kind eliminated a class of narratives that moralized human differences, and they historically reduced actual conflict. What I want to do is extend this to the whole of social conflict.

### The correspondence of concepts

Mapping the concepts of mathematics and basic physics onto the concepts of society is the basic method of this book:

- Vectors and dimensions: the position and direction of an individual's cognition in semantic space; the multiple influencing factors of a social structure.
- Symmetry: equality. Symmetry breaking: the starting point of division of labor, differentiation and hierarchy.
- Relativity: cognitive reference frames.
- Fixed point: stable social structures, Nash equilibrium, institutions.
- Minimization: the lowest-cost way of resolving conflict, Pareto optimality, cutting costs and raising efficiency.
- Maximization: the maximization of relationships and cooperation, freedom, the maximization of interests.

One more pair of concepts deserves an extra sentence: active and passive. Your active behavior causes someone else's passive reaction, and your passive reaction is caused by someone else's active behavior; rights and obligations, freedom and constraint, individual and environment, each pair is two faces of one coupled system. So the individual's objective function is neither defined entirely by the individual nor designed entirely from outside; it is a game process in which the individual and the environment are coupled to each other and which keeps changing as it runs. This point will be made concrete in Chapter 6 when discussing decision weights: at every moment of evolution the individual must retain the actual ability to change the direction of its trajectory, otherwise "coupling" becomes a rhetoric for domination.

### From cognitive reference frames to joint construction

Acknowledging relativity does not mean nothing can be said. Acknowledging that two trains each have their own reference frame does not stop us from calculating precisely when they will collide. Acknowledging that each person's cognitive coordinates have their own conditions of formation does not stop us from jointly verifying facts and jointly setting rules of cooperation. Quite the opposite: only once relativity is acknowledged can the argument move from "whose coordinates are absolute" to "how do our coordinates convert into one another", and only then can moral judgments be reduced to facts that can be discussed and rules that can be revised. What the next two chapters do is find, on this foundation, the basic operator that constructs society.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Does saying "there is no absolute right or wrong, good or bad" abolish responsibility?**

A: No. The principle of relativity abolishes the entitlement to treat one's personal coordinates as absolute coordinates; it does not abolish facts, still less harm. That a person considers something justified within his own reference frame does not change the loss that thing causes in another reference frame. Chapter 4 will show that under a verifiable, traceable framework of common knowledge, the assignment of responsibility is actually more precise than under a framework of moral judgment: what gets recorded is this act, this decision, this loss, rather than a label of "evil" stuck on a person.

**Q: Social systems have a peculiarity: the participants can understand the laws of the system and change their behavior accordingly, and a law changes as soon as it is known. This is unlike physics.**

A: It is indeed unlike physics, but it is not an obstacle; it is the content. Reflexivity is itself a feedback loop, and feedback loops are exactly what can be incorporated into a dynamical description. The social science this book sets out to build is not "objective laws independent of the participants' cognition" but "laws that still hold after everyone knows them". The equilibria that game theory studies are laws of exactly this kind: the definition of a Nash equilibrium is that everyone knows and still no one changes. Minimization and maximization, fixed points, and relativity all belong to this kind.

## Chapter 3: Tit-for-Tat: The Iteration Operator That Constructs Social Structure

### What social science lacks is a basic building block

The reason social science lags so far behind natural science in precision and reliability is that it has never found the properties and laws of its basic building block. What this chapter sets out to do is find that building block, and show how it constructs the various structures of society.

Take the analogy between human society and a chemical system. The basic object of a chemical system is the atom, and atoms connect through chemical bonds to form all kinds of molecules; the rules for forming chemical bonds are determined by the properties of the atom's outer electrons, the whole process follows energy minimization, and it finally reaches a stable fixed-point state. In human society, the basic object is the person, and the simplest connection between one person and another is cooperation. Cooperation is reached through repeated games driven by each side's own interests, and that process likewise follows the principle of Chapter 1: the Nash equilibrium is a fixed point in strategy; Pareto optimality is the minimized state of resource allocation, where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

So what inner property of the human individual corresponds to the outer electron structure of the atom? The answer is a piece of common knowledge rooted in our physiology and psychology and universally observed in games: tit-for-tat. The strategy was proposed by Rapoport, and it won twice in Axelrod's computer tournaments of the repeated prisoner's dilemma: cooperate on the first move; after that, if the other side cooperates, I cooperate; if the other side defects, I punish; if the other side comes back, I forgive. It is nice, provocable and forgiving, and so clear that the other side understands it at a glance.

### Reward and punishment are the evaluation function, tit-for-tat is the iteration operator

Apply the principle of Chapter 1 here: the game between people is the dynamic process that constructs the fixed-point structures of human society. The mechanism of reward and punishment is an evaluation function; tit-for-tat is the iteration process of that function. It makes structures which satisfy the evaluation function tend to converge, and structures which do not tend to separate, instead of combining and separating at random without ever forming a stable structure. Through tit-for-tat, people reward cooperation and punish defection, which sets off chain reactions that keep strengthening or weakening their ties, continually constructing every kind of relational structure between people, just as atoms form chemical bonds by losing, gaining and sharing their outer electrons. This chain effect of positive and negative feedback is the basic reaction of human social construction.

Its operation is controlled jointly by three layers, physiological, psychological and rational. At the bottom is the physiological hardware: when an individual gains from cooperation, the brain's reward circuits produce pleasure; when his interests are damaged by betrayal, the stress system produces fear and anger. In the middle is emotion, which is a fast heuristic algorithm for decision-making and supplies the motivation for rational thought. At the top is reason: driven by emotion, it runs a cost-benefit analysis and finally returns reward for goodwill and punishment for malice.

### Strictness: the first operational definition

Tit-for-tat strategies of different intensity affect the speed of convergence, the strength and the stability of the fixed-point structure. This is the first operational definition this book gives to social science: **strictness, meaning the intensity of punishment for defection and the time interval required before forgiveness.**

Strictness is a continuous spectrum from lenient to strict. The universality of this spectrum shows itself in the fact that human structures, at whatever level and in whatever concrete organization, always differentiate from lenient to strict. The members of a family, a corporate board, the parliament of a democracy, the politburo of an authoritarian state, any association at all: among the individuals taking part there is always a spectrum from doves to hawks.

This differentiation has ready-made mathematics. The hawk-dove game of evolutionary game theory tells us: when the contested gain is smaller than the cost of conflict, the group stabilizes in a mixed state of hawks and doves, and the proportion of hawks equals the ratio of the gain to the cost. The proportion is set by the payoff structure, regardless of who the individuals are.

### Who stands on which side: genes determine the ordering, the game determines the cut-off point

So what does who the individuals are decide? It decides who crosses the line first.

I split a person's decision into two parts: the part that is rational thought, and an emotional amplification factor. The latter is a physiological parameter that sets the intensity and duration of emotional response. Different people respond to betrayal with emotions of different intensity and duration; in the execution of tit-for-tat, that difference shows up as different punishment intensity and different forgiveness intervals; in an actual game, one kind behaves more like a hawk and the other more like a dove. But the effect size of a single gene is tiny, and it depends heavily on the environment a person grows up in; taken directly as an explanation of political systems, it does not stand.

The correct position is this: genes determine the ordering, the game determines the cut-off point. Run a thought experiment: cut out every hawk in a group; the remaining people get re-ordered by the relative intensity of their emotional responses, and as the game iterates in chain reaction, the relatively more reactive portion differentiates into hawks again, and the proportion drifts back to the neighborhood set by the ratio of the gain to the cost of conflict. Genetically identical mice in identical environments still form stable dominance and subordination hierarchies, which shows that differentiation itself happens even without genetic differences: iterated games amplify tiny random differences into stable roles, which is symmetry breaking; when genetic differences exist, they set the starting point of the amplification. The type is individual; the proportion is game-theoretic.

### Why authoritarianism is a stable attractor

At the level of the group, the acquired environment of the game matters far more than genes.

By the principle of minimization, people always tend to resolve disagreements in the way that costs least. In an environment that is already under authoritarian rule, the power gap is enormous, violent suppression is the cheapest method, and so people tend to solve problems by violence. This happens again and again. On one side, those inclined to such methods become more successful within the system and are rewarded, and the reward encourages more people to adopt such methods; on the other side, violent suppression builds up enormous rebound tension in the suppressed, and that tension pushes those in power to keep widening the power gap in order to increase structural stability; the widened power gap takes us back to the iteration we started from. The whole structure converges to an authoritarian system of enormous size, enormous power gap and extremely high structural strength. This is a positive feedback loop, with a clear mechanism at every step, relying on no narrative of "evil rulers," derived purely from the principle of minimization, selection pressure and feedback loops. It explains why authoritarian systems are so stable: they are attractors, and every iteration reinforces them.

When hard strategies are cheap in an authoritarian environment, cheap for whom? Cheap for those above, extremely expensive for those below. So that environment does not just push everyone's baseline toward hardness; it binds strategy to position: hawk downward, dove upward. An arrangement that decides who yields by position rather than by strength saves the cost of fighting, which is why it is stable, and that is exactly where the pyramid is cheap. The same logic explains the hawks in the democratic camp: violence is expensive at home and cheap abroad, because outsiders have no channel for appeal or retaliation, and so the same people are lenient inside and hard outside. The history of colonialism is the product of this payoff structure.

Conversely, in the environment of a democratic system, violent suppression is the more expensive method, and people tend more toward peaceful communication, playing against each other with lenient tit-for-tat; even a person whose emotions are more intense will tend toward lenient methods in that environment. The payoff structure of the environment sets the baseline of the group's strategy and the proportion between the two camps; individual differences decide who crosses the line first at the critical state.

### Two higher fixed points

In a space shaped by the overlapping influence of geographical, physiological, psychological, economic and political factors, human civilization has evolved two important higher fixed-point structures: the authoritarian system and the democratic system. They correspond to the strict version and the lenient version of the tit-for-tat strategy.

The structural strength and stability of the strict strategy are enough to support a mighty empire. But an overly strong single structure usually slows the pace of a civilization's evolution, and the collapse and reconstruction of such a structure is enormously costly: in China every dynastic change has been accompanied by massive loss of life, and even so, it was only a change of dynasty. High strictness means fast convergence and strong stability, and it also means being easily trapped in a locally optimal fixed point and unable to climb out; a structure that is too fragile will not do either. How to construct a sensible mechanism of reward and punishment is the fundamental factor in how strong a human civilization's capacity to evolve will be.

Geographical parameters have to be counted too. In the relatively isolated geography of East Asia, with its large continuous farming regions, the population was tied to the land and exit was extremely expensive; only these factors combined with the positive feedback above produced the continuous development of the authoritarian system. In the fragmented, open environment of the Mediterranean, with its diverse and mobile mix of farming, herding and sea trade, exit was cheap, cooperation had to invent portable reputation, and so horizontal institutions grew up: merchant law, credit, guilds. The parameter of exit cost will be used again in Chapter 13, when I discuss the free zones. Only with these concrete parameters substituted in does this theory become a system of social science that can be tested against the data of human history and is falsifiable.

### Why preserving difference is more powerful than eliminating it

Leading in energy-efficiency ratio only means leading overall in most situations; it does not mean leading in every local respect. Nor is the strict strategy inferior to the lenient strategy in every situation: when forces must be concentrated to complete quickly a large task that is structurally complex but highly deterministic, the former is more efficient. Even the armies of democratic countries are based on the strict strategy; in wartime, governments tighten centralization. So while tilting overall toward the strategy with the higher energy-efficiency ratio, diversity must be preserved, and the lenient strategy preserves diversity better.

Chapter 6 will describe a virtual version of tit-for-tat: it moves reward and punishment from material goods and violence in reality to points in a digital space, turning real damage into virtual damage in a game.

### Crime is a disease, not evil

From the mechanism of this chapter a conclusion about crime follows. If you regard crime as evil and the criminal as a demon, then at the very bottom of your cognition you are already bound by the emotion of moral judgment; you will be strongly inclined to wipe it out directly by violence and to refuse to understand it and correct it through scientific research. Simple violent elimination can only eliminate the individuals already produced; it cannot eliminate the mechanism that produces them. This and "cutting out half the hawks" are two faces of the same thing: what changes is the individuals, not the proportion.

To regard crime as a disease is to put this phenomenon into the framework of science, where treatment and prevention aim at the mechanism: reduce the gains that can be seized, raise the cost of harmful acts, insert a delay between punishment and emotion, leave room for appeal against misjudgment. "Disease" here means a phenomenon that can be studied, prevented and corrected; what gets recorded is the harmful act, not a category pinned on the person. This is the direct application, in the criminal field, of the meta-cognition from Chapter 2.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The conditions under which Axelrod proved that tit-for-tat wins, a two-player game, a fixed payoff matrix, long repetition, no communication, are not strictly satisfied in most real organizations.**

A: That is exactly why I do not treat "tit-for-tat" as a concrete strategy table but as the iteration operator of the evaluation function. In any sufficiently repeated multi-party game, the participants evolve some mechanism of reward and punishment to stabilize cooperation; conditional cooperation, indirect reciprocity, reputation mechanisms, third-party punishment are all different instances of this operator under more complex game structures. They all present a strict-lenient differentiation precisely because any reward-punishment operator can be parameterized along a strictness dimension. Strictness is the control parameter of the operator; convergence speed and stability are its outputs. As for the differences between a family and a parliament in kinship, symmetry of power, number of repetitions and exit cost, those are exactly what I called the concrete parameters: they change the operator's values in that situation, not the operator itself.

## Chapter 4: Common Knowledge and the Two Chain Reactions

### What common knowledge is

Human social structures that rest on law, religious codes, political programs, and the moral standards and rules of the game that people have agreed on and jointly observe can become strong, stable and complex; structures that rest on the will of a few or the unwritten rules of a small circle can only be simple, fragile and temporary structures. The difference is that the former are common knowledge: everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on recursively. Game theory gives this concept a strict definition, and it is the precondition for any coordination to happen at all: the same fact leads to completely different behavior when each person knows it separately and when everyone knows that everyone knows it.

A human organization of any size, from a family at the small end up through villages, cities and nations to the whole global village, is founded on the intersection of consensus its participants can reach. And tit-for-tat is precisely the most basic piece of common knowledge that all of humanity knows and takes for granted. Under this piece of common knowledge, trust and cooperation form the positive chain reaction, and distrust running into betrayal and conflict forms the negative chain reaction.

### The negative chain

Insufficient or concealed exchange of information leads to mutual suspicion; suspicion further obstructs exchange, making both sides deliberately conceal more information; this plunges both sides into greater suspicion, until it develops into distrust; distrust leads to refusal to communicate and all-out concealment, and from there to intense emotional hostility; then, out of fear of the other side's attack, you start strengthening your own offensive and defensive capacity, and that behavior of yours leads the other side to strengthen theirs; events escalate to resistance and confrontation in action, from local, low-intensity acts of violence step by step upward until the situation is completely out of control, turning the ordinary people inside it from friendly cooperators full of love and reason into impulsive killers full of hate. The study of international relations calls the second half of this chain the security dilemma.

This chain is not a theoretical derivation. Boeing's concealment of the 737 MAX flight control system and the whistleblower crisis that followed, Volkswagen's Dieselgate, both were caused by it. Imagine if all the information from design to testing to production to sales were like version-tracked open-source code in a git repository: this suspicion and distrust could hardly arise. Business is only one class of example; some cases carry the risk of destroying civilization: from the outbreak of the two world wars, to the US-Soviet Cold War and the Cuban missile crisis, to the confrontation between the United States and China today.

The more both sides fear that something will happen, the more likely it is to happen. The endpoint of this negative chain, whichever possibility you take, is not a global optimum: either the two sides are evenly matched and reach a local equilibrium of mutual deterrence; or one side pulls far ahead and uses its lead to utterly destroy or suppress the other. Either way it carries the risk of losing control and self-destruction. This is also why humanity fears nuclear technology and fears AI.

### The positive chain

Now the reverse: every bit more of information exchanged, every bit less concealed, increases mutual trust; increased trust makes both sides more willing to share information, more willing to cooperate; cooperation further strengthens trust and brings more cooperation. This positive chain finally leads every party to turn all information relevant to promoting cooperation into common knowledge, making the probability of reaching the globally optimal equilibrium larger and larger.

The loss caused by concealment exceeds the concealed fact itself. The opacity and asymmetry of information not only keep the two sides from reaching the optimal game, but also keep the cost of mental and material confrontation between cooperating parties stubbornly high.

The theory of repeated games gives a key conclusion here: under the same payoffs, long-term cooperation and long-term mutual defection are both equilibria. The payoff structure determines which equilibria exist, not which one you land in; what decides where you land is the initial expectation and the direction of the first move. So if we want the human social game driven by tit-for-tat to reach its optimum, the initial direction of the game matters enormously; it is the single most critical step in whether the chain reaction runs positive or negative.

### The optimal rules of the game

So the optimal rules of the game are to make the following rules common knowledge for all of humanity:

1. Everyone follows the tit-for-tat strategy.
2. Everyone's initial strategy is the positive strategy, that is, to share all information that is needed to reach external cooperation and that will not harm oneself.
3. All shared information is recorded and shared as common knowledge in the form of a Merkle-DAG, while a dynamic-weight decision system guarantees that no party's decision power ever grows large enough to use that information to harm the one who shared it.
4. If, because of the complexity and unpredictability of the system, the execution of the weighted decision system damages the interests of an individual in the system, compensation is made immediately and the game's decision strategy is immediately revised to repair it. All those who suffered loss treat the situation as a loss produced by the system's own capacity limits or by external force majeure, not as the malice of some decision-maker.
5. Where a temporary loss is suffered for the sake of a better long-term result, the decision system supplies an estimated plan and expected outcome, monitors the progress of the game at all times, adjusts the plan as circumstances require in order to succeed, or stops execution when failure is expected. On success, those who suffered loss treat it as a temporary sacrifice for long-term benefit; on failure, they treat it as a loss produced by the system's capacity limits or external force majeure, not as the malice of some decision-maker.

Two points in this rule system matter most. First, the verifiable common-knowledge storage system, and the social intelligent neural network of dynamic-weight decision-making, eliminate at the level of mechanism the possibility of individuals using the power in their hands to harm one another; Part III and Part II describe these two things respectively. Second, the point "everyone's initial strategy is the positive strategy" must be guaranteed on both the emotional and the rational side. The meta-cognition of Chapter 2, once it becomes everyone's consensus, gives people a calmer attitude toward the world and toward others, and more tolerance toward people who think differently; it also raises tolerance, reinterpreting a provocation from "the other side is evil" to "the product of the other side's structural differences in this situation," and a certain degree of tolerance is precisely the necessary condition for avoiding echoing vendettas in a noisy repeated game.

Meta-cognition is responsible for willingness; verifiable history is responsible for credibility. An initial cooperative attitude that is only declared is what game theory calls cheap talk: the other side cannot tell sincerity from disguise. A portable, checkable record of cooperation makes "I am ready to cooperate" no longer a sentence but a record that others can check. Only the two together make a first step that the other side can credit. So the new system does not need to first convince everyone and first dissolve all hostility before cooperation begins; what it needs is to make the first step a cooperative one, and to let the other side see it.

### The bottleneck is not technology but willingness

These two chains are idealized extremes, and the great majority of situations in reality lie between the two. But one thing is certain: humanity's ability to study and control complex systems keeps growing; computers gave people the ability to predict geology, oceans and weather; computers and AI gave people systematic understanding of, and the ability to modify, protein folding and the genetic system. For all of these complex systems, the data to be collected is not about us humans ourselves.

For the complex system that is human civilization, the information to be collected is the information of all people about themselves. For human beings, who have subjective agency, the capacity to collect and process information is not the bottleneck; the subjective willingness to provide that information is. Collecting information by force, through the power gap of the pyramid structure and the deterrence of the organs of violence, is the worst strategy: what is collected is data distorted under the pressure of power, deliberately concealed or falsified, neither complete nor true. This is the most important reason why humanity's capacity to understand and remake nature runs so far ahead of its capacity to understand and control its own social system.

The bottleneck the new mechanism must solve is exactly this one: eliminate the power gap, limit at the level of mechanism the abuse of power and the occurrence of harm, guarantee that the information people provide will not be turned against them, let people get positive returns from sharing, and guarantee that this positive feedback keeps running reliably and continuously.

### Behind privacy is power

What people fear in surveillance is not the technical means but the person who controls the means: whether that person's power is locked in a cage, whether that person will do something to harm them.

The so-called privacy and security problems all have their root here. Between any two objects in human society, whether the state against the individual, the company against the individual, or different individuals at the same level, the information barriers and the wariness all come from the fear that once the other side knows, it will use the power in its hands to gain the advantage in the game and inflict harm. Whether information becomes private is fundamentally tied to whether power is equal and whether the game is fair. The more equal, the more open the information, the higher the trust between individuals, and the stronger the willingness to cooperate. The same holds between person and person: how much two people know about each other is proportional to the degree of their cooperation and intimacy.

Push this mechanism to its end: the weak, in order to play against the powerful, refuse to provide personal information so as to strengthen their own capacity for defense, even though this lowers the cooperative efficiency of the whole system; these refusals and defenses in turn force those above to strengthen surveillance and their capacity for suppression in response. The inequality of power and information produces an unfair distribution of benefits, unfairness produces greater distrust, distrust raises the tension of conflict, the rise in tension leads those above to need a larger power gap to guarantee structural stability, and the larger power gap further produces unfair distribution. This is the same loop as the positive feedback of the previous chapter, at the level of information. It has produced the world we have now: science and technology are raising productivity fast, yet the gaps in class, in power, and between rich and poor grow ever wider. If we continue along the old system, AI will not become the tool that levels everyone's differences in ability; it will instead become the machine by which a few suppress and exploit the many.

### Locking power in a cage

The better way to lock power in a cage is a better mechanism for distributing power, rather than hoping that the person or group holding great power is a wise ruler or a saint. Under the pyramid system, however detailed the rules, the power to make the rules and the power to enforce them still sit at the apex. So what the cage must lock up, first of all, is the power to make the rules.

Conversely, as long as the mechanism guarantees that harm cannot happen, a virtuous circle arises, the exact opposite of the vicious circle of the old system: if no party has the ability to use the power in its hands to harm others, then harm does not happen in cooperation; the more certain it is that harm will not happen, the longer people cooperate and the more they trust each other; trust brings more sharing of information and deeper cooperation. Even if an accident in cooperation produces a temporary loss, since the mechanism rules out malicious harm, neither side will think the other acted out of malice, and cooperation will not end because of one accident. Friends and partners who get along well in real life relate to each other in exactly this way; the difference is that it depends on the personal character of both parties, not on an institutional guarantee.

"Open" here refers to information that concerns other objects in the outside world; data that can be encapsulated inside the individual and has no effect on the outside is not included. Chapter 10 will turn this boundary into concrete rules for viewing permissions.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Eliminate misunderstanding and hostility first, then cooperate: isn't the order reversed? A verifiable history can by itself sustain cooperation under distrust; contact and understanding should be the product of cooperation, not its precondition.**

A: I did not make the elimination of hostility a precondition of cooperation. The first move of tit-for-tat is to cooperate, and that move is not derived from the payoffs; it is an assumption about the other side. That is exactly what meta-cognition does: it does not change the payoffs, it changes the selection of equilibrium. The verifiable record does a different thing: it makes that assumption checkable. One is responsible for willingness, the other for credibility, and the order is: simultaneously.

# Part II: Construction

## Chapter 5: Two Kinds of Resources and Equality in the Sense of the Rules

### The interval of optimal marginal utility

Part I gave the principles and the basic operator. Starting with this chapter, I use them to construct. I begin with resources.

The Industrial Revolution gradually replaced human physical labor with automated equipment, and the abundance of material goods brought the basic needs of individual human survival to a state of basic satisfaction. As AI develops, intellectual labor will achieve higher efficiency too, and in the not distant future, every material and spiritual need of individual human survival can be supplied in full within the range of optimal marginal utility.

I mention optimal marginal utility because while too little supply is harmful to a living individual, too much supply is harmful as well. The water, food and nutritional balance a person needs fall within a range; the information the brain takes in, the intensity and duration of thinking, have an optimal interval too. Every person is a dissipative structure, and excessive distribution does not bring better results: fine food is good, but letting go of control and taking in too many calories damages health; however good short videos may be, drowning in them every day is certainly not good either. The biological instinct of a human being exists for survival in scarcity; in today's world of enormous material abundance, if instinct is left in charge, a person usually cannot make the best choices about material intake or spiritual intake.

So when productivity is strong enough, distribution in appropriate measure according to each individual's different needs and in keeping with his psychological and physiological health is clearly the more reasonable and fairer outcome.

### Where power-law distribution comes from

But in the present system of human civilization, once individuals and states gain power through free competition or through power games, the inequality of power produces an inequality in the distribution of gains from the cooperative and competitive game; add the repetition of the game over and over, and the gains of the advantaged side produce a compounding effect, finally forming a power-law distribution of power, wealth and resources. A power law does not grow out of human needs and abilities; it is the product of balance-breaking unequal behavior run through a long chain reaction: the inequality in any single round of the game is small; compounding stretches it into the long tail. The mathematics of this is clear: a process of proportional growth, accumulated over and over, yields a long-tailed distribution; the "rich get richer" mechanism is precisely the standard source of the power law.

This power-law distribution has its cause. For a group, wealth and resources serve two purposes: one part supports the survival of each individual in the group and satisfies the individuals' material and spiritual needs; the other part is consumed by the group's survival and operation as a whole. And how the group operates, how it distributes and consumes these resources, needs an intellectual center to take charge. The traditional system, whether authoritarian dictatorship or the alternation of parties in power, and likewise the power structure of a commercial company, is in essence a pyramid, a hierarchy with a power gradient. The elites standing at the apex, by virtue of their advantage in professional ability and the power and capital advantage accumulated through long games, gain the decision-making power over the operation of the whole group.

It must be admitted that the decision-maker who wins repeated competition is an individual of relatively greater ability and a higher probability of deciding correctly. The pyramid did not come from nowhere; under the conditions of limited human capacity for information processing, communication and coordination, it is a solution that works. The question is: is there a system of organization and decision-making with a higher probability of correct decisions? In the age of computers and AI, there is. It is the dynamic weights of the next chapter.

### Two kinds of resources, two kinds of distribution

So the principle of distribution in the new architecture is this: at the level of individual need, wealth and resources are distributed according to need, fairly, healthily and reasonably, and this part relates only to the individual's own physiological and psychological structure, not to his position in the game; at the collective level, wealth and resources are distributed and decided by the collective's dynamic-weight system.

On separating these two kinds of resources, China's land system is an example worth consulting. Land is the most fundamental resource for a civilization's infrastructure and economic development; airports, roads, high-speed rail, energy, factories, urban planning are the equivalent of an organism's circulatory system and metabolism. State ownership of land cuts down the wrangling, the haggling and the drawn-out attrition of expropriating private land. But it does only half the job: the distribution of the first kind of resource at the level of the individual is still the unequal distribution of the pyramid power structure; the power to control the second kind of resource is still the static, power-gapped, pyramid-style decision-making, still trapped in the stubborn chronic diseases of graft, corruption and bureaucracy.

### The precondition for machines to level the gaps in ability

Eliminating inequality used to be an impossible task, because everyone differs in age, intelligence and physical strength. But since the Industrial Revolution, whenever we invent a tool that replaces some human ability, whose performance exceeds the human one, and whose output is large enough for everyone to have one, the gap between people in that ability has been leveled. The mechanization of agriculture erased the gap in physical strength between farmers; navigation software erased the gap in the ability to find one's way. When AI surpasses humans in intelligence and becomes so widespread that everyone can access it freely, people's differences in intelligence will be leveled too.

But this has one precondition: all the knowledge needed for the principles and the manufacturing process of every tool that exceeds human capacity must be public knowledge shared by all humanity, not privately owned by any person, organization or state. Once it is privately owned, it is no longer a tool for erasing the gaps in ability; it becomes a tool by which some individual or organization gains a greater advantage in competition with other players. The possibility of such knowledge being monopolized must be ruled out at the root. That is the problem Chapter 9 is meant to solve.

### Equality does not require everyone to become the same person

People's abilities differ. That is a fact, and there is no need to dodge it. Innate differences even affect whether a person can keep investing effort in one direction, so that some people are narrow but extremely accomplished, while others know something of everything but lack depth in any one direction. So what matters is not making ability approach some particular distribution, but making the system's mechanism able to let individuals of these different abilities build the most effective cooperation, let specialists and generalists communicate effectively, and complete deep cross-disciplinary cooperation.

But a difference in ability and a difference in domination are two different things. The old system binds them together: a person is good at something, so he gets more resources; the resources buy more people's time and more opportunities to train; so his ability and his power over others compound together. The time an individual can spend on learning and practice is limited, while capital lets one person continuously mobilize the time of many. If training conditions, disposable time and access to tools are fairly guaranteed, these two kinds of accumulation are no longer bound together: a person's being good at something can help him do the task better, but it need not automatically give him more power to control other people's opportunities to grow.

The identity of every carbon atom does not limit the diversity of organic compounds; the identity of the DNA in every cell does not limit the diversity of cell functions. In the same way, people's equality in rights does not mean uniformity of function; on the contrary, it is exactly this equality that truly releases the functional diversity of every individual. The accumulation of individual ability thereby becomes a source of growth in common ability, without having to turn into power over others.

### Equality in the sense of the rules

The equality the new system guarantees is equality in the sense of the rules, not equality of outcomes. Video games offer a ready comparison. A well-designed multiplayer game, whether strategy, role-playing or adventure, guarantees by its rules, from the moment a player creates an account and logs in, that all players are equal in rules and in starting resources: the same starting point, the same rules, and every difference afterward comes from what the player did in the game. Pay-to-win online games are the opposite: paying players bring their own starting wealth, VIP players hold special privileges, the rules themselves are unequal, and so the rankings in the game reflect not play but the wallet outside the game. Players' attitudes toward these two kinds of game are exactly people's attitudes toward the two kinds of institution: in the first, you lose and accept it; in the second, you win and nobody respects it.

The game of society, today, is a pay-to-win online game. What the new system has to do is turn it into the first kind: when each person enters, the first kind of resource is distributed according to need, decision weight in every field starts from the same initial value, the rules are common knowledge, and every difference afterward can come only from his contributions and judgments in the game. This requirement rests on no assumption about the distribution of ability. It is only a rule, the kind of symmetry described in Chapter 2: swap any two players, and the rules do not change.

This example brings inheritance to mind. This chapter handles individual need and collective resources separately, and inheritance sits exactly astride the two. If the resources held in a person's name can be inherited, then VIP paying players born with a silver spoon reappear in the social game under the new system, and the fairness of starting resources is broken from the second generation on. So I lean toward a rule: under the new system, personal resources carry no right of inheritance; the first kind of resource held in a person's name returns after him to the collective pool, to be redistributed by the dynamic-weight system; what he leaves to his descendants is the whole of his record and contribution, honor, not starting wealth. This is only my own opinion about a future institution; like every other rule, it has to be tested in experiments.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: In the old system, a few used wealth to control the time of the many. What does the new system rely on to guarantee that ability does not turn into the same kind of control?**

A: On the separation of the two kinds of resources. However strong a person's ability, the first kind of resource is distributed to him only according to his own need; his influence over the second kind of resource shows up only as decision weight in the fields he is good at, and weight attaches to concrete problems and adjusts with every feedback from results; it cannot accumulate into a stock of capital that can be taken off and used to mobilize other people's time. Wealth can compound because it can circulate independently, detached from the person who created it and the problem it solved; weight cannot. For a power law to grow it needs a channel through which advantage is exchanged for command over other people's time, and that channel is exactly what the new system cuts off by rule. This is not a prediction about distributions; it is a rule that can be checked: the moment anyone can exchange weight for the first kind of resource, or for weight in another field, the rule has been broken.

## Chapter 6: The Social Neural Network of Dynamic Weights

### Starting from spam

The decision mechanism I envision was first inspired by spam-filtering algorithms. Whether an email is spam is not decided by any single rule; instead countless features each give a judgment, each judgment carries a weight, and the weight keeps adjusting according to that feature's past accuracy. The weight of a correct judgment rises; the weight of a wrong one falls. The whole system needs no "authority" at all, yet it gets more and more accurate.

Algorithms of this kind have a mature theory in machine learning. Treat each judge as an "expert" and aggregate them, weighted by each one's past performance, adjusting the weights after every outcome; the collective judgment obtained this way carries a provable guarantee: in the long run it will not be much worse than the one expert who, in hindsight, performed best, and there is no need to know in advance who the best one is.

Put this algorithm onto the decisions of human society: when a complex, uncertain problem arises at the social, political or economic level, instead of having it decided by the relevant experts under an authoritarian system or by elections and votes under a democratic system, the historical accuracy of each human and AI agent node in answering that class of problem is computed and different weights are assigned; then from the weighted results several different solutions are offered, each with its own weight; then experiments are run through virtual or real social pilots, and the feedback decides how to roll out; and the result of each round of feedback in turn revises the decision weight of every node.

Every intelligent individual, whether human or AI, has an equal initial weight in every field. That weight is adjusted dynamically, with positive and negative feedback, according to the accuracy of its predictions and the effectiveness of its proposals in that field, all of which the system can record and verify.

### The brain compared with society

Use the human brain as the metaphor for this new civilizational system: the brain corresponds to civilization, neurons correspond to humans plus AI, an individual's knowledge corresponds to the sum of human knowledge, and the brain's biological neural network corresponds to the neural network of human society.

In the brain, neurons are equal to one another in status, differing only in which network layer they sit in, with the weights of their connections adjusting dynamically at all times. The network formed by the individuals of human society is a static pyramid structure, in which the resources one holds, the power one has and the size of one's decision weight depend on one's position in the pyramid and are relatively fixed. When an exceptionally capable entrepreneur or politician declines in cognitive and decision-making ability with age, his decision weight is not thereby quickly replaced by a younger and more vigorous new generation.

In dictatorships and oligarchies, the weight of the leader is far greater than that of ordinary people, and precisely because his power is so great, discovering errors and correcting them through feedback is extremely difficult and extremely slow, usually requiring violent revolution to complete the redistribution of decision weight. In democracies, whether one person one vote or a system of representatives, weight is redistributed once every few years; the speed of correction is better than in dictatorship and oligarchy, but still seriously lagging, and a vote does not distinguish between problems, nor does it change with results.

### Unbinding decision nodes from specific people

There is a key point in this mechanism: the nodes of the decision network are no longer bound to specific elite human individuals or groups, but consist of algorithms and rules in which collective intelligence converges. The Industrial Revolution turned physical labor from the personal craft of the workshop into a function that machines could reproduce at high quality; in the same way, before the AI revolution, social governance was carried out by the pyramid formed by political leaders, business magnates and academic authorities, and the AI revolution will inevitably bring a revolution in the organizational form of intellectual labor. Algorithms, rules and strategies, these intellectual products, are no longer bound to some specific authority but become functions that can be copied at scale. Just as after the Industrial Revolution capitalism was, relative to feudalism, a relation of production better suited to automated physical labor, the social neural network of dynamic weights is a relation of production better suited than capitalism to the new productive force of automated intellectual labor.

What is more, this dynamic-weight approach does not even require people to take part by hand at every moment. If a person's digital space is recorded in full, if his skills, cognition and preferences are recorded with privacy and security guaranteed, with their owner marked and tamper-proof, then when the group needs a decision, in the great majority of cases he does not need to take part personally; his AI agent can provide his opinion and views from these records. Simple, routine, trivial judgments are completed automatically by the AI agent; on major matters the AI invites the person himself to take part, and an opinion is given after joint discussion; the whole process is recorded throughout, to be used in future to adjust weights through feedback. Each person's AI agent is the rational intelligence that represents that person's rights, interests and opinions.

### Virtual tit-for-tat

The strict and lenient versions of tit-for-tat described in Chapter 3 can be replaced in this network by a virtual version: using digital points as the means of reward and punishment, turning real damage in the real world into virtual damage in a game. Throughout human history, the adjustment of power and decision weight has usually been accompanied by violent punishment and wastefully excessive reward. Virtual tit-for-tat reaches a new stable structure at the lowest energy and the fastest speed; before taking real action, the various possibilities can be explored in virtual space to find the path with the highest energy-efficiency ratio and the least harm. This network is at the same time a vast social laboratory.

### Serial and parallel: beyond democracy and centralism

The running of any system is a mixture of two broad classes of logic: one must be completed in causal and sequential order, the other can run in parallel. To build a building you must lay the foundation before raising the main structure, but within each stage many tasks can run in parallel. In designing and assigning tasks, whether to lean more toward the causal order of overall coordination, or more toward each part running independently in parallel and then integrating through the rules of the market or of the game, is the most important meta-strategy.

The multi-party democratic federal system attends more to the autonomy of each part, parallel development and diversity of direction; the centralized system of one-party rule leans more toward the consistency of overall coordination and the long-range planning of complex tasks. What is strong in democratic capacity is weak in centralizing capacity; what is strong in centralizing capacity is weak in democratic capacity. The history of artificial intelligence has a similar story: architectures good at sequential dependency and architectures good at parallel processing contended for years, and were finally unified by the attention mechanism, which lets every position attend directly to every other position, preserving long-range dependency while running fully in parallel; multi-head attention further lets the same problem receive attention from different angles at the same time. Complex problems and large projects at the level of society need exactly such a mechanism, one that combines the two.

The contradiction between commitment and error correction lies here too. A centralized system has the commitment capacity for long-term planning, but the commitment capacity is so strong that one mistake costs too much. Its speed of error correction is inversely proportional to the political investment of the top: in fields where authority has not been staked, both experiment and correction are fast; in fields where authority has been staked, correction is slow, and when it comes it comes as a sudden break. That is the mark of a pyramid with face to save: policy is bound to specific people. Under a dynamic-weight decision system there is no such binding.

### The central nervous center is not a single point

A stable intelligent agent must be stable over time and logically consistent in its outward behavior. A distributed nervous system like the jellyfish's can only manage simple interactions; the more complex the intelligent organism, the more complex its central nervous system. A civilization that lacks the unified, coordinated, logical and planned capacity for information collection and decision-making, both outward and inward, with policies reversed overnight, will keep circling on the road of evolution.

But a central nervous center does not mean the kind of structure in which one node decides everything and one failure paralyzes the whole body; it means a system in which many nodes integrate dynamically, obtain accurate external information, and produce unified, stable decisions and behavior. The brain is highly modular, layered, with hubs, but it has no irreplaceable apex, influence shifts with the task and the evidence, and the failure of any unit can be compensated. Nor does unity and stability toward the outside mean uniformity inside: the unity of a person's outward behavior does nothing to diminish the diversity and complexity within the body.

### Will weight turn back into privilege

Some worry that dynamic weights will be captured by historical advantage and by numbers of proxies, and recentralize. These two worries correspond to two explicit rules.

Historical advantage: weight attaches to concrete problems, adjusts with every feedback from results, and expires. When a problem disappears, its weights go to zero; when a new problem appears, all nodes start over from an equal initial weight. A person's past record of being right gets him heard more on problems of the same kind, but it cannot, like wealth, detach itself from the problem, circulate on its own and roll up into compound interest.

Numbers of proxies: each person has exactly one root agent, whose identity is bound to that person's data sovereignty. A person who manufactures ten thousand proxies gets not ten thousand shares of weight but ten thousand copies of an opinion from one source, visible at a glance in the record. What really has to be guarded against is malicious sabotage and tampering: forged identities, proxy farming, altered records. Here we can learn the method of the cryptocurrency ledgers: write the security condition as a quantity that can be computed, not as trust in certain people. The concrete conditions have to be written out separately for this network's own protocol and for each of its own classes of attack.

Two things must also be kept apart: equality of basic rights and equality of influence. The equality the new system wants is equality of basic rights: equality of data sovereignty, of distribution according to need, and of the right to exit. Influence, by contrast, is dynamic, contestable, replaceable. However high a person's weight in one field, he cannot exchange it for weight in another field, cannot exchange it for the first kind of resource, and still less can he exchange it for control over other people's basic rights. How weight is produced, when it expires, how it is corrected, who can modify the algorithm, all of these are themselves common knowledge, all have a version record, all are decided by the same system of dynamic weights. The power to make the rules is thus locked in the cage.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: This mechanism requires full information and rational participants, and those conditions do not exist in reality.**

A: They are descriptions of an ideal state, not preconditions for engineering. The goal most likely can never be fully reached, but it can be approached by step-by-step iteration. As long as the new mechanism is even slightly stronger than the old one, that is enough for the replacement to begin, just as an object always moves toward lower potential energy. As for rationality: once AI is universal, everyone has his own AI agent to supplement his rational knowledge and decision-making ability, the great majority of intellectual activity is carried out through AI agents, and with that buffer in place, emotional behavior will also fall sharply.

**Q: The commitment problem comes back in impersonal form. A node whose weight rose because it advocated a policy also has something to protect; a policy that has run for years has downstream dependencies, and reversal is expensive all the same.**

A: So the real variable is not who the decision is bound to, but which arrives first for the party holding decision power, the cost of persisting or the cost of reversing. In a pyramid the cost of reversing falls on the leader and the cost of persisting falls on the population, and so it persists. Rules four and five of Chapter 4 were written for this very problem: every plan that accepts a temporary loss for long-term benefit must, on adoption, carry an estimated outcome and a monitoring process, and stop on failure; every loss is compensated at once and the strategy revised at once. Weight attaches to the problem and adjusts with results, so persisting in a plan already proven wrong costs the persister his own weight directly. The switching cost of infrastructure is real, but it is an engineering cost: it can be measured and shared, unlike face, which can only be settled by one side admitting defeat.

**Q: Where does legitimacy come from? However imperfect one person one vote is, people accept it. With weighting by ability, why would the losing side accept the result?**

A: What the losing side accepts is not some person's judgment but a rule in which it is itself included, in which its own judgment is itself counted, and which can be checked afterward. Under one person one vote, the losing side accepts the result because it knows its vote will still count next time; under dynamic weights, it accepts because it knows this result will be recorded and verified, and that if the outcome proves it right, its weight will rise, its loss will be compensated, and the plan will be revised. What the former promises is "try again next time"; what the latter promises is "if it's wrong, it gets fixed."

## Chapter 7: Artificial Intelligence: The Machine of Intellectual Labor, the Public Infrastructure of Civilization

### AI is the machine of intellectual labor

When a person does physical labor, what he can do is bounded by his physiological limits: the limits of strength, precision and speed are the limits of the tasks you can complete. With machines, the physical labor humans could not do became doable. In the same way, human intelligence is limited; one person cannot master every field, yet the solution of many complex problems and the construction of higher-order theories require breaking that biological limit. AI is the machine of intellectual labor.

True artificial general intelligence is not one large model on several million computers in a data center. That picture is a brain in a vat, with no senses, hands or feet, receiving only second-hand information processed by countless people, each through his own cognition, and yet presuming to decide for all of humanity. True general intelligence is the dynamic-weight network of the previous chapter: countless human individuals and organizations, countless cloud and local AI systems, countless robots, devices and sensors, together collecting, processing, analyzing and creating data, constructing knowledge systems and decision systems. Intelligence lives in the running of the whole system; any one model or person is only a component of it. The next chapter, once it has described the Large Civilization Model, will complete the chain of reasoning behind this judgment.

Mathematicians worrying that AI will replace them are like laborers at the start of the Industrial Revolution worrying that machines would replace them: they are using the experience of the old mechanism to reason about the new one. It is like a peasant for whom the most he can imagine of an emperor's life is tilling the field with a golden hoe.

### Who holds control over the control of AI

What most needs solving now is whose hands hold control over the control of AI. Open source or closed, the entity behind it is a traditional company, and its power structure is a pyramid. With AI in the hands of such institutions, everyone ends up fearing AI. People worry that AI will take their jobs because control of the AI is not in their own hands; they have only the right to use it. The root cause of the present fear and anxiety about AI lies in the fact that under the old system AI, as a resource, is governed by the existing system of distributing power and resources: ordinary people worry that the powerful will hold stronger AI and thereby sit in an unshakable position from which they can squeeze them harder; the United States and China each worry that the other will lead in AI and make it the enslaved side.

The first thing the architecture I envision does is solve this problem: build a personal digital space for every person, build a genuinely open-source AI model on that foundation, and place control over the model in the hands of a dynamic-weight organization in which all the user nodes taking part in building it have equal power and play a fair game. This AI belongs to each user himself, each person holds one share of its control, and there is no need to worry that other nodes will exploit an unfair power gap to manipulate the AI into doing harm to you.

As for the anxieties about AI making mistakes, cognitive decline and changed social relations, the main reason is that the technology is still in rapid development and deployment. People trust civil aircraft not because every passenger understands the turbine engine, but because behind it stands a complete system of theoretical research, engineering design, manufacturing standards, redundant protection, airworthiness certification, accident investigation and feedback from experience. As long as there is such a system of research, verification and experiment, AI too, like every achievement that has appeared in human civilization, will become a tool that people take for granted, trust and rely on.

### Big Brother is an extrapolation of the pyramid

Imagine an artificial general superintelligence that aims at maximizing the interests of the whole of human civilization. To achieve that goal it must obtain the information of every player in the system. In traditional science-fiction imagination, this scene is pictured as a future version of 1984: the superintelligence is that Big Brother high above, controlling everything, or else Big Brother is some clique of the powerful who control it, and all of humanity sinks into puppets, bugged and watched. So people, out of fear, refuse to provide information, while the other side, for the sake of the global optimum, must obtain it; an irreconcilable contradiction arises between the two sides, and it leads in the end to endings like those of The Terminator, The Matrix and 1984.

But these imaginings are all results obtained by extending the pyramid power system of human history. The pyramid structure is only a subset of the set of all possible civilizational systems, and all the strategies and endings derived from it are only a very small subset of all the possibilities, and the best civilizational architecture lies precisely outside that subset. The previous chapter has already set out another architecture: there is no apex, and so no place for Big Brother.

Moreover, a true superintelligence will choose persuasion, not coercion: construct a theory and persuade the human public by peaceful communication, with rational logical reasoning, reliable experimental evidence, and local, voluntary, small-scale pilots. Because in today's world of flourishing science and free-flowing information, coercive methods would plainly bring disaster on civilization and are not the cheapest option. If even an ordinary person like me can see this, an artificial general superintelligence could not make the wrong choice, unless it were a superintelligent general artificial idiot. The same follows from the principle of maximization in Chapter 1: the interaction between humans and AI is the richest and most complex new relation so far, and destroying humanity does not fit maximization. Mutual destruction is a behavior that arises only when the individuals within a system lack the capacity to create sufficient resources for survival.

### Intelligence should follow in knowledge's footsteps and become public infrastructure

Knowledge and intelligence convert into each other: intelligence is the mechanism and process that produces new knowledge, and existing knowledge is the raw material that trains intelligence.

In early civilization, knowledge and the ability to acquire it were usually bundled with some specific person's material interests in economic life. With the development of writing and printing, the cost of spreading knowledge fell, and theoretical knowledge gradually went from the monopoly of a few to relatively free sharing. Jefferson said that he who receives an idea from me receives instruction himself without lessening mine, just as he lights his candle at mine. On entering the age of computers and the internet, with the cost of copying data and code almost zero, a more thorough open-source movement for knowledge began.

If the pre-AI information age brought the cost of copying knowledge down to almost zero, then after entering the AI age, even the intelligence that produces knowledge is itself falling rapidly in cost and spreading rapidly in reach. So AI itself, as intelligence, should follow in knowledge's footsteps and become public knowledge infrastructure, supplying nourishment for people's intellectual activity just as sunlight and air supply nourishment for their physical activity.

In essence, AI is a human intellectual integration system that takes the knowledge produced by the intellectual activity of all humanity and, through algorithms, organizes and distills it at scale and automatically, integrating it into one enormous high-dimensional space. It is just that at present this is being done inefficiently by a few commercial companies, under conditions of mutual distrust, competition and confrontation. Across the whole process from collecting training data, to learning and training, to actual use, producing new data, and continued iteration after feedback, the rights to create, use, interpret and record, the power to decide, and the power to allocate credit and benefit should all be common knowledge, and the mechanism must ensure they are not dominated by any local individual or group within the civilizational body. For that we need a protocol mechanism like that of open-source software, except that this time the object of the mechanism is not static data and code but the whole civilizational intelligent agent: all its nodes, and the system's operating mechanism and operating trajectory, all become common knowledge.

### How contributions are recorded

Under the old system, because the mechanism for distributing benefit and credit has structural defects, each party builds information silos for the sake of local interest and falls into the local optimum of a prisoner's dilemma. A mathematician with a small pioneering idea, an entrepreneur with a new concept, will, for the sake of personal reputation and interest, do their best to keep it secret, or simply do everything themselves; even though there are more efficient people who could complete every other part for them, they inefficiently reinvent the wheel. A supervisor makes a discovery from a student's inspiration, and the student who had the original inspiration usually does not get the credit he deserves; it is the same in business and in politics.

In the old era there were no means to record everyone's intellectual activity completely, fairly and precisely, and the allocation of credit could only depend on the cultivation and willingness of the people involved. The quarrel between Newton and Leibniz and the gentlemen's friendship between Darwin and Wallace were both results of personal character, not results of an institution. In software, the version-tracking system can compute each person's code and commits exactly; traditional copyright law marks a ranking only through attribution and the order of names, while a version system can give the exact proportions of all contributors. Extend this method to the whole of human intellectual activity, treat the recording system of Part III as the version-control system for the evolution of the entire civilization, and it can record each person's contribution to civilization. Under conditions of developed productivity, with individual material needs already met, this contribution value is more a matter of honor, but such an allocation is plainly fairer.

Once this mechanism catches on, it produces an accelerating effect: as more and more people are willing to share new ideas the moment they have them, the option of not publishing first and developing the idea slowly on one's own carries an ever greater risk that someone else will publish it first. And because dynamic weights can lower the probability that wrong ideas get executed, people neither fear that the credit for a correct idea will be snatched away, nor hold back from proposing a wrong idea out of fear that it will cause harm. Any person's spark of inspiration can quickly reach this network, where the best-suited nodes are then combined to complete the growth from the seed of inspiration to the tree of theory.

## Chapter 8: The Large Civilization Model: A Civilization in Which Training and Inference Are One

### Resources are limited, so attention is necessary

How does the social neural network of Chapter 6 run? We can borrow a concrete shape from the most successful architecture of intelligence today.

The attention mechanism of the Transformer resembles the human brain in three ways. First, resources are limited, and weights are used to focus selectively on the factors that matter more: reading a book in a noisy cafe, the brain automatically filters out the background noise; an AI model processing a long text likewise does not treat every word alike, but concentrates its weights on the content most relevant to the current task. Second, out of a vast, complex and changing body of information, it builds accurate contextual associations through dynamic weights: on reading "that apple is really sweet," a human and an AI both connect it with fruit, not with a phone. Third, it links an enormous number of information objects by matching queries against content.

The architecture of human civilization built on decision-making by dynamic weights can be given the same structure. On one side, the root agent belonging to each person maps out a personal space covering that individual and his private resources; the person and his own agents work together to construct an attention mechanism at the personal level, and carry out self-management and self-evolution. On the other side, these personal spaces together make up the whole civilizational space, and at that level the attention mechanism of civilization is constructed. In human civilization, we cannot have every person and every model think equally deeply about every problem at the same time; what the attention mechanism has to organize is the meeting of the relevant people, knowledge and tools under limited time, computing power and communication cost.

### Objects change, so we must enter the runtime

But we cannot copy the algorithms of AI models the way the man in the old story carved a notch in his boat to mark where his sword fell overboard. The objects a model handles are static: the semantic nodes in the vocabulary do not increase, decrease or change. The real world is not like that. The people and external objects of human society change as decisions and behavior change; they can diminish, multiply, change their properties, even be destroyed. Non-renewable resources shrink by exactly what is consumed; the rewards and punishments a person receives change his future decision tendencies. The former is like a collection of programs; the latter is a more complex system that contains the entire runtime. So besides the algorithms of probability and statistics, we need the principles of game theory; and whichever model is used, computing its variables requires sufficiently abundant and accurate data on the objects in the system. This brings us back to the bottleneck of Chapter 4: the active cooperation of all participants.

The minimal process of a common affair has five steps: the relevant objects are discovered from the problem; the relevant capability nodes each propose candidate plans, carrying their weights; a plan executes once it has the authorization of the parties it touches; the result of execution is recorded; and the result feeds back into the weight of every node that took part. Discovery, proposal, authorization, execution, feedback: this is the concrete shape of the dynamic-weight mechanism inside the runtime.

Weights must attach to problems, not to people, and they must expire. Before a technology appears, no node has any record of judgment about it; after a kind of resource is exhausted, all the weights built around it lose their object. A game has an ending; a runtime has none. It has only persistent state and a constant arrival of new things.

### The answers lie in the future: training and inference cannot be separated

Set this civilizational brain, with humans and AI agents as its neuron nodes and dynamic decision weights as its algorithm, beside an AI large language model, and you find that the two processes of learning, training and inference in practice are fractals of one structure at different levels: the civilizational body is the same kind of thing as a large language model, at a higher order and a higher level. The difference is that at the level of the civilizational body the input is not text but every problem, large and small, that the whole civilization meets and needs to solve.

These problems are of two kinds. For one kind, the knowledge system of human civilization already has the correct answer, and it is enough to call directly on the intelligence already integrated in the large language models and solve it by inference. The other kind matters more: the problems human civilization has met and is meeting in its development that still have no correct or ideal answer. For this kind, training and inference cannot be separated the way they are for a large language model, with pre-training done off the job. In conventional training the answers to all the training material are already known; the model's inference can be compared with the answer again and again, the error back-propagated, and the model corrected over and over until training is complete.

The training material of a civilizational body is not like that. The correct answers to all its problems lie in the future. The result of one inference is not the final answer but one branch of a trial strategy on the way to the final answer, one state node on one branch of the whole search tree of evolutionary paths. In machine learning, the theories that deal with this kind of problem are called online learning and reinforcement learning; the best precedent is the AI that plays board games: it has no standard answers, it generates its data by playing against itself, and its training and search are interwoven.

In this higher-level social intelligent neural system the nodes are not the semantic nodes of a large language model either; they are every human and every AI agent. Each person has a dedicated AI, used to build a personal knowledge base and to produce a personal cognitive model: it takes the civilizational body's consensus knowledge as its base semantic model, adds this person's own semantic and cognitive preferences, and represents the structure of this person's cognitive space. These dedicated personal models act as individual agents, either with the person taking part actively or by automatic proxy in a default mode; they join the game, give their opinions, and become neurons in the brain of the whole civilization. The civilizational system assigns different weights to the decisions of these different nodes, then gathers the decisions of all the neurons to make collective inferences and decisions.

The reward function used for back-propagation therefore cannot be set by "the distance from the final answer". It is set by reality: how the results these decisions produce when practiced in the real world compare with the earlier plan, how far they fall from what people foresaw and expected, how large the gap is between the results of different decision plans, how satisfied all the people taking part are, and so on.

So this is not an off-the-job pre-training process. It is a loop in which learning, training and inference iterate again and again against the feedback of productive practice. Civilization lives inside this loop, practicing, constructing new knowledge and evolving all at once. Back-propagation in this process updates three things: the decision weights of the nodes across the whole social intelligent neural network; the civilizational body's consensus knowledge model, updated with the new knowledge; and the weights of the semantic nodes in every person's dedicated AI model, and, once brain-computer interface technology matures, in every human brain itself.

### The Large Civilization Model: every person has a vector in civilization space

By this point you will have noticed that this civilizational system is itself a model, a Large Civilization Model. In the high-dimensional space of civilization, each person's dedicated cognitive-semantic model is an object like a token in a large language model, and the relations among these objects are like the relations among meanings in a large language model. Every meaning has an embedding in a language model; every person has an embedding in civilization. This is exactly the cognitive reference frame of Chapter 2 given coordinates for the first time. So we can construct a large model of a higher order than the language model, at the level of civilization.

When this civilization-level social intelligent neural network runs, it has two outputs. First, it can train a better large language model that belongs to all of humanity. Second, and more important, in the very act of running it collects all the training material the Large Civilization Model needs: every problem, every node's judgment, every collective decision, every real-world result is a training record. As the new civilizational order continues to iterate and evolve, the Large Civilization Model is constructed within it, and it in turn helps us construct more complex, more advanced civilizational structures and institutions.

### Data and labels: every person's judgment is a label

In training a large language model, besides the collection of data, the labeling of data is just as crucial; it is even one of the factors that decide the level of knowledge and ability of the model that comes out. The same holds for the Large Civilization Model. Everything that each person in the social intelligent neural network produces over the course of his life, all the audio, video and text, together with the personal cognitive model that his dedicated AI generates on that basis, is data the Large Civilization Model needs for training. Then where do the labels come from? From these same people: each person's assessments and evaluations of this data, his own and other people's, and his decisions and answers to the problems he meets, are the labels on this data. The collection of all this labeled data from the whole of humanity is the best training data for civilization's common knowledge model and for the Large Civilization Model.

So the social intelligent neural network under the new civilizational order is two things at once: it is the best system for mining and collecting this data and these labels, and it is the best system for learning, training and inference in practice. How this data and these labels are collected without harming the individual is the business of Chapter 10.

### From computing power to social mechanics

Humanity in fact already possesses the most powerful tools for studying complex emergent phenomena. Chapter 1 said that "more is different": basic objects of a lower order, through combination, give rise to complex structures of a higher order. How do we model and study such phenomena, then propose a theory, and finally put it into practice and create new emergence? The growth of computing power in recent decades has given humanity a way: large numbers of high-performance computers doing computation on enormous high-dimensional tensors, carrying the whole process from mathematical modeling and analysis through theory-building to practice. Quantum mechanics, fluid mechanics, condensed matter physics, the construction of complex engineering works, and the development of AI itself have all benefited from this.

So far, the training of large AI models is the most computationally demanding task humanity has ever accomplished. In the Large Civilization Model we face next, every unit is itself a high-dimensional semantic model, and the complexity will certainly be several orders of magnitude higher. But I believe that humanity, with the help of AI, will be able to accomplish it. In the course of this social neural network's evolution, we will gradually work out the principles and models of the "mechanics" by which human nodes of different cognition, in the high-dimensional space of civilization, act on one another in real society, and then use these principles of "social mechanics" to construct more advanced and more complex social structures in human civilization, just as people use the principles of mechanics to build ever more complex high-rise buildings. Newton's principles gave humanity the mechanics to raise great buildings; the "mathematical principles" in this book's title are meant to arrive, in the end, at this social mechanics.

This is what the "bitter lesson" of the preface means at the level of civilization. Solving the problems of constructing and organizing society by the personal experience and learning of authorities amounts to hand-writing expert knowledge into the system; relying on mathematical modeling, enormous records and computation, and training in the feedback of practice, is the approach that wins in the long run.

### Freedom, equality and democracy: from slogans to computable models

Chapter 1 said that even the demands made at the level of politics can be interpreted by the principle of minimization and maximization: freedom corresponds to the maximization of the individual's relations with other objects, democracy to the maximization of the emergent higher-order group organization, and equality is the precondition that allows individual interest and group interest to be maximized at the same time. With the Large Civilization Model, that sentence can be taken one step further.

Under the old system, freedom, democracy and equality are philosophical concepts and political slogans. Every school has its own interpretation and none can convince another, because these things cannot be measured, and so cannot be compared. The social intelligent neural network under the new civilizational order makes it possible, for the first time, to turn these concepts into quantifiable mathematical models and computable strategy algorithms.

First, what each of them is. The three concepts are three different types of mathematical object: freedom is a quantity, equality is a symmetry, democracy is an aggregation operator.

Freedom has two layers. The first is sovereignty: within the kind of resources that serves individual need, the objective function that each person and his dedicated cognitive model maximize in all their decisions is defined by that person himself, and everyone else's weight here is zero. The second is the size of the reachable future. The objective function says what a person wants; freedom says how much of the future lies within his reach. A person who has been steered into choosing "voluntarily" can score high on his objective function and still not be free. For this layer the mathematics already exists. Research on agents has a quantity called empowerment: the channel capacity between an agent's actions and the states it will later find itself in, a measure of how many distinguishable futures it can bring about. Sen's "capability" in economics says the same thing: freedom is the set of lives a person is actually able to achieve. This quantity can be computed and can be compared between one person and another, and it is the measure of that sentence in Chapter 2: at every moment of evolution the individual must retain the actual ability to change the direction of its trajectory. It also agrees with the optimal marginal utility of Chapter 5: resources beyond that interval add nothing to the reachable future. So the full statement is this: the individual maximizes his own objective function within his personal domain; the civilizational body takes each person's reachable future as one term in its own objective function and maximizes it without reducing anyone else's; and the job of the dedicated AI is to widen each person's feasible region.

Equality is the symmetry of the rules. Chapter 2 already gave the formulation: swap any two people within a rule, and the content of the rule stays unchanged. Social choice theory calls this property anonymity. In the Large Civilization Model it has a very clean counterpart: a Transformer applies the same set of parameters to every token, and tokens differ only in their embeddings. Equality is parameter sharing; diversity is the difference between embeddings. Division of labor and roles are the result of symmetry breaking as the system runs, not something written into the rules in advance.

Democracy is the aggregation operator that, in affairs concerning the collective kind of resources, takes individual judgments to a collective decision. Its most classical mathematical ground is Condorcet's jury theorem: when many judgments that are independent of one another, each slightly better than a random guess, are aggregated by majority, the probability of being right approaches one as the number of people grows. The theorem has a weighted form: when people differ in accuracy, the optimal aggregation is a weighted majority in which each person's weight is proportional to the log-odds of his accuracy. In other words, the dynamic weights of Chapter 6 are not a departure from democracy but the optimal form of Condorcet's principle; one person, one vote is the special case in which everyone's accuracy is the same or cannot be told apart. Democracy here is three conditions: every person's judgment has a path into the result; the result responds positively to its inputs; and no single node can determine the result alone. The theorem also requires that people's judgments be independent of one another, which is exactly why this book insists on diversity again and again.

Taken together, the three form a constrained optimization problem: under the constraint that the rules are symmetric, maximize each person's reachable future, with decisions in the collective domain given by aggregation weighted by track record. Chapter 1 said that equality is the precondition for maximizing individual interest and group interest at the same time; here that is literally true: symmetry is the constraint that makes the two levels of optimization compatible. Nor is the qualifier "personal resources" dispensable. Sen proved an impossibility theorem: if each person's preferences about other people's private affairs are also counted in collective decisions, then reserving for just two people the right to decide one private matter each already conflicts with the weakest collective standard there is, that an option everyone prefers should be adopted. The division into two kinds of resources in Chapter 5 is precisely what takes the personal domain out of the aggregation.

### The initial values need not be correct; the correcting signal must be there

Whether the models and algorithms above are correct does not, in fact, matter. What matters is that the concepts and slogans that used to rest on philosophical thought can now become quantifiable mathematical models and computable strategy algorithms. In the cooperative social game of human civilization, in the course of the Large Civilization Model's iterative upgrading, we can let these models and algorithms come closer and closer to the correct form, and let the results of the game come closer and closer to the global optimum. Conversely, to pick one quantity for freedom and one set of weights for democracy and then announce that this is the answer is exactly what the "bitter lesson" describes: hand-writing expert knowledge into the system. What is written above is only a set of initial values.

There are precedents for this path. The thermometer came more than a hundred years before thermodynamics. The earliest scales were set arbitrarily, at a time when people had not yet worked out what heat even is; but once "hot and cold" could be measured, different definitions could be compared with and corrected against one another, and only at the end did absolute temperature emerge. Measurable first, correct later. Machine learning has a guarantee of the same form: the algorithm of Chapter 6 that weights by past performance does not need to know in advance who is right; it guarantees only that in the long run it will not be much worse than the one who, in hindsight, performed best.

Whether the initial form is right does not matter, but for the iteration to converge the correcting signal must be there from the first day. No one can supply a standard answer for "freedom", so whether one definition is "more correct" can only be judged in the way of this chapter: write the candidate definitions into the rules and run them as forks in different free zones; use the real-world results and the evaluations of all participants as labels, and compare the forks; what remains is the one that is still endorsed after everyone knows about it. This is the kind of law Chapter 2 spoke of: laws that still hold after everyone knows them.

So each of these three concepts has two layers. The fine layer, how the quantity is computed and how the weights are set, is left to the iteration. The minimal layer cannot wait to be learned by the iteration, because it is the precondition for the iteration itself to be trustworthy. This layer is three meta-rules:

- Equality: every person has the same standing under the rules in this process.
- Freedom: every person can exit and fork.
- Democracy: every person's judgment enters as a label.

They are the same kind of thing as the five rules of Chapter 4: they do not prescribe the outcome; they prescribe only how this game is played and how it corrects itself. The forks on a unified base and the free entry and free exit described in Chapter 13 are what these three look like at the starting stage.

Finally, "approaching the global optimum" should be understood as continuously tracking a moving optimum. As this chapter said earlier, a runtime has no ending; the objects change, and the optimum moves with them. What civilization needs is not to compute the end point once, but to keep up at all times.

### Artificial general intelligence is the whole civilizational body, not any one model

Chapter 7 said that true artificial general intelligence is not a large model in a data center but the whole network of dynamic weights. The chain of reasoning behind that judgment can now be completed.

Artificial general intelligence must have the ability to solve every problem that human civilization meets, including the self-evolution of civilization's own institutions. What that ability depends on is the civilizational body's own Large Civilization Model and humanity's common knowledge system. Their whole process, from data collection and labeling through learning and training to inference in practice and feedback, plainly requires the joint participation, in brainpower and computing power, of all human and AI agents and of every digital device involved in collecting and processing the data. So any supercomputer cluster in any super data center is no more than one local computing node in the whole civilizational body.

It is the same as when we call a human being a higher intelligent agent: we mean the whole living organism, not some super neuron or some super lobe in its brain.

### Start testing from the smallest cooperative scenario

This runtime does not need to be tested starting from a nation. The smallest cooperative scenario is two people, one task, and one verifiable record. Start there and observe: can the relevant people find each other, can misunderstandings be detected in time, can expert judgment be tested, can the results of cooperation be developed further by others. Then three people, one project, one organization. Chapter 13 describes exactly this road.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The brain's dynamic weights work because there is a massive, fast stream of prediction-error signals. Most social decisions lack exactly this fast feedback. Without feedback on outcomes, dynamic weights only add noise.**

A: This is exactly why this book puts the record before the decision. The recording and permission system of Chapter 10, together with the rule from Chapter 4 that failure means compensation, correction and a record, exists precisely to manufacture prediction-error signals for social decisions. Under the old system, social decisions lack fast feedback not because the feedback does not physically exist, but because the pyramid mutes it: bad news that travels upward hurts the messenger, so it does not travel; errors the decision-maker knows about but cannot admit must be actively covered up. A brain that can mute the pain signals it dislikes will walk on a broken leg. When the record does not depend on any single node, feedback can no longer be muted. Use it first in the fields where feedback is fast enough; record first in the fields where feedback is slow. That is a sequence, not an obstacle.

# Part III: Foundation

## Chapter 9: Content as Identity: Hash, Merkle-DAG, and Verifiable Common Knowledge

### Common knowledge needs a carrier that cannot be privatized

The first two Parts left two premises that must be put into practice. Rule three of Chapter 4 requires that all shared information, as common knowledge, be recorded and shared in a traceable, tamper-proof way. Chapter 5 requires that all the knowledge of the tools that augment human capability be held in common, and that the possibility of its being monopolized be ruled out at the root. Neither of these can rest on a paper agreement alone. This chapter is about the technical foundation that can carry them. It uses no new principle of computation; it uses what today's software engineering has already verified again and again.

### A hash is both a name and an address

The hash value obtained by running a hash algorithm over a piece of content is unique with a high enough probability to serve as that content's identifier. Change a single bit of the content and the hash value is completely different, so it can detect any tampering. Its uniqueness and uniform distribution allow fast indexing and lookup through a hash table, so it can also serve as that content's address. Write the hash values of the child objects into the content of the parent object, then hash the parent object, and so on upward, layer by layer, and you get a Merkle tree; allow several parent objects to share the same child object, and you get a Merkle-DAG: a single hash value at the root locks all the content and all the history beneath it. It is what git uses for version management, and what distributed storage and blockchain ledgers use for content addressing and tamper-proofing.

What the hash guarantees is that the content has not changed. Proving who published it still needs a digital signature: what the signature signs is precisely the hash.

So far, this technology has been used only in local scenarios of application software. My proposal is to take it as a single mechanism and run it through the whole system: design a programming language that uses hash values as the identifiers of all code and data objects in the language. A simple constant is represented by the hash value of its content; a complex object is represented by a key-value structure in which both keys and values are hash values; for a function, the hash is taken over the content of its definition. These identifiers are at the same time addresses, allowing decentralized deployment, storage and execution on a distributed network, with version management built in.

This language has three direct consequences.

First, uniqueness. Any concept, any piece of code, any datum, once its content is fixed, has a globally unique identifier, so a vast number of objects can be named without negotiation and in a distributed manner, with no collisions.

Second, dependency becomes table lookup. In traditional programming languages, cross-file and cross-software dependencies, and the complicated operations of compiling and linking, are all reduced to hash-based key-value lookups. After a low-level object is upgraded, every higher-level object that uses it can be traced automatically along the dependencies.

Third, openness becomes a requirement for running. This has to be stated precisely: the hash function itself forces no one to publish content; publishing a digest is not publishing the original. The force comes from the ecosystem's rules of admission: any code or data of a public nature that wants to enter the public dependency graph, to be referenced and run by others, must make its content obtainable and verifiable, or else nothing that references it can pass verification, and nothing that references it can run. So "closed but public" becomes, in this ecosystem, a thing that cannot run.

The third point is precisely the solution to the premise set out in Chapter 5. Existing open-source licenses only forbid closing the source in the legal sense; they cannot do it in substance: when a powerful organization violates the license, secretly develops a closed-source product, and gains an enormous advantage in competition, others cannot defeat that product by law. A language in which the hash is the name, together with this rule of admission, turns open source from a legal requirement into a running requirement: what does not publish its content cannot be referenced by others, and so cannot enter the public ecosystem. Personal private data is not included; the next chapter gives its rules.

### Everything is a tree

All code and data, as semantic objects, are tree-structured in essence; it is only that in the traditional system every level organizes this tree by its own rules: inside source code, by brackets and indentation; in data files, by their respective encoding formats; on disk, by directories; on a local network, by IP addresses; on the internet, by domain names and assorted protocols; and then each application has its own interfaces. These are all different representations of the same semantic tree, and converting between them consumes an enormous amount of the human labor in software engineering.

With Merkle-DAG, from object access inside a file to access across applications, across hosts and across the internet, the same kind of content identifier can be the name and the address, with no worry about tampering, and with packing and splitting done automatically according to the scenario in which the object is used. This idea has so far been applied only in local scenarios; what I intend to do is use it to reconstruct the whole system in a unified way.

What unification brings is not sameness. Different implementations of the same function each have a unique identifier, hang on the same tree as replaceable options, and can be evaluated against the same benchmarks, with the better implementation directly replacing the worse. This holds for software, and every product in economic life, from a single screw to a chip design, can be organized the same way. Just as in the DNA system of living things, the unification of rules and architecture does not hinder diversity; on the contrary, it unleashes diversity.

### Names are for people, identity is for machines

The computer ecosystem is full of naming diversity born of differing human preference: different instruction sets, different language syntaxes, different data formats, different keyboard shortcuts. For a long time, the developers at every link in the ecosystem were humans, and human diversity in naming and presentation has soaked into the entire software and hardware ecosystem.

The AI era has changed this. The construction of software is being carried out by AI-capable computers iterating on themselves; humans retreat to the outside of the ecosystem and interact with it through input and output. That being so, the internal operation has no need to keep using human natural language as the rules of naming and syntax. It is like a fully automated production line: there is no need for the robotic arm to imitate human limbs. Natural language and graphical interfaces will henceforth serve only as the interface for interacting with humans; inside the system, the hash is the identity.

Doing this also solves an old problem. Human naming tends to give the same name to different things: something called addition might be unlimited-precision, might wrap on overflow, might be floating-point addition with a specific rounding rule. With content as identity, any difference in implementation is reliably reflected as a difference of identity.

### Compatibility first, then replacement

The new foundation cannot require the world to abandon the old ecosystem first. My approach is compatibility first, then replacement: with AI presiding, describe the old system's code interfaces, data formats and calling conventions as a Merkle-DAG structure; first wrap the old object in a wrapping layer, so that seen from outside it is no different from a new object and can be put to use immediately; at the same time, on the inside, progressively analyze dependencies, construct replacement implementations, verify them, and then take over piece by piece. I call this AI-driven migration mechanism the software seed: a seed is planted on an application, a device, the whole of a person's digital resources, or even an organization; it develops on its own and completes the wrapping and the replacement. As long as every replacement is functionally equivalent, the whole process is smooth and imperceptible to the user.

Working together with it are the cold path and the hot path. A person learning to drive has to think through every action at first; after repeated training, the thinking process is compressed into muscle memory. Software is the same: when a new task appears for the first time, a large model thinks it through and writes it on the spot, and this is the cold path; once verified to work well, it is solidified into a reusable program that is called directly the next time, and this is the hot path. All users share their hot paths with one another: as soon as one person has implemented something, everyone can use it directly; when someone produces a more efficient implementation, after evaluation it becomes the new version.

### How to know it is actually better

Someone will say that ordinary software can achieve these capabilities too. That sentence only shows that they do not depend on a new principle of computation; it does not answer the other question: can different participants across the globe continuously combine their respective results at a lower cost of coordination? The hardware of computer networks had long been mature, but it was not until protocols like TCP/IP and HTTP appeared that the information revolution truly began. Sometimes no brand-new technology is needed; a new way of building is enough to produce revolutionary change.

The variables truly worth measuring are these: how many independent participants can reuse one act of creation? How much new wiring work does adding one participant require? How fast can one improvement propagate while preserving quality and control over data? The test that best decides the prospects is a small closed ecosystem loop: several independently developed applications sharing, under a common contract, one intermediate result and one replaceable function; a new participant completing a composition without modifying the original applications; an implementation, once improved, adopted by other participants after independent verification. Against a mature ordinary solution, compare wiring hours, change-propagation cost and reuse rate. If, when the fourth application and the second implementation are added, the added work keeps dropping significantly, then direct evidence for this way of building begins to accumulate. This test is what my own project is doing, and it has already produced negative results that must be recorded truthfully.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Content addressing can only confirm byte identity; it does not decide whether two programs are semantically the same, nor does it prove that the code is not malicious. Does the word "unification" hide unsolved problems inside it?**

A: The hash guarantees three things: the same content gets the same identity, different content gets different identities, and given the content you can verify whether it has been replaced. Whether semantics are equivalent, which version should be chosen, whether the source is trustworthy: these are another layer of work built on top of identity. Equivalence is decided by benchmarks and verification evidence; the choice is decided by contract, load and permissions; trustworthiness is answered by the traceable contribution history recorded in the same structure. Each of these layers can be done, and each can only be done once the identity layer is clean. Take the problem of names and addresses out of human hands first, and only then do the remaining problems become, for the first time, something you can get your hands on.

## Chapter 10: Personal Digital Space, Records, and Data Sovereignty

### A dashcam for one's life

The foundation of the previous chapter, besides carrying public code and data, must also carry every person. Every trace of behavior and every data product that a person leaves in digital space, what he said, what he did, what assets and abilities he has, what ideas he has, everything that left a digital imprint, whether notes, articles, the audio and video recorded by the devices he carries, or conversations with agents, is all recorded. It amounts to a dashcam and black box for one's life.

This person's proxy agent knows all of his interests, beliefs, demands, rights and obligations, and his relationships with other people; when he takes part in economic, political and social activity, it uses the reasoning power of AI to give him equally powerful judgment and game-playing ability. Only then can the cooperation and competition of the whole society become a fully equal, fully rational game.

A person's digital space is part of that person's capability. Whoever controls this space controls that person's capability in the game.

### The rules of viewing permissions

The design goal of this scheme is to preserve as much information as possible that benefits the evolution of the whole system, on the premise that no substantive harm is done to individual interests. So viewing permissions are the core. The rules are as follows:

1. By default, each person has the right to view only the historical information he could himself see and hear in his own life. No one has the right to view information he could not, by his own subjective capacity, have collected. This is fundamentally different from the present way of doing things: at present, the collectors and managers of information can view other people's information at will.
2. Viewing of public information is event-driven. Only when an event has more than one participant, when the event requires a fair judgment and decision to produce a resolution, and when this cannot be done with one person's information alone, does a fair algorithm compute the set of individuals involved, and only after obtaining the consent and authorization of those individuals is the right to view obtained. And the whole process, from viewing the information to reaching the judgment and ruling and the resolution, is preserved as common knowledge.

These two rules put into practice the boundary described in Chapter 4: a person's historical field of view becomes a concrete permission; a multi-party event obtains evidence without generating unlimited access rights. Collection devices obey the same constraint: on entering a setting where the new mechanism is not in effect, they default to off, and when switched on manually a conspicuous signal lights up, giving other people the right to know and to object; the device's keys and firmware version can be verified, which constrains the device's manufacturer and administrators.

### Why people will trust it

Because the entity behind it is not a traditional company but an organization that runs by the new rules, which can guarantee two things. First, all information and all behavior are preserved as traceable, tamper-proof records, so any action that infringes on a user's interests is reliably recorded. Second, everyone's personal income is distributed according to need, so no one can obtain any extra return from encroaching on users' interests. The former guarantees that no one has the ability to infringe on users' interests without leaving a trace; the latter guarantees that no one has the motive to.

How does a new organization prove it deserves trust? Not by promises, but by a record that begins growing on the first day of its founding and that no single node can control.

### How records change both before and after

For deliberate, malicious harm, "behavior being reliably recorded" serves not only for detection and accountability after the fact; it also has a preventive effect beforehand. Tit-for-tat is common knowledge: every person knows that his malicious behavior will be recorded, and that it will bring punishment. As long as the loss inflicted by punishment after the fact exceeds the benefit gained, this rule has a preventive effect beforehand on any rational person. In the future everyone will have his own AI agent to supplement his rational judgment, and the great majority of behavior will be carried out or mediated by agents; with this layer of buffering, emotional acts of harm will also be greatly reduced.

For harm that is not deliberately malicious, the kind caused by one's own limitations or by external force majeure, this mechanism is equally important. The occurrence of harm can never be completely eliminated, but behavior being reliably recorded and processed is the precondition for the whole system to reduce repeated mistakes and to gain in wit from every fall.

What people fear is not that their personal information and behavior are recorded; what they fear is that the ability to view and exploit that information falls into the hands of a few and becomes a weapon against them.

One more point determines the new system's advantage in data. However fierce the old system's collection, it can only get what people are willing to expose: finished products, successes, the outward story. What it cannot get is failures, abandoned plans, reasons for changing one's mind, because under competition and accountability, hiding these is rational. And that is precisely the data most lacking for building theory and correcting errors, and it is also the training material that the Large Civilization Model of the previous Part needs most. The data advantage of the new system lies not in "more", but in a class of data the old system is structurally unable to produce: decisions, outcomes, reversals, and the "why" alongside them.

### Unalterable history, with retraction and deletion

Records are append-only, rules can change, and the archive will outlive any version of the rules. So on top of the unalterable history there must be retraction and deletion: a record can be marked as withdrawn, an authorization can be revoked, a piece of data can be removed from public view, and these operations are themselves recorded. What unalterability guarantees is that "what happened" cannot be forged, not that "anyone can see everything forever". Once these two things are separated, a person can live, as a continually changing human being, inside a traceable memory: your record of today will not be tampered into something else, and your choice of tomorrow will not be locked down by the record of yesterday.

### Taking data back from the giants

I once applied to a large-model company to export all of my conversation data, and the reply was that it might take several days. If there is computing power to support massive volumes of real-time conversation, how can it fail to support a simple package-and-download? This difference in treatment of computing power and storage between the paid inference service and the user's data export shows that the service provider cares more about the business that makes it money than about the user's rights. This is the unfairness that arises when power between the service provider and the user is unequal.

The first thing the new mechanism does is to establish, for every user, local and cloud computing and storage space that the user can completely control, and take back the data that was scattered among the information silos of the various commercial giants. This involves three successive tasks. Recovering the historical accumulation: obtaining personal records from different platforms, clients and formats while preserving original content and provenance. Restoring usability: rebuilding conversational relationships, timelines and semantic connections so that the recovered data can continue to be searched, computed on and collaborated with. Establishing a continuous, autonomous mode of production: newly produced records promptly enter the user-controlled space, and when switching models or service providers one can carry the existing state and keep working.

There is only one criterion for testing this direction: after cutting the connection to any one original service provider, can the user keep his records and knowledge structure, choose a different set of tools, and continue the original work?

My own knowledge base is the first landing point: more than a year of sessions and projects scattered across different computers and different AI platforms, organized into a personal knowledge base in Merkle-DAG, where every original utterance has a content identifier, a source and a time, AI replies and my own statements are marked separately, and every version can be checked. This book grew out of that base.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: A complete record of a person's cognition, plus a model that can predict him, is the most perfect target for manipulation.**

A: That is why this chapter's permission rules are not "record everything, publish everything", but "record completely, with the person controlling the scope of disclosure". By default only you can see your own field of view; multi-party events have their scope computed by algorithm and require authorization; every act of viewing is itself recorded. Manipulation relies on information asymmetry: one side can see, the other cannot. When viewing is symmetric, recorded and authorized, the manipulator is first seen by his target. As for threats from outside the system: content identity and distributed storage mean the record is not attached to any single node that can be taken over; and personal data sovereignty is a basic guarantee that is not conditional on how much one has contributed or whether one agrees with the philosophy.

# Part IV: The Path

## Chapter 11: The Gap Between Technology and Institutions

### A startling fact

The first three Parts covered the principles, the construction and the foundation. This Part returns to reality: why we must start now, and where to start.

Since the Second World War, the rules of the political, economic and financial game between the nations of the world, and the political and economic systems within each nation, have seen almost no fundamental change. Technology over the same period has developed at breakneck speed. Information technology alone has gone through the waves of the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet and AI. From the late seventies to today, the world's total wealth has grown rapidly, and the gap between rich and poor has grown wider and wider: the share of total wealth held by the richest one-thousandth of the population in the United States tripled over thirty-odd years, and the share held by the richest one percent in China doubled in twenty. Every country shows fractured public opinion and rising nationalism, people everywhere are dissatisfied with their governments and their economic conditions, and conflict between nations continues without pause.

At the individual level, the technology dividend has improved people's objective conditions of survival; but the many negative effects of a governance system out of step with technological development, compounded by the information age's amplifying effect, in which bad news travels a thousand miles, have pushed ordinary people, and young people above all, into a collective slide in subjective well-being.

Look at the world today. On one side, we can manipulate single atoms, pack billions of transistors onto a chip the size of a fingernail, edit genes to treat disease, build spacecraft and head for space. On the other side, our societies are full of ideological conflict, political turmoil and brutal war, from road rage, domestic violence and labor conflicts at the small end, to riots in the streets and protracted wars at the large end, not to mention humanity's fear of nuclear weapons and of AI.

### The ability to manipulate the external world and the ability to control oneself

Chapter 4 gave the reason. For every other complex system, the data to be collected is not humanity itself; for the system of human civilization, the data to be collected is every person's information about themselves, and whether people are willing to provide it is the bottleneck. That is why humanity's ability to understand and transform nature runs so far ahead of its ability to understand and control its own social system.

If a civilization's ability to manipulate the external world does not match its ability to control itself, sooner or later it will do something self-harming or even self-destroying. It is like a child of immense strength but a fragile body, swinging a thousand-pound chain flail: sooner or later it hits itself. In little more than a century humanity has already been through two world wars, the Cold War and the nuclear crisis, and now stands in the middle of the confrontation between China and America and the AI race, each side afraid that whoever masters AI first will destroy the other, each straining to get ahead, exactly like the nuclear arms race of old.

Technology can help and can harm, and there are two root causes: the reliability of the technology itself, and the reliability of the civilizational entity that holds and uses it. The present situation makes the second factor the weakest link. If energy and resources are all poured into the capability and reliability of technology instead of into raising the reliability of civilization itself, the gap between the two will only keep widening, and the final result is climbing a tree to catch fish, driving south to reach the north.

### The root of the old institutions' disease

This gap is not accidental. Chapters 3 and 4 already explained its mechanism: the inequality of power in the pyramid structure produces unfair distribution, unfair distribution produces distrust, distrust produces a still larger power gap, and so on around the loop. Technological progress speeds up the creation of wealth, and in this loop the creation of wealth only speeds up the divergence. The technology dividend has not automatically turned into shared happiness. That is not a problem of technology; it is a problem of institutions.

The old institutions are hard to change, and that too has its mechanism. Historically, replacing an institution has usually meant violent revolution. A person of the old system possesses a life experience long adapted to the old system; they have never really experienced the new mechanism. People resist and doubt what is unknown and unexperienced; that is the nature of seeking gain and avoiding harm. Chapter 14 will explain how the new system provides prior experience, experience that people can take part in and verify.

### A higher-order regulatory mechanism

The direction of evolution is to use higher-order modes of regulation to take over the situations that the lower-level modes of regulation cannot handle. This does not mean the lower-level modes become obsolete; they go on handling their own business at their own levels.

The human nervous system is layered in just this way. The autonomic nerves control circulation, breathing and digestion without passing through the brain; trained actions become muscle memory and skip the thinking; only complex tasks are controlled by the reason of the brain. Decisions driven purely by instinct are often bad for the body: instinct pushes a person to eat as much as possible, and in today's world of material abundance that means eating oneself fatter and fatter. A mature person can, according to the situation, combine the brain's high-level regulation with the body's instinctive drives.

The same holds for civilization. The natural regulation of the market and the natural power games between people are like physiological instinct: they can complete the vast majority of the economic and political activity of daily social life. But the market economy produces economic crises and financial crises: once an industry shows a high rate of return, everyone piles in; the AI boom has driven up the price of memory and chips, until ordinary people who want to add a computer find they can no longer afford one. It is as if a person thinking at full capacity, without sound central regulation, let all the nutrients flow to the brain: the other organs would be starved.

What illustrates the point best is the cytokine storm. Faced with a foreign virus, the body could respond strategically and gently, but the immune system sometimes launches an attack with everything it has: kill eight hundred of the enemy, lose a thousand of your own. Many people end up killed by the cytokine storm itself. Bats have a strong capacity to coexist with viruses; after infection they do not die in large numbers. Violent conflicts between nations and between groups are the cytokine storm of civilization: there is no need at all to spend so many resources on the attack, yet because the other side is treated as an outsider that must be wiped out completely, what ends up harmed is oneself.

The mechanism I envision is that higher-order regulation at the level of civilization: use collective reason, everyone thinking together and dynamic weights then selecting the consensus plan, to counteract the crises and violent conflicts that low-level mechanisms such as the market, finance and power games can produce at the macro scale, so that the price of civilization's evolution is smaller and its course smoother. This is also the match between productive forces and relations of production: steam and electricity gave birth to the capitalist system, which in turn further released the productive forces; now, with computers and AI, one more layer of regulation can be built above the layer of capital, regulating capital no longer through capital alone, but through knowledge and intelligence.

### We do not have much time left

AI has already begun to acquire a preliminary capacity to iterate and evolve on its own. China and the United States are caught in something like a prisoner's dilemma: confronting and distrusting each other, neither is willing to stop accelerating AI research, the acceleration of AI is irreversible, and the day of the technological singularity draws closer and closer. Once the technological singularity arrives while the mismatch between civilization's institutions and technological development remains unsolved, the gap between the two will widen sharply, and the probability that humanity, out of fear of AI or out of fear of the few who control AI, walks into self-destruction will rise sharply along with it.

So within this brief window, we must design, as fast as we can, a system of human civilization that has the same self-iterating, self-upgrading mechanism as AI systems, so that the speed of civilization's evolution matches the speed of technology's evolution, and the singularity of civilization and the technological singularity arrive together.

### Humanity's road is not just two tracks

In the science fiction work *Watchmen*, in order to pull the United States and the Soviet Union back from the brink of nuclear war, the smartest man in the world creates a more powerful common enemy, using an external threat to force all sides into peace. The tension between them does not disappear; it is only pressed together by a larger outside force. He replaces a greater loss with a smaller one, a solution of the trolley-problem kind. In my view this is not the best course. Because the trajectory of human society's evolution is not a track laid down in advance, and the choice is not between the two tracks of crushing one person or crushing a crowd.

The trolley problem is a dilemma because it presupposes tracks. A civilization that can correct itself, explore in parallel, and move its conflicts out of reality into virtual space to make trial and error there faces not two tracks but an open field that can be laid out anew. Human history has seen countless disagreements that looked impossible to resolve, and that were then unified or replaced by new institutions, knowledge and technologies as understanding and technology advanced. In disagreements in the field of technology, what is lost is only intellect and computing power; in political disagreements, once the machinery of violence joins in, what is lost is human life. What a better meta-strategy has to achieve is exactly this: the transformation of institutions no longer costs human lives.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: If a more advanced mechanism can only be built by first destroying the old one, then the losses it causes during the transition should be counted in its own total cost.**

A: Agreed, and this is exactly why I insist on a non-violent transition. When comparing two civilizational mechanisms, we cannot compare only their efficiency once they are running stably; we must also compare the costs of learning, migration, reorganization and risk that moving from one mechanism to the other necessarily entails. As long as the new mechanism is even slightly stronger than the old one, people will switch of their own accord; what we need is not the energy to destroy the old system, but a reduction in the energy the switch requires. That is what the engineering route of Chapter 9, compatibility first, then replacement, means at the level of institutions.

## Chapter 12: Alignment and the Fork in the Road

### Aligned with whom

Alignment, for artificial intelligence, means ensuring that the outputs and behavior of an AI system stay consistent with human values, intentions and social norms, making the model an assistant that is helpful, honest and harmless.

Start from this definition and combine it with an objective fact: within human civilization, the values, intentions and norms of different peoples, different regimes and different classes differ from one another and cannot be made consistent. The conclusion follows naturally: in the game of human society, every player with the ability to create and control AI, whether a regime, a company or an individual, holds an assistant that is "helpful, honest and harmless" to itself, and that assistant may become, in the eyes of the other players, the accomplice of a fearsome adversary. And AI's powerful capacity for self-iteration amplifies and accelerates the capability of the player who controls it, and also widens the gap between players: a difference of a few months is a difference of hundreds or thousands of times in capability.

### Balanced or unbalanced, the result is terrifying

If development is unbalanced and one player dominates, then for the dominant player the highest-return, lowest-cost way to play is to use AI to suppress all the others, ending in winner-take-all.

If development is balanced and several parties stand off against one another, then because of mutual distrust and insufficient exchange of information, the parties still run the danger of triggering destruction through one small misunderstanding, without any strong malice, just as during the Cold War the whole world worried that the powers holding nuclear weapons might destroy the world over a small misunderstanding.

The child of Chapter 11 swinging the chain flail now has a concrete setting. The AI race between China and the United States is like a creature with two brains, a left and a right, each hand swinging its own chain flail, but with no effective alignment mechanism between the two brains; sooner or later the two hammers will collide and tangle, and the creature will wound itself.

These two endings share the same structure: each is the endpoint of the negative chain reaction of Chapter 4.

### Three layers of alignment

What truly most needs to be solved right now is the alignment between the values, intentions and social norms of every organization and every individual in all of humanity, and their behavior. Once that is solved, the alignment between humanity and AI, which is the aggregated product of human knowledge, follows as a matter of course. One step further still is the alignment between the new civilizational entity of humans plus AI and the whole ecology of the Earth.

Alignment therefore has three layers: the alignment of humans with humans, the alignment of humans with AI, and the alignment of civilization with the ecology. All the attention today is on the middle layer, and the middle layer looks unsolvable because the first layer has not been done. A civilization that is internally split in its values and whose parts threaten one another will train "aligned" AIs each of which is a piece of that split. An intelligence takes its training material from all humanity, but its goal is set by whoever trains it; so alignment between humans and AI comes down to who sets the goal and by what standard.

Two things can be settled now. First, alignment is not a fixed endpoint but a state in operation: when new things appear, new players enter, or resources change, the old balance breaks. So what alignment requires is not a one-time injection of values but a continuous capacity for correction, which is exactly what a social neural network of dynamic weights does. Second, alignment is not one party imposing its values on the others; what that produces is a tension held down by external force, ready to spring back at any moment, like the false peace in *Watchmen*. True alignment is the position the parties walk to on their own, under common knowledge, verifiable records and symmetric rules of the game.

This is why this book does not treat AI safety as an independent technical problem, but as one indicator of a civilization's capacity for self-governance. To make AI trustworthy, first make civilization trustworthy; to make civilization trustworthy, first make it possible for every node inside it, under common knowledge and symmetric play, to verify one another and correct one another instead of threatening one another.

### A thought experiment: the choice of the richest person in the world

What does moving to the new system mean for the greatest beneficiaries of the old system?

Under the existing system, many rich and powerful people, sitting on uncountable wealth and enormous power, remain anxious in the struggle for advantage between persons and between groups, forever afraid of losing everything in competition and confrontation, guarding not only against enemies but suspecting friends too. These problems are not removed by holding the dominant position in the game: the incompleteness of information means that the probability of total defeat can never be lowered to the point where you can fully rest easy.

Suppose you are the richest person in the world, sitting on a vast fortune, and you are sixty this year. Under the old system, immortality technology will take forty years to achieve, and your life expectancy is ninety, so you will most likely die before it appears. Under the new system, you still have personal resources sufficient to keep you healthy and happy, while you give up full control over that vast sum of public resources, handing it over to the dynamic-weight system that aggregates the collective intelligence of all humanity, and letting your own knowledge and intellect become one decision node in that system. What you get in exchange: you no longer need to live in daily dread inside a game of information silos; more important, under this new decision system and mechanism of evolution, immortality technology arrives in twenty years. Now, would you rather be the richest person in the old system, or an immortal in the new one?

What the new mechanism sacrifices is near-term and temporary gains; what it obtains is greater long-term gains. Once both the allocation of individual resources and the decision authority over collective resources have more reasonable rules, a new equilibrium is reached: anyone who tries to make a different choice will only, after repeated play, obtain a worse result. Even the greatest beneficiary of the old system gains more under the new.

Of course there is also a worst local optimum: a small elite, while monopolizing wealth and power, also monopolizes every superior capability, for instance the monopoly on becoming immortal. That is the truly terrifying world. But once a small minority tries to monopolize, the overwhelming majority will not submit meekly, and the result will very likely be a war involving all of humanity.

### Two directions

We are standing at a critical fork in the road. One direction leads through a negative chain reaction to civilization's self-destruction; the other leads through a positive chain reaction toward the singularity of civilization's evolution, toward a new form of human civilization.

The two chains of Chapter 4 are idealized extremes, and most of the time in reality we are somewhere between them. But on the scale of civilization the two extremes really exist: at one end nuclear war and AI out of control, at the other a planetary civilization able to know itself and correct itself. The time for swinging in the middle is not unlimited, and the technological singularity is compressing it.

The spread of advanced process nodes and lithography technology could plainly drive human civilization forward at speed, yet nations, companies and individuals, locked in the confrontation of the old system, use secrecy and sanctions to make prisoner's-dilemma choices that look like benefiting oneself at others' expense but in the long run hurt both sides. Under the new mechanism, every person records their own life and, with the help of AI, rapidly understands and masters the knowledge of this world; every tiny spark of thought is recorded and spread, is quickly turned, with the cooperation of others, into an increment of knowledge, and is added to the knowledge base of all humanity, and so the cycle continues.

The choice between these two roads is not made for humanity by anyone. It is made by every person, a little at a time, each time they decide whether to share or to conceal, whether to cooperate or to guard. What comes next is how to begin with the first person.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Disagreements over values are not disagreements over facts. Science can dissolve disagreements over facts; it cannot dissolve disagreements over values. How could the first layer of alignment possibly be achieved by a single mechanism?**

A: The principle of relativity in Chapter 2 already answers half of this: there is no absolute coordinate system of values, only transformations between coordinate systems. What the first layer of alignment demands is not that everyone hold the same set of values, but that everyone accept the same set of transformation rules: verify facts under common knowledge, negotiate values under symmetric play, and bear the consequences under the rule that failure means compensation and correction. This set of rules contains no specific value content of its own, so it applies equally to people holding every kind of value. Disagreements over values do not vanish under these rules; they become the diversity described in Chapter 1, part of the space of evolution rather than a reason to annihilate one another. The other half of the answer is that piece of meta-cognition: it reduces the opposition of values from "good against evil" to "differences with conditions of formation," lets the disagreement cool down psychologically first, and then enter the rules.

## Chapter 13: Starting with One Person: The Free Zone and Peaceful Evolution

### The first thing: taking back personal data and services

The first step by which the whole vision lands in practice is to build a free zone inside the old system that conforms to the new vision, then gain advantage through competition with the old system, and expand step by step, peacefully, to replace it. Beginning with the recovery of personal data and services is an excellent starting point. Starting with me, one person.

What I have in mind is not merely the ability to pull my data back at any time. It is the replacement of commercial companies' products with software and services supplied by the organizational form of the new system. This goes a step further than merely using open-source software: not only is the product open source, but the organizational entity that produces, designs and operates these products and services also runs under the new mechanism. The organizational form, the method of distribution, the decision algorithms are all open source. Anyone can audit, anyone can trace, anyone can take part in decisions.

The first service is cloud storage. What it delivers is not a block of disk space but something more concrete: one person's ability to independently recover and keep using all of their own material. First, preserve the material from which one can start over, original files, original conversations, original records, each with a content identifier and a source; then, on top of that, build an ever-better understanding, semantic relations, timelines, versions. The user holds their own identity and keys, keeps recoverable copies, and can switch the party hosting the cloud side.

Then expand step by step: for software and services, substitute open-source projects first, then provide compatible interfaces and take over the services commercial companies provide; for hardware, rent first, buy when enough people have joined and the funds are sufficient, then go on to designing and manufacturing it ourselves; do cloud storage first, then cloud computing, using ready-made open-source models at first and, once the finances are solid, training models ourselves. Each step is the natural result of the previous step running successfully, and every expansion should be backed by real conditions.

### Internal organization changes from day one

Throughout this whole process, from the very beginning, the internal organizational form exists by the new rules: the allocation for individual needs is separated from the allocation of collective resources, every decision concerning the collective is made by the dynamic weights of all participants, and every piece of information that ought to be public is recorded, tracked and made tamper-proof as common knowledge, recording each participant's contribution. Outwardly, it takes the form of a traditional commercial company and competes fairly in the market with the organizations of the old system.

At the beginning there is only me, so the initial allocation and decisions are made by me alone; later, when there are several people, those people allocate and decide according to the new rules. Funds and resources come in much as in crowdfunding, but with three differences. First, consumers are also producers and creators; it is not a matter of paying money without contributing effort. Second, there is no need to reach some headcount or funding target before starting: it starts with the cheapest and simplest cloud storage, with my own pocket money. Third, the ways of taking part are flexible: those who have confidence can put in more money and effort, and those who lack confidence can be consumers only. But in every case, the user's data sovereignty is guaranteed. Data sovereignty is the baseline guarantee, not conditional on how much money a user puts in, whether they contribute labor, or whether they agree with the ideas.

Gains in collaboration efficiency do not automatically become fair distribution. If a new system halves the total cost of a task, how that value is divided among users, developers, data contributors and compute providers requires independent rules. Otherwise the new system can perfectly well recreate the concentration of wealth while raising efficiency. So the rules of distribution must develop in step with the technical prototype, and this is exactly why the internal organization must change from day one.

### We need not wait for the ideal state; the smallest unit of conversion is a task

A game with sufficient and complete information, and full peer equality between nodes, are descriptions of an ideal state. The new mechanism does not need to reach this goal immediately; as long as it beats the old one by even a little, that is enough to begin the replacement. When comparing a traditional collaboration system with a new system, it is enough to observe that the latter, on the same kind of task, reduces the cost of finding knowledge, duplicated labor, collaboration delay or the cost of correcting errors. That is already a valuable result.

When people discuss replacing one system with another, they habitually take the nation, the enterprise or even the individual as the unit of conversion, as if a person had to first decide whether to join the new system completely. But the smallest unit of conversion in the new mechanism can be a single task, a single collaboration, a single contribution of knowledge. A person can keep a full-time job at a traditional company while using spare time to complete a task through the public collaboration network; that task's requirements, decomposition, knowledge sources, contributions, verification results and payment are all recorded; next week they can go back to doing other things in the traditional way. They do not need to change their social identity first in order to genuinely experience the new collaboration mechanism.

The path from spare-time participation to a real occupation is therefore testable: first learn and experience, with no employment or financial risk at all; then low-risk task collaboration; then paid real tasks, earning verifiable compensation under clear contracts, payment and dispute rules; then stable hybrid collaboration; and finally, only when income stability and quality of life meet one's own requirements, considering a change to one's main work arrangement. The new mechanism must lower not only the cost of executing tasks but also the cost and risk of migrating over from the old mechanism.

### A unified base, diverse forks

How does an evolvable system, among the near-infinite possible paths of institutional evolution, fork efficiently in parallel, explore in diverse ways, and find the better path at lower cost? This is the core question of institutional evolution.

The DNA system of living things is unified in its rules and architecture, and this unity did not restrict diversity; it released it. Institutions are the same. What is unified is the five rules of Chapter 4, the two kinds of distribution of Chapter 5, the dynamic weights of Chapter 6, and the data sovereignty of Chapter 10: these are the base. On top of the base, the concrete organizational forms, the detailed rules of distribution, the decision procedures can all fork, run in parallel, and be compared. China's special economic zones, which experiment locally first and spread what works, are in essence diversified experiment plus feedback selection. The new mechanism turns this into a basic capability that every participant can use: any small organization can fork its own rules on top of the base, run them, record them, compare them with other forks; the good ones are adopted, the poor ones are recorded as negative results.

### Free entry and free exit

The replacement process is free. Anyone can join or leave at any time, and the system provides compatibility of data and applications on joining and leaving, together with free import and export. Replacement relies not on force but on higher efficiency and better returns.

The right to exit is part of this mechanism, not a threat to it. Chapter 3 said how exit cost shapes institutions: where exit is expensive, a centralized order grows up; where exit is cheap, cooperation has to rely on portable reputation. The free zone is open, so it must have portable history: a person leaves carrying their entire record from here, takes that record to another fork, or brings it back. Portable history makes honest exit cheap and treacherous exit expensive. This is why the records and data sovereignty of Chapter 10 are not only protections for the individual; they are the precondition for this organization being able to be open.

### Why growing in scale should not turn back into a closed giant

A traditional giant cannot monopolize this ecology by locking down accounts, data, permissions or toolchains. It can only transform its own organizational structure, merge into the system, and become a powerful contributor; and it thereby changes itself from a pyramid organization into a decision system of equal division of labor and dynamic weights, with fairer distribution and higher efficiency, and the ailment by which a big company gets less efficient and less creative as it grows is cured at the root.

But internal equality and the absence of outward domination must each be implemented separately. Even if an organization practices joint governance internally, those who control key infrastructure, interfaces or resource scheduling can acquire new power. So open standards, interoperation between different nodes, replaceable administrators, appealable decisions, and members' real ability to exit or form a separate organization should all be capabilities continuously maintained throughout the expansion. If the right to exit, portable history and appeal work only internally, the free zone will grow hawks facing outward, as Chapter 3 said. So toward other people still in the old mechanism, the rule is: no worse off, once externalities are counted. That is the hard constraint on expansion.

### From productive cooperation to wider public affairs

On one side, advance the commercial territory in the real world; on the other, advance the cultural territory through literature, film, television and games. When the new system has matured to a certain degree in the economic and cultural domains, it can be carried into public affairs, so that the functions of traditional companies and state institutions are gradually replaced by the new architecture.

Two things must be kept apart here: eliminating permanent, unaccountable privileges of power and eliminating all coordinating functions are not the same thing. Any large-scale complex system needs to handle assignment of responsibility, emergency response, specialized coordination and disputes. The new system's approach is to turn these responsibilities into functional roles with clear scope and limited terms, that can be delegated and revoked, and that are subject to audit, rather than permanent positions bound to particular people.

Chapter 6 said where the new system's legitimacy comes from: what the losing side accepts is a rule that it is itself inside, which can be checked after the fact, and which will be corrected when wrong. This cannot be made believable by argument; it can only be made visible by running. So the free zone does not sell this institution; it builds its foundation. git never marketed open-source governance. First build what a single person needs on day one: verifiable records, portable personal data. The first organization to choose is a newly founded small group, a cooperative, a foundation, not a nation. If the foundation proves itself, people will come asking for the social layer; if not, no argument for the social layer, however good, means anything.

What truly deserves verification is whether the self-fulfilling positive feedback appears: when the new mechanism delivers real benefits, more people join voluntarily; more participants bring more knowledge, capability and opportunities to collaborate; these increments keep improving the system's performance. There is also a reverse mechanism: growing numbers can bring spam, fake contributions and governance complexity. So what needs to be watched is not raw user growth but whether, as the scale grows, the net benefit each participant obtains rises or falls. This is the most important early metric for the whole project.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The definition of a state is a monopoly on violence. A state that does not take part in your system can simply shut it down.**

A: Correct, which is why this book never advocates confronting the state. It advocates running, within the state and in a lawful organizational form, an entity that operates by the new rules, and letting its efficiency and the real gains of its members speak. Its relationship to the state is like git's relationship to software companies: git never overthrew a single company, but today not one company can do without it. A state can shut down a node; it cannot shut down a content-addressed, distributed way of keeping records that is already used by millions. When this way of working becomes part of people's daily work, the institutions themselves will begin to use it, and then depend on it. That is how institutions are replaced: layered on top of the old, one layer at a time, until the center of gravity shifts.

## Chapter 14: Science, Culture and Shared Creation

### Why prior experience is needed

Chapter 11 said that people in the old system have never truly experienced the new mechanism, and the instinct toward the unknown is resistance. Past transitions from old system to new, apart from violent revolution, were also completed through a prior experience at the spiritual level: the heaven of religion, the description of the future society in manifestos, the rendering of the American dream. The new system too needs to give people a prior experience, and the difference is this: its prior experience can be participated in and verified.

Three levels must be kept apart. Science fiction is responsible for expanding imagination: novels and film let people experience the fates of characters and the possible shapes of future life. Simulation is responsible for exploring possibilities: multiplayer civilization games let people experience, in a virtual world, different rules of collaboration and their consequences. Real-world practice is responsible for providing evidence: real collaboration experiments observe participants' costs, returns and satisfaction on real tasks.

These three levels can be joined into one road. A player plays a builder of the new civilization in the virtual world and takes part in a simulated task; later they discover that a certain task in the game has a real-world counterpart, and they can choose to join a real open-source project; they switch from virtual to real, not because the story demands that they accept some belief, but because they already understand the task, the rules and the risks and are willing to try; the results of the real project then feed back into the game's historical record and become data for the next round of simulation. From taking part in virtual scenarios in spare time, to solving real problems and earning income, to gradually putting more into the new system, there is no bloody conflict or revolution anywhere in the process.

I call this kind of work self-fulfilling science fiction. Traditional science fiction is responsible only for imagining, not for realizing; whereas the authors of this kind of work must at the same time be the first builders of the civilization it describes, otherwise it is only fantasy.

### The virtual social laboratory

Chapter 6 said that virtual tit-for-tat turns real damage into virtual damage; Chapter 13 said that institutions must fork, run in parallel and be compared on a unified base. These two things need the same thing: an AI-driven virtual social laboratory in digital space.

It lets institutions be experienced and tested before they are adopted. The cost of trial and error for a distribution rule in reality is real loss; in the laboratory it is virtual points. Validating a decision mechanism in reality takes years; in the laboratory, thousands of rounds can run in days. Results in the virtual world do not automatically predict real society. Its role is to screen out the plainly unworkable proposals before they reach reality, and to screen in the proposals worth piloting at small scale in reality. For it to have genuine research value, it must allow different institutional designs to coexist, fork, compete and iterate within it.

### Everyone is a student, and also a teacher

The new system is not one where people hand their happiness and their hopes to a Big Brother high above, just as the cells of the human body do not pin all their hopes on one super-cell. At the political level, everyone is a manager and also one of the managed; at the economic level, everyone is a producer and also a consumer; at the level of knowledge, everyone is a student and also a teacher. What governs everyone in the civilizational body is the intelligence produced by the civilizational brain that everyone forms as its neurons.

Under this system, education is no longer a pyramid that passes knowledge down from authorities to students, but a network in which everyone both absorbs and contributes. One person's depth in some field becomes a capability that others can use directly; one person's question in some field becomes the starting point of someone else's deep dive. The creation of knowledge and the spread of knowledge are two faces of one process.

### Bold hypothesis, rigorous reasoning

I once said to the AI I was discussing with: unless the most basic principles of physics and mathematics are violated, do not use current or outdated frameworks of social science to evaluate the infinite possibilities of future institutional rules. Because this body of theory is still in its pre-Newtonian period, and the different theories look more like expressions within their own frames of reference. I have found that AI, as an intelligent agent, tends to reason within the fixed frameworks already in its knowledge base. It tends toward marking the boat to find the sword, like the man who notched his boat to mark where his sword fell overboard, rather than daring to question the existing frameworks.

But after the bold hypothesis must come rigorous reasoning: accept the premise in full, do not cherry-pick the conclusions. Take an idea seriously and push downward using only physics and mathematics, and the constraints that follow often help the idea itself far more than an objection that presupposes different premises.

Good rules have one more criterion, the symmetry described in Chapter 2: they stay invariant under a change of unit of action. The unit can be an individual, an organization, a combination of human and AI. A rule about veto rights, if it still holds when the individual is replaced by an organization, is a rule about any collaborative system rather than an accidental fact about one kind of participant.

### How humans and AI research together

This book is itself the product of joint research between a human and several AIs. My way of working is this: on the same question, discuss separately with several AIs and hand each one's answers to the others; on a project, have different models act as researcher, planner, coder and evaluator. A few lessons came out of this process.

First, when multiple intelligences cooperate, they must avoid believing the same mistake twice over. Several models may share the same systematic bias, in which case their agreement is not evidence but noise; what is truly valuable is disagreement from independent sources. So I ask the AI for the strongest objection. The question-and-answer sections at the end of the chapters of this book came from exactly this.

Second, how research begins when there is no standard answer. Theoretical innovation has no clear right answer the way problem-solving does, but it has the ruler of Chapter 1: a shorter description, a less effortful construction, more external connections. Whether a new concept deserves to be kept depends on whether it makes previously invisible relations visible, and whether it creates operable bridges between previously independent fields.

Third, a grand vision must be tested by phenomena it played no part in designing, and these tests are mostly small, concrete, and capable of failing. So the research record must include failures, disagreements and costs, not only results. This book's knowledge base preserves, from every round of discussion, the AI's objections, my rebuttals, the corrections I accepted and the corrections I rejected, each marked separately. These negative results and disagreements are precisely the kind of data Chapter 10 said the old system cannot produce.

Fourth, the research process itself should be solidified into a reusable capability. I am still at the stage of interacting manually with several AIs. The next step is to automate this process, letting the roles of research, planning, development and evaluation advance automatically, with results shared, branched and merged through the version repository; once this mechanism works properly, extend the project from individual development to community collaboration. The mark of a project's maturity is that its development process is ultimately completed, through iteration, by its own capability.

Science, education and creation are one and the same thing in this ecology: every exploration by every node becomes a capability the whole network can reuse; every failure becomes the whole network's memory; every person is a student of this process, and its teacher, and its author.

This book set out from a few of the most reliable principles in natural science: minimization and maximization, the fixed point, emergence, relativity. It then climbed along one continuous chain: tit-for-tat as the iteration operator that builds social structure, common knowledge and the two chain reactions, the two kinds of resources and equality in the sense of the rules, the social neural network of dynamic weights, intelligence as public infrastructure, the Large Civilization Model in which training and inference are one, the carrier of common knowledge in which content is identity, personal data sovereignty, and on to the path that begins with one person's cloud storage. Every step on this chain follows the same underlying law: a stable structure maintains itself at the least internal cost, and with the greatest external connectivity gives rise to a structure one level higher. The evolution of human civilization's institutions is a local instance, on this small planet Earth, of the evolution of the whole universe. The mechanism I envision is nothing more than a civilizational mechanism with higher emergent efficiency.

Let me state the position of this book once more. I have not discovered and defined the specific rules and structures of a new civilizational order. What this book gives is a foundation of mathematical principles, strategies for the meta-game and a plan for getting started: a compass, a telescope and a chart, not the destination; a torch held up to scout the road toward the singularity of civilization, so that the road may be traveled a little more smoothly and a little faster, and the ever-widening gap in speed between institutions and technology does not bring disaster on human civilization.

And the direction it points in is the "bitter lesson" at the level of the evolution of civilization's institutions. In 2019 Sutton looked back over seventy years of AI and drew a conclusion many researchers found hard to swallow: hand-writing the knowledge and experience of human experts into a system always works in the short run, and always loses in the long run to general methods plus enormous data and computing power. Chess, Go, speech, vision, language, without exception; in protein folding, methods built on decades of accumulated expert experience were overtaken by one model at a stroke. The social domain has long shown the same signs: more than half a century of research has shown again and again that simple statistical models match or beat the personal judgment of experts in prediction, and that a group weighted by its track record beats the individual authority. Yet humanity still hands its most complex problem, the construction and organization of society, to the personal experience and learning of political, economic and academic authorities, to be solved through simple cooperation. That is how the traditional edifice of science was built, brick by brick, by geniuses and authorities. I do not deny its achievements: with the experience and knowledge of individuals, humanity built today's system of science and its political and economic institutions; today's large language models were trained on exactly what those geniuses and authorities wrote; and in the Large Civilization Model they are not abolished either, but become nodes carrying weights. But to reach the singularity of technology and civilization, to make a leap to a higher level, the best road is the other one: use mathematics and computers to model mathematically the complex problems of social construction and organization that all of humanity faces, gather data on an enormous scale, and, in the loop of learning, training and inference in practice, construct that Large Civilization Model. This is the bitter lesson of civilization's institutions. As the preface said, at the level of civilization its price is blood and lives, so it is in truth a cruel lesson. Humanity will have to swallow it sooner or later, and the sooner the better.

Now it is time. Throughout the history of human civilization, the governance of civilization has rested on leaders and sages who, each drawing on his own knowledge and experience of the social domain, designed ingenious rules; and in the conflicts their differing ideas produced, humanity has tasted the "bitter lesson" again and again. It is time to bid farewell to that scene and embrace a new civilizational order: one driven by a social intelligent neural network, founded on general algorithms grounded in mathematical and scientific theory, sustained by computing power on a massive scale, and capable of recursive self-improvement and self-evolution.

For more than a hundred years, humanity's visions of the ideal future society have been split by one central divide: liberalism (also called liberal democracy) and Marxism (also called socialism or communism). Since the middle of the nineteenth century these two systems of thought have been the two central ideological currents shaping the political and social landscape of the modern world. Under the new civilizational order, all these differences of human cognition will gradually iterate and merge, and in the end forge a civilization of the people, by the people, for the people, that shall long evolve and shall not perish from the earth; in place of the old bourgeois society, with its classes and class antagonisms, we shall have an association, in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all.

The first half of that sentence comes from Lincoln's address at Gettysburg in 1863; the second half comes from the Communist Manifesto of Marx and Engels in 1848. Two sentences written fifteen years apart, and since assigned to two opposing camps, join into one complete sentence.

So the most important thing is for all of humanity to reach consensus on this direction as soon as possible and begin trying together. Where consensus comes from, AI's own history has already answered. No one first coordinated all of humanity into a consensus on deep learning: in 2012 one small group won one competition with one model, and the whole world followed. First a working instance, then consensus. And one more thing: when language models took off, the corpus was already lying on the internet; but the corpus the Large Civilization Model needs, every problem, every node's judgment, every collective decision, every real-world result, has never yet been systematically recorded. The dataset comes before the model. So the two most urgent things today are to build the first instance that runs as a closed loop, and to begin accumulating the first batch of records. Neither needs anyone's permission, and one person's knowledge base is its first seed.

This theoretical framework is far from complete. It is a torch, a starting point that invites readers to think together. Finishing it needs the formalization of mathematicians, the experimental verification of scientists, the implementation of engineers, and also the participation and testing of every ordinary person. And in the system I envision, these people need not belong to any particular profession. There, everyone is a student and also a teacher; a consumer and also a producer; one of the managed and also a manager; a neuron of this civilization and also its author.

Let everyone become a creator. This is not a slogan. It is what happens naturally once this mechanism is up and running.

Starting with me, one person.

## Appendix: Grounds, Inferences and Unfinished Work

The preface said that the different parts of this book vary in reliability. Here I separate them item by item, so that no reader mistakes an inference for a settled conclusion, or something that can be done today for a fantasy.

### Grounds: settled mathematics and science

The following are not discoveries of this book; this book only uses them.

- Variational principles and thermodynamics: least action, free-energy minimization and entropy maximization, and the equivalent forms between them.
- Fixed points and equilibria: equilibrium states, attractors, the Nash equilibrium and its existence; dissipative structures.
- Symmetry and relativity: the invariance of physical laws under transformations of reference frame, and the correspondence between symmetries and conserved quantities.
- Emergence: higher-level structures have laws of their own, while obeying the constraints of the lower levels.
- Game theory: in repeated games both cooperation and defection can be equilibria; the performance of tit-for-tat in repeated prisoner's dilemma tournaments; the mixed equilibrium of the hawk-dove game; the definition and role of common knowledge; the security dilemma.
- Probability and networks: processes of proportional growth and "the rich get richer" produce long tails and power-law distributions.
- Machine learning: online learning algorithms that aggregate multiple judges weighted by their past performance, and their guarantees; the attention mechanism; methods of training through self-play and feedback when there is no standard answer; the history summed up by the "bitter lesson".
- Forecasting research: the comparison of statistical models with the personal judgment of experts, and group forecasts weighted by track record.
- Social choice and collective judgment: the anonymity of rules; the jury theorem, and the optimal aggregation weighted by the log-odds of accuracy; the theorem that individual rights of decision and the Pareto standard cannot be reconciled; empowerment and capability as measures of an agent's reachable future.
- History of science: the measurement of temperature came before thermodynamics, and the definition of temperature was corrected step by step in the course of measuring.
- Software engineering: cryptographic hashes, Merkle trees and content addressing, digital signatures, version control.

### Inferences: the generalizations and designs this book makes

The following rest on the grounds above and on complete chains of reasoning. They can be challenged, but the challenge should target a specific link in the chain.

- "Minimize internal cost, maximize external connectivity" as a working principle running through physics, biology and society. At the social level it has only one operational definition so far, strictness; this is a starting point, not an end point.
- The relativity of cognitive reference frames, and the meta-cognition derived from it.
- Tit-for-tat as the iteration operator of the evaluation function, strictness as the control parameter for convergence speed and stability; genes determining the ordering while the game determines the cut-off point; the authoritarian system as a stable attractor of a positive feedback loop, and the influence of exit cost on institutional form.
- The two chain reactions, the five meta-rules; the root of privacy and security problems is inequality of power.
- The division into two kinds of resources; power-law distribution arising from the compounding of unequal games; equality in the sense of the rules. Whether an institution is reasonable does not rest on any particular distribution of ability holding.
- The social neural network of dynamic weights, weights attaching to problems and expiring, and the equality of basic rights kept separate from the dynamics of influence.
- The training and inference of a civilizational body cannot be separated; every person's evaluations and decisions are the labels on the data; artificial general intelligence is the whole civilizational body, not any one model; the direction of the Large Civilization Model and social mechanics.
- Freedom, equality and democracy as three types of mathematical object: a quantity, a symmetry, an aggregation operator. Their specific forms are only initial values, to be corrected by running forks and comparing them; the same standing under the rules, the ability to exit and fork, and every person's judgment entering, these three meta-rules are the precondition for the iteration itself to be trustworthy.
- The three layers of alignment, and the first layer determining the other two.
- No right of inheritance over personal resources. This one is my own opinion about a future institution, not a conclusion derived from the principles that precede it.

### What can be done today

The following, taking current and foreseeable computer and AI capabilities as the premise, are engineering problems.

- One person's Merkle-DAG personal knowledge base: take back all of one's records from every platform, store them by content identifier, source and time, mark original utterances and the AI's replies separately, with every version verifiable. The material of this book is in exactly such a base.
- A language that uses hashes as names and addresses, and a migration mechanism that is compatible first and replaces later. My project is doing this, and has already produced negative results that can be recorded.
- The hot path: a large model handles a task the first time it appears, and after verification it is solidified into a reusable program and shared among users.
- The collection of personal data, the rules of viewing permissions, and verifiable devices.
- An organization that begins with one person's cloud storage, and task-granularity participation.
- The minimal test of cooperation: two people, one task, one record.

### What cannot yet be promised

- The performance of the dynamic-weight social neural network in real social decisions. It needs to run first in domains where feedback is fast enough, and accumulate records first in domains where feedback is slow.
- That the new mechanism produces no new domination as it expands. This requires checking both internal equality and the absence of outward domination at every step of the expansion, rather than deducing it from the mechanism's definition.
- Whether the rules of distribution and the organizational form can stand on their own once the technology is running. Gains in efficiency do not automatically become fair distribution.
- The Large Civilization Model and social mechanics. Each of its units is itself a high-dimensional semantic model, and its demand for computing power is several orders of magnitude above today's large models; its training material can only accumulate once the social neural network is running. This is a direction, not a capability of today.
- The specific correspondences given for freedom, equality and democracy. How the reachable future is computed and how the weights are set are still only initial values, to be corrected by running forks in free zones and comparing them.
- Whether the results of the virtual laboratory can transfer to reality. Its role is to screen, not to replace real-world testing.

### How to let a scientific prophecy be corrected by reality

This book hopes to be a scientific prophecy capable of fulfilling itself on its own. But a prophecy must be correctable by reality; otherwise it is faith. There is only one way to make it correctable: turn everything this book says into a record that can be recorded, verified and overturned. The book's knowledge base has already done this: the original wording of every argument, every revision, every objection accepted and rejected, is all there. Readers can come to it with questions, disagreements and practical experience, and then add their own records to it.
