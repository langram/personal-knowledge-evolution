# New Principles: Modern Science and the Making of Human Civilization

## Preface: Rethinking Mathematics, Science, and Civilization

Let me be candid: the echo of Newton's *Principia* in this book's title is deliberate.

In an age of information overload, attention is scarce. A title that makes people stop and look can give an argument the chance to be read. That is part of my choice, but the more important reason lies in the subject itself.

Knowledge grows out of questions. At first, a question may seem quite small. Why does the same task keep going wrong? Why does a piece of software repeat work it has already done? Why do knowledgeable, capable people sometimes obstruct one another as soon as they form an organization? Follow such questions far enough, and difficulties that appeared to belong to separate fields begin to reveal similar structures.

How can one person's knowledge become something others can build on? How do simple components combine to acquire new capabilities? How can a structure survive over time without losing its ability to change? These questions arise in mathematics and natural science, in software and artificial intelligence, and in the organization of human society. I want to bring them together and look for the more basic patterns they may share.

### Beginning with the Relationship Between Mathematics and Science

Newtonian physics stands as one of the great landmarks in the history of science. With new methods of inquiry, powerful mathematical ideas, and rigorous reasoning, Newton built a coherent account of physical motion from a small set of laws. It described the world more accurately, and at a higher level of abstraction, than the natural philosophy it displaced. It helped establish the modern scientific enterprise.

His *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* became known simply as the *Principia*: the Principles. The shorthand almost suggests that the book contains the principles of everything. It does not. General relativity later showed why Newton's framework remains an extraordinarily useful approximation within conditions such as weak gravity and ordinary macroscopic speeds.

For several centuries, science has advanced through correction, extension, and reconstruction, becoming an immense force in human development. That inquiry has no final stopping point. Even an unsuccessful attempt can contribute, if we can learn from it. This book is one such attempt. I examine concepts and patterns that recur across scientific disciplines, look for their mathematical counterparts, and ask whether a more basic abstraction could help us reconstruct parts of our knowledge in a simpler, more unified way. If Newton's *Principia* arose from a reconstruction of natural philosophy, *New Principles* names the direction of an attempted reconstruction of mathematics and modern science.

There is also a more specific continuity. My inquiry begins with Newton's methodological Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy and with the idea of a limit, fundamental to the mathematics of continuous change. I ask whether these starting points lead toward a more general abstraction.

Newton's concern with causes, similar phenomena, and empirical generalization has something in common with the demand for simplicity associated with Occam's razor. If we want to take “do not multiply entities unnecessarily” seriously, one promising direction is to clarify the relationship between mathematics and science and place them within a shared framework. In that sense, a more precise title for this undertaking might be *Unifying Principles of Mathematics and Science*.

Mathematics and science are often treated as different kinds of knowledge. Because mathematics is abstract, it has long been regarded as a tool for scientific inference rather than simply another empirical science, although it is also classified among the formal sciences. The difference in their foundations is striking. Much of modern mathematics begins with specified axioms and derives theorems by rigorous inference. Empirical science begins with proposed laws about the world. Mathematical reasoning can establish what follows from those laws, but observation must test whether the resulting predictions actually fit reality. A theory may be logically impeccable and still require revision, or replacement, because the world behaves differently.

Any undertaking of the kind proposed here must address that relationship. We cannot claim to unify the two while leaving their connection unexplained. Yet their intimacy is already visible in every physical or chemical formula. A measured quantity joins a number to a physical unit: 3.2 milliseconds, 1.8 kilograms, 1,000 kilometers. Operators such as addition and division, and dimensionless constants such as π, e, or the golden ratio, belong to mathematics. Together these elements describe real regularities. Their integration makes it hard for me to accept a clean division between mathematics and the world without asking how the two fit together.

Consider the number three. On a page it is a number. Three seconds, three meters, and three kilograms refer to different measurement procedures. We can calculate with numbers, but we cannot simply add three meters to three seconds. To interpret a mathematical relation as a law of nature, we must specify the objects, the measurements, the changes being compared, and the conditions under which the correspondence holds.

The movement also goes the other way. Once we abstract a shared relation from particular objects, we can use the same mathematics elsewhere. We do not need to invent a new addition for every apple and every stone. The ability to set aside particular content while retaining structure is one reason knowledge can accumulate across objects and disciplines.

The history of mathematics and science shows this exchange repeatedly. Scientific problems stimulate new mathematics; new mathematical tools make scientific problems tractable. Newton's development of calculus in the 1660s supplied mathematics for continuous change that would help make the *Principia* possible two decades later. Einstein drew on Riemannian geometry in developing general relativity. Research programs such as string theory likewise rely heavily on mathematical constructions. Some of the physical properties these theories describe lie far beyond unaided human perception and may be difficult even to measure with instruments. We approach them through the properties of abstract structures. Eugene Wigner's phrase, “the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences,” captures the puzzle: what connects an abstract mathematical property to a physical property of the world?

Major theoretical reconstructions have brought previously separate accounts into a common framework. Maxwell united electricity and magnetism; Newton brought celestial motion and motion on Earth under the same laws. My proposal is more exploratory: to seek a framework in which mathematics and science can both be expressed clearly, so that questions about the mathematical principles of science and the scientific meaning of mathematical concepts become questions about the correspondence between abstract structures and concrete objects.

That correspondence has to be examined in both directions. We must ask how concrete objects give rise to abstractions, and under what conditions an abstract structure can have a concrete realization. Part of this book therefore approaches the relationship from the less familiar direction: the scientific meaning of mathematical concepts.

### Why Step Outside a Familiar Framework?

Imagine a novice moving a queen directly into an opponent's attack. It looks like a blunder, and usually it is. Yet a great player may deliberately sacrifice a powerful piece to transform a tangled position and secure a later victory. Before the sacrifice, the move can be difficult to imagine. Afterward, the position may become clearer and the continuation almost natural.

Something similar can happen in a complex system trapped at a local optimum. Two players become absorbed in a contest over a particular position, each convinced that winning it means winning the game. Neither wants to concede. But the best outcome within that local contest may not be the best outcome available on the board. A deeper judgment may require accepting a temporary loss in order to reach a better overall position.

A sacrifice, of course, is not intelligent merely because it is a sacrifice. Defying common sense does not automatically produce knowledge. The real question is whether relinquishing an assumption allows us to explain something previously unexplained, construct something previously unattainable, or achieve better results in practice.

Nor does the possibility of a better theory erase the value of an existing one. Relativity did not make Newtonian mechanics useless; engineering still relies on it. A worthwhile new framework should explain why the older one works, where it works, and why it encounters limits elsewhere. Reconstruction should expand our capabilities by building on what we have learned.

Before developing a precise account, we can begin with an informed guess about the overall picture. Precision will require appropriate rules and mathematical forms. Finding those forms among the many branches of modern mathematics is no small task. My intuition points toward a field that deserves particular attention: category theory.

Category theory can be understood as a language for mathematics; mathematics supplies languages for science; science describes the universe. This suggests that category theory might offer a structural language for relating different levels of knowledge and revisiting complex problems through their more basic relationships.

A view from above reveals how roads connect, but it cannot replace traveling them. Describing two things in the same abstract language does not establish that they obey the same concrete law. We need both movements: downward, to identify structures and conditions; upward, to discover what those structures can do when combined.

### From Understanding the World to Building Civilization Together

Following this line of inquiry brings us to general intelligence and the organization of society. It also leads to practical proposals for rethinking human and computer languages, software, and political and economic institutions. I hope these proposals can help reduce conflict and separation, and suggest productive directions for civilization's further development.

The subjects range from category theory through computer science and AI to several actual software projects. Across them, I try to reason from first principles, taking existing arrangements apart conceptually and asking how they might be reconstructed.

Knowledge can be shared by many individuals, but the organizations that create and use it vary enormously. A biological brain acquires its capabilities through connections and feedback among many neurons. No single neuron holds permanent personal authority over the whole. Human organizations, by contrast, often arrange social networks into relatively fixed pyramids. Resources, power, and decision-making influence depend heavily on a person's position. An exceptional entrepreneur or political leader may lose some cognitive capacity with age, while retaining authority that does not readily pass to someone now better able to exercise it. Repeated interaction under unequal power and resource conditions can also produce waste and poor allocation.

Several pieces would be needed to turn an alternative into practice. A Merkle-DAG framework could preserve the evolving “DNA” of human knowledge. AI-assisted personal tools could use a large model for unfamiliar work—the cold path—then turn useful results into reusable procedures—the hot path. Such tools could become the means by which a person and their AI, together functioning as a social neuron, receive information and act on the world. In the analogy, they serve as synapses.

But stronger tools alone will not make human relationships more equal. Knowledge may remain trapped in disconnected silos. Individuals may be forced to rent access to memories held by someone else. More capable intelligence may still be controlled by a handful of organizations. We must examine how knowledge is preserved, how capabilities are combined, and how the organizations producing and using them operate.

Theory and practice therefore cannot remain separate indefinitely. We can begin with one person, reorganizing their records, ideas, code, and tools. Then we can connect the knowledge and capabilities of a few people. From there, we can test whether this organization accomplishes more complex tasks at lower cost and improves participants' lives. Concrete constructions will keep supplying new answers to the abstract questions.

I will explain the concepts in accessible language, using familiar examples where the ideas become abstract. The argument should proceed step by step, with premises and connections visible enough for readers to judge why a conclusion follows and where further inquiry is needed. My aim is a book that a university-educated reader can follow without specialist training in every subject it touches.

The framework is far from complete, much less formally proved. It is a blueprint for a thought experiment and an invitation to think together. Its analogies and inferences call for closer examination. If they prompt a useful question, challenge an established assumption, or offer someone a starting point for their own inquiry, they will already have served a purpose.

A truly comprehensive theory would require the work of many minds: mathematical formulation, physical experimentation, computational simulation, and the human insight of philosophy and social inquiry. I hope others will help turn these proposals into knowledge and practices that we can understand, test, and build together.

Let us begin.

## Limits, Paradoxes, and the Boundaries of Understanding

A theory that seeks to connect scientific knowledge needs mathematics capable of expressing objects, relationships, and reasoning clearly. Modern mathematics offers many possible starting points. My intuition directs me toward category theory. Intuition can choose an entrance; what matters next is what becomes visible through it.

One of mathematics' central activities is to identify what different things have in common, give that commonality a name, define its properties, and investigate their relationships. Category theory applies this approach to mathematical structures themselves. It takes concepts that arise in separate branches and studies their shared features under common names.

Sets, groups, vector spaces, and topological spaces can all be treated as **objects**. Functions, group homomorphisms, linear maps, and continuous maps become **morphisms**. In each setting, we can ask how transformations compose, how composition associates, and what counts as an identity transformation. We also encounter related constructions such as limits, colimits, and universal properties.

If these terms are unfamiliar, begin with an ordinary sequence of operations. We turn one representation into another, then the second into a third. We want to understand how the combined operation takes us from the first to the third. The transformations differ across fields, but their ability to compose is something we can study in its own right.

Abstraction does not erase the differences. In a transport network, we can study how roads connect before asking which vehicles they accommodate. A faster vehicle cannot repair a missing connection. Conversely, identical connectivity does not imply identical road surfaces, capacity, or cost. Abstraction lets us examine a family of problems together while keeping room for these distinctions.

Stepping back can also reveal patterns concealed by detail. Mountains look very different up close; from farther away, we notice how the same mountain changes its apparent shape with our viewpoint. Su Shi expressed the predicament in a famous poem:

> Across it, a ridge; from the side, a peak—
> different from near and far, above and below.
> I cannot see Mount Lu's true form,
> because I stand within the mountain.

Category theory offers something like this wider view. A common structural language can reveal features missed when inquiry stays too close to a particular instance. Yet distance has its cost: details disappear. A useful framework must let us move back and forth, extracting shared relationships and then restoring them to concrete problems to see what conditions we have left out.

Let us start with a question anyone can picture: how does someone finish walking down a road?

### Must We Complete Infinitely Many Tasks to Finish a Journey?

Zeno's paradoxes—including Achilles and the tortoise and the motionless arrow—draw attention to the relationship between motion and our descriptions of its parts. In the dichotomy paradox, a traveler going from A to B must first cover half the distance, then half the remaining distance, and so on. There is always another half. How can the traveler ever arrive?

The question of whether 0.999… equals 1 offers another way into infinite processes. In the real numbers, limits give a precise account of convergence. The terms of a sequence may keep changing while approaching a definite value. Infinitely many subdivisions do not necessarily require infinitely much time. Limits helped make it possible to describe continuous change through numerical operations, opening the way to calculus and the mathematical treatment of complex motion.

At a speed of one meter per second, half a meter takes half a second, a quarter takes a quarter, and an eighth takes an eighth. The list of segments never ends, but the total time approaches one second. Dividing the route infinitely often has not imposed a fixed extra waiting period on each segment.

Similarly, 0.9, 0.99, and 0.999 are distinct finite decimals, each smaller than 1. The notation 0.999… denotes the real number determined by the entire infinite decimal: the limit of its truncations. In the usual real number system, that number is 1. There is no final nine, and no positive remainder concealed beyond every finite decimal place.

But if a difference remains at every step, a reader might ask, why does it disappear “after infinity”? The phrase is the source of the confusion. Infinity is not an exceptionally distant next step in the sequence. A limit describes a property of the whole approach; it is not the last finite operation.

Zeno's puzzle also invites a broader question about understanding. How does a process in the world relate to the divisions we make in describing it? Perception and thought distinguish concepts and steps. Measurements often leave finite, discrete records, while our models may represent continuous change. We need to explain the relationship between those descriptions in each particular problem.

### In What Sense Are Two Things the Same?

A movement and its endpoint are different kinds of objects, as are a program and its result. Rules can connect them without making them identical in every respect.

In mathematics, sameness depends on the structure we retain and the transformations we allow. Picture a clay cup with a handle and a clay ring. If we care about a single through-hole and allow continuous deformation without tearing or gluing, we can set aside their everyday uses. If we want to drink from the object, those omitted differences suddenly matter.

Mathematics distinguishes isomorphism, homeomorphism, homotopy equivalence, and other notions precisely. Homotopy type theory supplies rich tools for studying equality, paths, and higher structures. None of this licenses replacing “a sequence has a limit” with “the sequence is homotopy equivalent to its limit point.” Each relation requires appropriate objects and conditions. [*Homotopy Type Theory*](https://homotopytypetheory.org/book/)

What interests me is the trade-off between retaining and omitting structure. Treating many internal changes as one stable object gives us a simpler description. If we later need to investigate its mechanism, however, we must be able to reopen what was compressed. Abstraction helps us advance, but it can also make us forget what we set aside.

### What Happens When a System Describes Itself?

Consider Russell's paradox: the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Does that set contain itself?

If it does, it violates the condition for membership. If it does not, it satisfies the condition and should be included. Both answers collide with the defining rule.

The difficulty is not a shortage of intelligence. We need clear conditions governing what objects may be constructed and how we may speak about them. We cannot allow every unrestricted condition to generate a set and then demand that the resulting system never encounter a contradiction.

Ordinary language offers a similar intuition in “This sentence is false.” Treating it as true appears to make it false; treating it as false seems to support the opposite answer. But writing a program that alternates between true and false produces an alternating process. It does not thereby produce quantum superposition or resolve the logical issue.

Self-reference matters because it exposes boundaries in what a system can express, construct, and prove. Lawvere's work on diagonal arguments places several problems of self-reference in logic and computation within a shared categorical framework. I take from this a reason to investigate the structures behind the limits, instead of treating each famous paradox as an isolated curiosity. [Lawvere on diagonal arguments and Cartesian closed categories](https://www.tac.mta.ca/tac/reprints/articles/15/tr15abs.html)

Gödel's incompleteness theorems suggest a further question to me. Could we study a system whose capacity to extend itself is part of its design? New stable structures would emerge, interact with existing ones, and introduce new mathematical properties. The system would search for simpler constructions while exploring more complex structures, with new fixed points interacting with older ones. Might this support research into new axioms and construction rules—perhaps a dynamic theory of evolving categories of indefinitely higher order?

Such a proposal does not repeal incompleteness. A theory capable of continued extension is different from a fixed theory that has already proved everything. Each extension brings new questions about what its rules can and cannot do. Calling a level “higher” does not exempt it from boundaries of its own.

The productive question is whether, when the current structure cannot answer something, we can record what is missing, construct new tools, and preserve the relationships between the old and new structures. A boundary can then become an entrance to further inquiry as well as a stopping point.

### Finite Observers in an Expanding Space of Possibilities

I also want to consider scale relative to constructive capacity. A space may be mathematically finite while vastly exceeding what a particular finite system can examine item by item. The power set of a finite set remains finite, but its size can grow very quickly. I use “finite infinity” as a metaphor for this gap between a finite construction space and an observer's practical ability to explore it.

A set of ten elements has 1,024 subsets. The number grows exponentially with the number of elements. Everything remains finite, yet being able to state the construction rule is very different from being able to enumerate its results. A practical account of knowledge and intelligence cannot ignore that difference.

The framework I seek must therefore accommodate stable objects, change, construction, and the conditions under which observers can handle these processes. It should explain both how we compress a complex world into intelligible concepts and how those concepts return to the world as tools for creating something new. These questions bring existence, change, and constructibility to the center of the inquiry.

## Existence, Change, and Constructibility

When we say, “There is something here,” we already assume two things: we can distinguish it from its surroundings, and we can still recognize it after some change. Stability does not mean that nothing happens inside. A person keeps breathing, eating, and learning; the material of their body changes, yet we continue to regard them as the same person.

Without relationships that persist, we could not speak of an object at all. Without change, we could not investigate how it interacts with other objects. Existence and change are therefore two ways of approaching the same process.

### Stable Existence and Stable Construction

What conditions allow a complex structure to persist and continue evolving? Without recognizable existence, there is nothing definite to identify. Without change, a structure cannot develop or reveal itself through interaction. Our knowledge of properties depends on perception, measurement, and effects we can distinguish. If a purported property never makes an observable difference, observation cannot tell it apart. If light's intensity and wavelength never varied, for instance, our eyes could not use those differences to distinguish brightness and color, and the corresponding concepts would not develop in the same way.

Persistence and regularity of change together make stable structures possible. Stable structures, in turn, allow simpler components to form more complex ones without the construction immediately falling apart. Repeatable structures and processes provide conditions for repeatable observations. Those observations help us form stable concepts and regularities—the material from which mathematical and scientific theories are built.

Three ideas organize this inquiry:

- **Existence:** an object must have enough stability for us to identify what we are studying.
- **Change:** interaction and transformation allow structures to develop and become observable. Morphisms offer one mathematical language for examining such relationships.
- **Constructibility:** we must investigate the stable conditions under which objects and transformations combine. Without them, construction cannot readily accumulate.

A pile of unrelated material is not yet a building set. Each piece needs a sufficiently stable shape; joining pieces requires relationships they can sustain; the result must hold together. Better still, a completed small structure should be usable as a unit in the next construction. The builder then need not begin again from the smallest components every time.

Constructibility asks more than whether two things can meet. What forms when they do? Can it persist? Can it participate in further construction? These are the conditions under which development can build layer upon layer.

### Could Objects and Transformations Share a More Basic Concept?

This leads me to ask whether the basic concepts of category theory could be organized differently. An ordinary definition of a category specifies **objects**, **morphisms**, and their relationships. Could we instead begin with a component that expresses existence and change together, then explain how those two perspectives emerge?

Duality is pervasive in category theory. Reversing arrows yields dual formulations; limits and colimits, products and coproducts provide familiar examples. This recurring symmetry suggests to me the possibility of a deeper organizing principle. Objects can be associated with existence and morphisms with change, while existence and change form an ancient philosophical pairing. That association is a proposal to investigate, not an identification already established by categorical duality.

I provisionally call the proposed basic component a **Dualoid**. The aim is to express objects and morphisms through a more fundamental duality, and then build higher concepts from it. As a philosophical image, one might think of yin and yang arising from the Dao.

At the outset, I do not specify a particular physical material from which this component must be made. I propose approaching it through its relations to other objects, including objects derived from it, rather than assuming an internal structure in advance. This is reminiscent in spirit of the Yoneda perspective: under precise mathematical conditions, an object can be characterized through a coherent system of relationships with other objects. Existence, change, and constructibility would be basic attributes to investigate in the proposed definition.

A new name does not establish a new mathematical foundation. “Dualoid” earns its place only if it helps us specify which constructions are allowed, what relationships must be preserved, how composition works, and when the result remains an object of the required kind.

A computational unit offers an accessible example. As a program stored in a file, it can be read, copied, and compared. When executed, it accepts input and produces output. One view emphasizes what it is; another emphasizes what it does. Connecting them raises useful questions. Can different internal implementations provide the same external function? Can the same internal structure behave differently in different environments?

Hardware and software modules both have internal structures and external functions. Their structure, code, and data can be preserved as particular versions; their changing runtime state requires a further description. In operation, a module transforms data and can compose with other modules when interfaces and conditions match. Relationships among category theory, certain typed lambda calculi, and logic provide tools worth exploring here. I want to use them to investigate the data–code duality of computational modules, and eventually the relationship between propositions and inference rules in human reasoning.

### Internal Structure and External Relationships

Different physical systems may supply components that play comparable organizational roles: atoms in material structures, base pairs in DNA, cells in organisms, neurons in brains, and people in societies. They maintain states, change with time and environment, and participate in relationships that can be transmitted or combined. As simple rules generate complex patterns in Conway's Game of Life, I hope the study of such conditions can illuminate how successive levels of organization arise.

Calling them comparable components refers to their roles in an inquiry. A cell is not a person, and a person is not a program. Yet when studying the emergence of a higher level, we can ask in each case how components maintain themselves, receive and exert influence, and establish relatively stable connections.

Without this distinction between levels, abstraction becomes the empty assertion that everything is connected and therefore everything is the same. We need to identify which relationships compose, what their composition preserves, and what new properties require study at the higher level. A shared language should make these differences easier to express.

The properties of components may themselves change as a dynamic system constructs more complex structures. Some may become more stable; possible connections may multiply; successful combinations may become easier to achieve. Whether these changes occur together is an empirical question for each system. In biological evolution, DNA supplies concrete mechanisms of replication, variation, and recombination, making both large populations and diversity possible. Different structures evolve concurrently under interaction and competition; those able to persist and reproduce in their environments can continue. Greater complexity is one possible outcome, not an automatic advantage. Darwinian evolution examines how living systems change through these processes. My broader ambition is to investigate whether a categorical account of construction can help us relate biological evolution to other forms of change in the universe.

### Accommodating Uncertainty Without Abandoning Rules

The Dualoid proposal seeks to make self-reference, indeterminacy, and the formation of structure subjects of inquiry, rather than treating a boundary as a reason to stop asking questions. An initial intuition is to distinguish two situations:

- A self-description can be sustained. A statement such as “I exist,” when compatible with the construction rules, may participate in a stable, repeatably recognizable relationship.
- A self-description cannot be stably decided under the current rules. Something like “I am not myself” might lead to a cycle, a contradiction, or an undefined expression. These outcomes must be distinguished; calling all of them a superposition does not explain them.

The first situation invites the study of stable relationships. The second invites examination of a system's limits and possible extensions. Clear boundaries can distinguish an unsettled internal process from external relationships that are already well defined. The next task is to formulate constructions that actually yield new levels or structures, and demonstrate what explanatory or constructive power they add.

The larger proposal is to treat duality as a candidate foundational concept and use it to explore connections between ontology and epistemology, reduction and emergence. Its value would lie in better tools for analyzing and constructing complex systems.

The research process provides a practical analogy. A module may have several competing internal implementations while still offering a clear external interface. It can state what it currently guarantees, what remains unknown, and under which conditions its functions can be used. Work around it need not lose all structure simply because its internals continue to evolve.

Stability, on this view, need not end evolution. It can make the next level of construction possible. Change need not merely destroy stability; it can produce new stable relationships. To develop that thought, we must examine fixed points, minimization, maximization, and emergence.

## Fixed Points, Minimization, Maximization, and Emergence

Physics, chemistry, and biology connect in part because lower-level laws do not cease to apply at higher levels. Chemistry does not violate physics; biology does not violate chemistry. New organization introduces new properties and relationships that require study at the appropriate level. We can extend this perspective to consciousness and psychology, to relationships among organisms, and to human cooperation. My question is whether the relationships among fixed points, minimization, and emergence can help us understand the connections across this range—from basic physical structures through life, psychology, game theory, economics, and politics.

Such an inquiry should not discard everything those disciplines have discovered and leave us with “it is all ultimately physics.” We need to explain how lower-level conditions permit higher-level structures and how those structures acquire relationships that must be described at their own level.

A factory consists of matter, but an inventory of its atoms does not tell us what it can produce. The connections between machines, the order of operations, and the conversion of particular inputs into outputs belong to its organization. Explaining them means opening up the process of construction.

### Fixed with Respect to What?

I propose investigating fixed points and emergence as static and dynamic perspectives on construction. A fixed point directs attention to what remains unchanged under a specified transformation. Emergence directs attention to the formation of new structures. Minimization, together with the apparently opposing idea of maximization, may describe related tendencies in the maintenance and transformation of those structures. Their precise relationship remains to be formulated.

Mathematically, if a transformation is called f and an object x satisfies f(x) = x, then x is a fixed point of f. The crucial qualification is *of f*. An object can remain unchanged under one operation and change under another.

Sort a list according to a specified rule. Sort the resulting list again, and it stays the same. The procedure may pass through many intermediate states before reaching a result stable under that operation. Add a new member, however, and the input has changed; a different result is to be expected.

Applied to complex systems, this perspective asks which relationships persist through repeated interaction and which changes create new relationships. A long-lived organization can replace its members; a living body can exchange its material. To explain their stability, we must identify what is preserved and the processes that preserve it.

### How Can Minimization and Maximization Occur Together?

I call this research proposal the **fixed-point–minimization/maximization–emergence principle**. Consider a class of evolving systems that repeatedly form stable structures. A structure might simplify its internal organization or reduce its maintenance cost while preserving its function. At the same time, it might expand its capacity to establish useful connections with other objects. Those connections create more possible routes to new structures. From a higher-level perspective, a new object may perform an existing function more economically; from below, that simplification may be explained by a reorganization of connections among components. I want to investigate when these processes reinforce one another and when they conflict.

Science provides an intuitive example. A theory that explains more phenomena through fewer basic relationships may increase our ability to intervene in the world and construct things that did not exist before. The new instruments and technologies can then advance fundamental research, continuing the cycle.

A tool that becomes lighter and easier to maintain while performing a wider range of useful tasks illustrates both directions. The burden of sustaining and using it falls, while its effective reach grows. This does not mean every component must shrink. Supporting additional functions may require more complex internals. The comparison concerns the whole construction, its purpose, and its cost.

Nor are more connections automatically better. An expensive, unreliable, or useless connection may burden the system. “Maximization” must specify what is being maximized: useful possibilities for construction, attainable functions, or some defined measure. Treating every increase as an improvement would leave us unable to explain why systems sometimes benefit from removing connections, reducing dependencies, or maintaining boundaries.

Action and response are coupled: one object's action becomes an influence on another, while its own behavior responds to influences elsewhere. Freedom and constraint, power and obligation offer further pairs whose relationships invite study. I am therefore interested in objectives that couple an individual to its environment, rather than evaluating either in isolation.

Perhaps the appropriate account is not a static objective function at all, but an evolving process of interaction in which the objectives themselves change. That possibility matters. A single score cannot permanently rank the universe, every living system, and every human life. New knowledge and capabilities change both the available choices and what participants seek. An account of evolution must preserve those changes too.

### Is the Shortest Description Also the Cheapest Construction?

To reconsider Occam's razor from a constructive perspective, take three elements: an intelligence capable of inference and computation, a phenomenon to explain, and a theory that explains it. We can represent them abstractly:

| Starting point | Computational representation |
|---|---|
| A reasoning intelligence | A Turing machine T |
| A theory explaining a phenomenon | A program P for T |
| The phenomenon to be explained | An output string D |

Under this model, the shortest explanation becomes the shortest program P that outputs D and halts on the chosen universal machine T. The length of that program defines **Kolmogorov complexity** relative to the chosen description system.

Digital records make the distinction intuitive. Two strings may each contain a thousand characters, yet one consists entirely of zeros and the other has no usable pattern. The first can be generated by a short instruction to print a zero a thousand times. The second may require something close to recording every character. Program length captures a kind of descriptive complexity that raw file length misses.

This is a static measure once the computational model is fixed. A working self-extracting program provides an upper bound on description length, not proof that the shortest description has been found. Real objects also have histories of construction. Computational complexity studies costs such as the number of steps and the memory required to produce a result. Parallel processors can reduce elapsed time for some tasks, but the additional processors, memory, and coordination must also be counted.

The mathematical definition of a shortest program does not supply a general procedure for finding it. Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable in general. We can compare descriptions and constructions we have actually found, without declaring the latest improvement an absolute optimum. [Vitányi on the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity](https://homepages.cwi.nl/~paulv/papers/incomp.pdf)

Imagine a very short program that searches exhaustively for an immense length of time, and a longer program that uses a prepared structure to return the same answer immediately. The first wins on code length; the second may win on response time. If the task is repeated, the preparation cost can be spread over later uses. Evaluating construction therefore requires a time horizon.

Physical implementation adds another layer. Real computation and storage consume resources: processor activity, data movement, storage devices, and the environment needed to sustain operation. Fewer characters or graph nodes do not translate directly into a known energy saving. Access frequency, hardware, implementation, and operating conditions intervene.

My aim is to study those costs together, not substitute an easily counted quantity for harder measurements. Minimization becomes operational when we compare the complete processes required to deliver equivalent functions under comparable conditions.

### Bringing a General Principle into Particular Disciplines

Unlike a theory confined to a particular physical domain, this proposal is a framework for inquiry across natural and social sciences. It first needs to establish correspondences between their objects and relationships, then define measures appropriate to each. The fixed-point–minimization/maximization–emergence idea should ultimately be expressed with mathematical clarity.

Applying a general framework to a discipline would resemble implementing a generic interface: the abstraction needs a concrete realization. Existing principles concerning extrema and stability may offer starting points for those correspondences.

But does the framework improve research? In evolution, for example, can measures of a mutation's effects on the energy efficiency of survival, or on an organism's flexibility in interacting with its environment, improve predictions of persistence? That requires comparison with existing accounts. In institutional reform, can explicit models, simulations, and feedback about people's lives and relationships predict outcomes more usefully than political slogans and debate alone? Again, the answer must come from results.

The HotPath project offers a possible practical setting. Benchmarks can guide the reduction of unnecessary paths and nodes, while Merkle-DAG structures may support richer combinations of reusable objects. If the project eventually develops into a viable alternative software ecosystem, it would supply concrete evidence relevant to this direction. A later social network with more equal standing and adaptable decision-making influence would offer a much broader domain of inquiry.

Two disciplines are needed here. The absence of a complete formula for every field should not prohibit investigation of shared structures. Equally, an improvement in one software task cannot count as proof of every physical and social implication. Each application must say which part of the proposal it tests.

We should begin with constructions that can be described clearly and compared repeatedly, give the abstract terms concrete meanings, and bring both successes and failures back into the framework. Its unity should grow from relationships that can actually be connected.

## The Physical World and Nature's Successive Levels of Construction

A framework for construction should do more than redescribe what we already see. I want it to help explain why structures arise, why some last only a moment, and why others persist, reproduce, and become material for further construction.

The physical world is the most direct place to begin. It already offers theories with precise calculations and testable comparisons. A new framework has to engage with those relationships, rather than stand outside them and announce that everything obeys its principle.

### Why Can Building Blocks Build—and Where Do They Come From?

Lego bricks can form countless shapes, but a brick is not itself a primitive shape. Its studs and recesses are already complex structures. They enable interlocking, whereas many simpler shapes cannot readily form stable assemblies. The brick's constructive power depends on structures that were themselves constructed.

We can simplify the image further. Imagine a world of many small spheres, governed by rules that permit stable combinations. A combination acquires a new property; if that property supports a useful function, the structure may persist or be replicated. It can then serve as a component at the next level, where further combinations yield further properties. This thought experiment suggests a way to picture successive construction from basic physical structures to life and intelligence. Its starting rules would have to support identifiable existence, change, and enough regularity for structures to persist.

The key is not the spherical shape. It is that the capacity to construct has a history. A brick connects because of its surface; that surface depends on material and manufacturing processes at a smaller scale. At the next level, an assembled component can become a building block in its own right.

We need not declare one shape the unchanging fundamental unit at every level. Instead, we can ask what serves as a unit here, which of its properties permit higher-level construction, and why lower-level details can temporarily be set aside.

Physical theories such as string theory define their own precise objects and relationships. A theoretical “string” is not an ordinary rope. Images of open and closed loops cannot replace derivations of particle properties. A complicated description may motivate a search for simplicity, but complexity alone does not prove that an object consists of some smaller entity we have already named.

### Why Are Some Components Especially Versatile?

Some components are particularly effective at supporting diverse structures. At the atomic level, carbon and silicon offer striking examples. Which features of bonding and structure make their constructive roles possible?

Why is carbon central to the emergence of carbon-based intelligent life, while silicon has become a major material for computers and AI hardware? Can we study these roles through the specific conditions under which an atom maintains its structure and establishes external relationships? At a much different level, this prompts a social question: might fair conditions for human development, together with limits on domination, create better conditions for complex cooperation?

Many levels separate carbon chemistry from society. Bonding rules are not social rules. But “How do components connect, and how can those connections combine further?” is a question we can ask at both levels. Chemistry requires accounts of bonds, environments, and reactions; social inquiry requires accounts of abilities, information, resources, and influence. The common question gains explanatory value only when supplied with the concrete conditions of each domain.

Constraint and freedom, stability and change may help us understand how minimization and maximization can be coupled. A shared foundation can also coexist with functional diversity. Carbon's versatile bonding supports a wide variety of organic functions. Many cells in a multicellular organism share much of their genetic information while performing different jobs. Likewise, fairer access to learning and participation through AI does not require identical human abilities. I want to investigate how a common foundation can preserve diverse strengths and enable them to complement one another across disciplines.

Software offers a simple illustration. Different programs can use the same basic interface without performing the same task. Common connection conditions make it easier to combine different functions. Shared foundations and functional diversity can support each other; “same” and “different” alone do not capture the relationship.

### How Can Order Arise While Entropy Increases?

Irreversible physical processes can coexist with the formation of ordered structures. We discuss increasing entropy while observing local complexity and organization.

There is no necessary conflict with the second law of thermodynamics. An open system can maintain local order through exchanges of matter and energy; an accounting of the full process must include the environment. Prigogine's work on dissipative structures examines how nonequilibrium conditions can support certain forms of dynamic order. [Prigogine, “Time, Structure, and Fluctuations”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17738519/)

This broadens what stability means. A stone mainly maintains its shape through its material and environmental conditions. Other structures depend on continuous flow. A flame may retain a recognizable form while fuel and air enter and heat and products leave. Stop the exchange, and the process ends.

Stability is not always stillness, and local order does not always mean continuously falling internal energy. We must specify the system boundary, exchange conditions, states being compared, and quantities being measured. Otherwise minimization and maximization become words that can be attached to any outcome after the fact.

### Does Greater Complexity Necessarily Accelerate Evolution?

We can treat the many influences on the formation of complex structures as different dimensions of a description, including time. Could a structure with richer connections support more parallel explorations and thereby accelerate discovery? In a complex society, for example, can people and organizations pursuing different directions find useful methods faster?

There are competing effects. More varied attempts expand the space explored, but attempts that cannot exchange results, or that interfere with one another, may merely add duplication and coordination costs. We need to study how diversity is preserved, useful outcomes spread, and failures become understandable to others.

This offers an important connection between natural and knowledge structures. Exploration accumulates when its results can become shared material for later work. An event that happens once and leaves nothing reusable has different consequences from a result that can be reproduced, understood, and combined.

### Making Large Questions Researchable

Constructing such a theory requires precise accounts of these relationships across levels, drawing on expertise far beyond one person. Within and between coherent disciplinary theories, we can investigate mathematical correspondences between a higher-level object or law and particular structures at a lower level. We can then ask how their accounts of change connect and whether any proposed common principle actually holds.

Can category theory express the fixed-point–minimization/maximization–emergence proposal? Could this line of work contribute tools for describing the evolution of mathematical models themselves? Mathematics would then be considered not only as a body of discovered regularities but also as a continuing practice of constructing new structures.

In disciplines with mature mathematical models, a reconstruction would need to preserve established successes while adding explanatory or constructive power. String theory, for instance, presents an enormous space of possibilities: what distinguishes candidates relevant to our world? Why do certain physical and chemical structures persist under particular conditions while others vanish almost immediately? Could the proposal help clarify any part of the tension between general relativity and quantum mechanics? These are research questions, not accomplishments implied by the framework's breadth.

A compelling analogy must not end the inquiry prematurely. A useful result should tell us more clearly which structures are compatible, which predictions distinguish competing proposals, or which calculations and constructions become easier. Where it does none of these things, the question remains open.

When structures can do more than persist and reproduce—when they can distinguish external conditions, select responses, and retain what they learn—another level of construction appears. Those capabilities provide the route into the next discussion of consciousness and intelligence.

## Consciousness, Information, and Self-Directed Intelligence

A structure's ability to preserve itself does not mean it understands its surroundings. A thermostat switches in response to temperature without thereby possessing human consciousness. Yet if we treat complex intelligence as an indivisible mystery that simply appears, we lose the means to investigate it.

I propose following a sequence of capacities: discrimination, response, memory, combination, and self-maintenance. We can ask how simpler capacities contribute to more complex ones without requiring every lower level to possess all the properties of the level above it.

### From Distinguishing Conditions to Acting on Them

One mathematical structure that interests me is the **subobject classifier**. A topos has rich internal logical structure and can support foundations for mathematics alternative to familiar set-theoretic formulations. A subobject classifier is an important part of that structure. Informally, it can be pictured as a general way of expressing truth values within the category. A subobject is characterized through a morphism into an object Ω, subject to the relevant pullback conditions. This makes it possible to express logic internally. I want to investigate how such mathematical accounts of distinction and logic can help describe actual information processing.

“Truth-value evaluator” is only an explanatory image. Ω, the terminal object 1, and the morphism true from 1 to Ω have distinct mathematical roles. Together they are not already a powered device that judges the physical world. A realization needs an account of input, state, energy, and action. [Fong and Spivak on subobject classifiers in applied category theory](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s097-applied-category-theory-january-iap-2019/a4175d61479a35340d6307ae5e48ef5a_18-s097iap19textbook.pdf)

Maxwell's demon is not a subobject classifier. But describing its distinction between faster and slower molecules requires some representation of the distinguishing condition. The imagined demon must also detect molecules, change its state, and control a gate. That is more than a classification relation. What additional structures would describe acting on a result, changing state, and retaining an effect?

The thought experiment brings acquiring information and using it into one process. The demon must keep operating, not merely open a gate once. A full accounting cannot include only the favorable result while excluding the controller and its memory. Bennett's work on the thermodynamics of computation gives particular attention to logically irreversible operations, including erasure, within complete computational cycles. [Bennett, “The Thermodynamics of Computation—A Review”](https://research.ibm.com/publications/the-thermodynamics-of-computation-a-review)

Intelligence likewise cannot be understood solely through its final answer. How it obtains information, what attracts its attention, how it retains experience, and how it receives the consequences of action belong to the same constructive problem.

### From Cellular Responses to Human Consciousness

Biologically, an account of human consciousness must engage with the combined activity of large numbers of neurons. In a language of layered construction, neurons and their connections would form part of the lower-level objects and relationships. Neuronal electrical responsiveness is a specialized form of cellular responsiveness. Other tissues and organs contribute their own processes. Signals from the body and environment interact with neural activity to support higher-level perception and action. We can investigate discrimination, response, and composition at levels ranging from organic molecules and cells to bodily systems and conscious activity.

A lower-level component that persists, changes, and participates reliably in construction may contribute its responsiveness to a higher-level information-processing system. Detection can make construction more selective and organized, rather than leaving every combination to chance.

The chain of explanation must actually be built: from distinguishable responses, to organized responses, to retained and usable experience. Each level requires new relationships that explain a new function. Renaming the earlier level does not accomplish that work.

A single signal may indicate that something changed somewhere. Several connected signals may indicate motion. With memory, the system may anticipate where the object will go next. Discrimination, connection, memory, and prediction provide more specific research questions than the search for a mysterious switch that turns consciousness on.

One particularly important distinction is between **the system itself and its environment**. A system that changes its responses according to that distinction can be studied as an observer, separating something like “self” from “not-self.” I want to investigate how such capacities relate to the human concept of “I,” and how they contribute to self-awareness and knowledge.

The question “Are there facts independent of an observer?” already invites us to consider an observer and something observable. If we presuppose an observer, is the observer's existence itself a fact? Could the observer's own existence be the first fact recognized in a self-referential account?

These questions do not require reducing the world to private experience. They require including observers in the world we study: how they form and change, how objects affect them, and how those effects become records and knowledge. Observation is itself a relationship that occurs in reality.

### Why Do Abstraction and Compression Matter for Understanding?

What is an intelligent system's intelligence? Why are vectors and matrices so useful in describing learning and inference? Human intellectual life developed language, writing, arithmetic, and equations. Even linguistic forms can be viewed through combinations of components: an initial consonant and a vowel-bearing final, for example, help specify a syllable. Coordinates such as (x, y) describe a location through a small number of components. These examples suggest a connection between abstraction and compression.

One important capacity of intelligence may be to decompose complex, high-dimensional structures into useful components, then recombine them in induction, inference, learning, and action. The search for better abstractions and compressions would be one aspect of that capacity.

Learning a word, however, is not merely deleting experiences and keeping a few characters. A useful abstraction helps us recognize relevant relationships in new situations and act on them. The concept of a cup groups differently shaped objects while helping us judge what will hold water and what may be unsuitable for hot water.

Compression that discards distinctions needed for a task damages understanding. An abstraction that preserves the right relationships lets limited memory support more combinations. Compression ratio therefore cannot be the sole objective. What can still be reconstructed, predicted, and built matters just as much.

### Why Self-Direction Requires More Than Waiting for Questions

One difference between a person and a passive tool is initiative. Code can describe a self-updating process, but the stored code is not the process running. Likewise, an AI that only responds when someone asks a question lacks, in that interaction, a continuing mechanism for directing its own activity.

In a conversation initiated by a person, the person supplies the initiative and the model responds. Both the system and the person retain some conversational context. But I also maintain another kind of context: an ongoing conversation with myself. It includes established knowledge from books, ideas that caught my attention in conversations, observations, remembered experiences, films, and other sources. Attention draws selected material into this continuing reflection. New material changes and compresses the context. That continuing internal conversation may offer a clue to self-awareness.

An AI architecture could experiment with a persistent context for reflection, selecting material from conversations and observations in light of its current questions, and initiating further inquiry. A problem encountered while reading would not disappear at the end of a chat. It could remain a thread to revisit when new evidence arrives. The system might connect the evidence, reassess a judgment, or propose an experiment capable of distinguishing alternatives. Input would then contribute to a continuing activity rather than trigger an isolated reply.

But talking to oneself is not the same as learning. A system that repeatedly feeds its own guesses back to itself may mistake repetition for evidence and become more certain of an error. Reflection must connect to observation, other perspectives, and the results of action. Initiative and feedback have to be designed together.

Rich connections may support new functions; sustained reflection may help a system examine its own limits and build new capabilities. A model that maintains such a context and uses tested experience in further learning might outperform some existing training arrangements. That is a question for concrete systems and comparisons.

This connects intelligence to the broader constructive framework. Stable memory lets experience accumulate. Discrimination and abstraction make it usable. Action and feedback let knowledge change. Communication with other intelligences expands the range of available experience.

Once several such individuals interact, new questions arise. How do they cooperate, handle conflicting judgments, and turn separate experiences into a shared capability? The connection between individual intelligence and social organization must be developed through those relationships.

## Cooperation, Conflict, and the Formation of Social Structures

Why do people cooperate? The obvious answer is that cooperation benefits them. But what if one person takes the benefit and fails to keep a promise? And if both parties prepare for betrayal before doing anything else, how does cooperation begin?

Even an established relationship may change. One person accumulates resources; another becomes increasingly dependent. What began as a negotiable arrangement can become a set of terms one party has little choice but to accept. To understand social organization, we must examine not only its rules at one moment, but also the relationships those rules produce through repeated interaction.

### From Atomic Bonds to Human Relationships

A chemical analogy can help frame the question. Atoms combine through bonds whose formation depends on their electronic properties, physical laws, environmental conditions, and interactions. In society, the basic participants are **people**, and one fundamental connection is **cooperation**, developed through repeated interactions involving their interests.

Game theory supplies two concepts that must be kept distinct. A **Nash equilibrium** is a profile of strategies from which no participant can benefit by changing strategy alone. A **Pareto-optimal** outcome is one in which no person can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

An equilibrium is not necessarily a good situation for everyone. Two people may distrust each other and spend heavily on defenses, neither daring to relax first. Their relationship can be stable while imposing losses on both. Stability describes persistence; judging whether the arrangement is desirable requires asking who gains, who loses, and what alternatives exist.

I therefore want to understand both how stable organizations form and how they retain pathways to better states. An arrangement that never changes may be extremely stable and unable to adapt.

Reciprocal responses can strengthen or weaken social connections. Cooperation receives cooperation; betrayal provokes a response. Repeated feedback helps produce structures of relationships. The analogy with chemical bonding concerns this formation of connections, not an identity between people and atoms. Different intensities of response may help explain variations within those structures. We observe relatively conciliatory and confrontational positions in families, companies, criminal organizations, parliaments, ruling committees, and political parties. Recurrence across levels makes the mechanism worth investigating.

The simplest tit-for-tat strategy begins by cooperating and then responds according to the other player's preceding action. Research on repeated games has made this simple strategy an important object of study. Its outcomes nevertheless depend on payoffs, opportunities for future interaction, mistakes, and the strategies of others. [Axelrod and Hamilton on the evolution of cooperation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7466396/)

Human relationships are richer than that minimal model. After a broken promise, one person stops cooperating immediately; another asks what happened. Some will resume cooperation after a single response, while others remain guarded for years. The relevant differences include the intensity, duration, and conditions for ending a punishment—not merely whether punishment occurs.

### How Do Conciliatory and Confrontational Tendencies Develop?

These tendencies need not be fixed, discrete categories. They can be understood as regions within a spectrum, much as named colors divide a continuous range of wavelengths.

We can pose the research questions in stages. Do differences in physiology and inherited traits affect the intensity or duration of reactions to betrayal? Do those reactions influence punishment or the time before cooperation resumes? Do they change the probability of adopting relatively hawkish or dovish responses? How do development and experience interact with them?

Several steps separate lower-level conditions from social behavior. One argument cannot establish that a person belongs to a permanent natural camp. Equally, the importance of environment does not establish that biological differences are irrelevant.

Research involving MAOA, for example, has examined associations between genotype and punitive responses to provocation under particular experimental conditions. Such findings make individual differences within interactions a researchable subject. They do not support genetic verdicts about a person, a social group, or a political system. [McDermott and colleagues' experimental study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2650118/)

For this framework, the task is to connect the levels: how a lower-level difference affects a response, how the response affects a strategy, and how an environment rewards or frustrates that strategy. The importance of a variable must be established within those relationships and conditions.

I suspect that differences in DNA can, through physiology and psychology, influence preferences among social strategies. I want that possible causal route investigated step by step. More generally, small differences at one level may be amplified through many layers of interaction and feedback. Understanding those pathways could reduce the temptation to explain conflict entirely through the language of angels and demons, good and evil—a language that can itself intensify conflict.

We associate red with joy, green with calm, or blue with sadness, but the corresponding wavelengths do not intrinsically possess those emotions. Likewise, violent behavior cannot be read directly from one low-level variable. Physiological traits, development, experience, and circumstances interact. A parameter should not be labeled morally good or bad in isolation. Different combinations can produce different higher-level outcomes; understanding their mechanisms creates possibilities for improvement.

### What a Model Leaves Out May Still Matter

The conclusions of Maynard Smith's hawk–dove model depend on its assumptions. When we use such a model to think about actual people, inherited traits, physiology, and experience remain potential influences on behavior. A model's failure to represent a factor explicitly does not establish that the factor has no effect in reality.

The converse is equally important. A factor's possible relevance does not mean it explains everything. Environments can amplify or restrain the same tendency. Models help by separating conditions and asking whether changing one changes the outcome.

Imagine two people prone to agitation when frustrated. One lives in an environment that rewards immediate retaliation; the other has reliable opportunities to clarify misunderstandings and repair cooperation. Their behavior may diverge over time. A purely genetic explanation would omit the environment; a purely institutional account might omit individual differences.

Repeated interaction adds another influence. In an environment with large, entrenched power gaps, repression may be cheap for those who command it. If harsh responses repeatedly succeed at little cost to the powerful, they have reason to keep using them. Even someone with a comparatively mild temperament may learn to rely on coercion.

In an environment that constrains arbitrary violence and provides effective communication and appeal, force may instead be costly and peaceful negotiation more successful. Even a person with intense emotional reactions may learn to use relatively measured responses.

Within either setting, differences can still appear around difficult or borderline cases. Broadly conciliatory participants may divide into harder and softer positions; even an authoritarian system can contain competing hawkish and dovish approaches. These possibilities are reasons to investigate the interaction of dispositions and institutions, rather than infer an entire social order from either alone.

### Why Does Success Change the Next Choice?

Suppose actors favor methods they perceive as less costly. In a strongly authoritarian system, a large power imbalance may make suppression seem cheap to those in command. Repeated success rewards both the method and those inclined to use it. Suppression creates resistance; resistance encourages rulers to widen the power gap in pursuit of stability; the wider gap makes further suppression easier. This simplified loop shows how local choices can reinforce a large and rigid structure.

But cheap for whom? An order may cost the issuer almost nothing while imposing enormous losses on those affected. An evaluation that counts only the issuer's costs will make the method look effective. Including the victims' losses, later resistance, and society's defensive expenditures may produce a very different comparison.

Persistence therefore does not establish shared benefit. Local success can make a path increasingly familiar, until alternatives become hard to imagine. An institutional alternative must change the organization of feedback, not merely replace the occupant of a powerful position.

Rewards and penalties can be viewed as an evaluative mechanism; repeated reciprocal responses apply that mechanism over time. Under suitable conditions, relationships strengthen or separate in patterned ways. The intensity of responses affects convergence, resilience, and stability. Too much rigidity can trap a system in a local optimum and undermine its capacity to evolve; too little stability can prevent sustained cooperation. Designing feedback that supports both persistence and change is a central problem for civilization.

### Why Preserving Differences Can Be More Powerful Than Eliminating Them

A civilization's capacity to evolve can be one measure without exhausting its value. A software benchmark assesses a program along one dimension; it does not express everything the program is for. Cats may be better hunters than mice, but this does not establish that cats have greater existential value. Nor is an unused structure necessarily worthless. Diversity supplies material for future combinations and emergence.

Energy efficiency can help compare strategies or institutions for particular tasks. Finding a more efficient option does not imply that all alternatives should be eliminated. Diversity can make development more robust, and opportunities to repair cooperation help preserve it.

Science fiction offers a familiar example: an enemy disables an advanced computer network, and an old radio becomes indispensable. The lesson is not to reject advanced technology, but to distinguish choosing a preferred implementation from erasing every other possibility.

An organization can use an efficient approach in ordinary conditions while retaining alternatives useful under unusual ones. Accepting different experiences and objections preserves material for recombination when circumstances change.

This includes ideas we dislike or do not yet understand. We should explain what an idea actually claims, rather than replace it with the easiest version to refute. Otherwise apparent debate merely reinforces existing positions.

### Understanding Conflict Does Not Mean Allowing Harm

When fear, anger, or incompatible assumptions block cooperation, a shared understanding of how people develop may help establish a more constructive starting point. Differences in appearance, physiology, temperament, knowledge, language, conduct, and needs emerge through processes that can be investigated. We should seek testable explanations of those processes instead of allowing fear of the unknown and anger from confrontation to harden into absolute categories of good and evil.

Through fuller communication and a verifiable, traceable knowledge framework, differences could become sources of diversity and development, rather than reasons for mutual obstruction or elimination. This is the role I envisage for a Merkle-based common knowledge system within cooperation.

Would explaining harmful conduct weaken protection for those harmed? The two tasks can support each other. An ongoing injury requires protection now. Preventing recurrence requires understanding how it arises. A society that responds only to each instance while leaving its generating conditions untouched will keep repeating the conflict.

My starting point is that explanation can open possibilities for change. Once a phenomenon is declared incomprehensible and unchangeably evil, destruction may seem the only response. Returning it to its conditions and feedback processes allows us to search for prevention that is more effective and less costly.

I sometimes describe this shift as treating crime like disease. The point is to approach harmful phenomena as problems of explanation, prevention, and correction. Destroying an individual without understanding the mechanism that produced the conduct does not remove that mechanism; new instances may follow. Scientific inquiry can help us investigate how to interrupt the cycle.

The analogy must not become a way to diagnose disagreement as illness or classify people as inherently unfit to belong. That would preserve the same rigid binary under new vocabulary. Understanding requires attention to particular behavior and circumstances.

The proposed social framework thus begins with difference, rather than waiting for people to become identical. It asks how differences can form richer relationships, how errors can receive useful feedback, and how a conflict need not destroy every possibility of renewed cooperation. This will require material conditions, knowledge tools, and practical capacities that allow more people to participate on equal terms.

## Machines, AI, and the Material Conditions of Equality

Moral persuasion alone cannot remove inequality. People differ in age, strength, knowledge, and ability. They grow up in different places, among different families and friends. A strong adult has advantages in many physical tasks. Someone raised with good schooling and a culture of learning has advantages in tasks that depend on education and professional training.

Since the Industrial Revolution, technology has repeatedly extended human abilities and compensated for limitations. When a tool performs a task better than an unaided person and becomes widely available, individual differences matter less for that task. Agricultural machinery reduces the effect of differences in physical strength. Navigation software reduces the disadvantage of unfamiliarity with roads. Autonomous driving may eventually reduce limitations associated with driving skill. As AI develops, broad access to it could similarly improve people's ability to obtain knowledge, understand problems, and participate in judgment.

My proposal depends on the knowledge behind such tools—their principles and the means of making them—being shared as a human common resource rather than exclusively controlled by a person, company, organization, or state. Otherwise, a tool that could improve everyone's development conditions and reduce unfair barriers may instead amplify one actor's advantages over others.

The institutional half of that argument is easily overlooked. The same machine can extend the capabilities of many people or the control of a few. Greater technical power does not answer who can use it, on what terms, or under whose authority.

Two people with navigation tools are less divided by their previous knowledge of a road network. But if one has the full map while the other receives only restricted directions, the disadvantage survives in a different form. The material conditions of equality include the distribution of knowledge, infrastructure, and rights of use, not merely the existence of an invention.

### Intellectual Work Can Have a Machine Revolution Too

Industrial machinery transformed many forms of physical work from individual craft into reproducible processes capable of high speed, quality, and volume. AI creates possibilities for a comparable change in intellectual work. Political, commercial, and educational organizations have often been built around hierarchies of intellectual authority. As parts of expertise become expressible and reproducible in algorithms, models, and procedures, their use need no longer remain tied to a particular authority figure. Reproduction still requires resources and appropriate conditions, but the organizational possibilities change.

A craftsperson's skill initially resides in their training, experience, and body. When repeatable operations are embodied in tools and production processes, others need not undergo the same long apprenticeship to obtain a similar result.

Something comparable applies to expert judgment. If a useful rule remains only in one person's mind, everyone must repeatedly consult that person. If its expressible, testable elements become shared models, algorithms, and knowledge, more people can build on them. The expert can continue making new contributions without retaining exclusive control over every capability already developed.

This does not mean all experience can now be extracted, or that a model's reply is equivalent to professional judgment. It identifies an organizational possibility: some capabilities formerly concentrated in individuals can become a shared foundation that others can test and improve.

### Equality Does Not Require Identical People

Information technology and AI allow us to investigate that possibility. Information suitable for shared use can become public knowledge, with tools for interpreting it. In a distributed system, each person could have an AI assistant that helps them reason beyond the limits of their prior education and experience.

A shared foundation of human knowledge could become available through personal AI, somewhat as many cells share genetic information while performing different functions. This offers a practical starting point for institutions with more equal standing and more adaptable decision-making influence.

Shared knowledge does not produce identical interests. One person loves mechanics, another art. One pursues a single problem deeply; another connects work across fields. Tools can reduce barriers arising from background, physical limitations, or education, while diverse interests enlarge the community's creative range.

Fair conditions for development therefore mean preventing lack of access from condemning someone to accept others' judgments indefinitely. Can a person without prior specialist training understand a decision that affects them, contribute an overlooked fact, learn, and join new work? These questions matter more directly than whether everyone's scores converge.

### Why Greater Productivity Still Requires Institutional Change

Automation has vastly increased material production and the technical capacity to meet basic needs. AI may similarly improve the productivity of intellectual work. I hope that continuing improvements in production and distribution can make material and intellectual resources sufficiently available to support people's lives, with attention to what actually improves their well-being.

Yet social networks often remain organized as relatively fixed pyramids. Resources and decision-making authority attach to positions and may not adjust when abilities or needs change. An aging entrepreneur or political leader can retain authority despite declining judgment. Unequal power can protect itself through repeated interaction, even while resources are poorly allocated.

The tension is that productive capabilities become easier to reproduce and share while organizational authority remains attached to fixed positions. Introducing new tools into an unchanged structure may simply distribute their benefits along existing lines of power.

We must therefore ask not only how much wealth AI can create, but also how it changes each person's ability to participate in production and judgment. If ordinary people supply data and bear disruption without being able to understand or influence the rules, technical progress has not automatically delivered institutional progress.

### If There Is Less Work, Will There Be Less Creation?

The question “What will people do when machines do the work?” often combines two different things: labor necessary to obtain a livelihood, and activity motivated by interest, responsibility, or the desire to create.

Reducing the first can free time for the second, provided institutions make that possible. If access to life's necessities remains tied exclusively to old jobs, fewer jobs become a threat. If basic needs are secure and tools open previously inaccessible forms of creation, the same technical change can create freedom.

Someone may want to make a film because they have a story to tell, not because the world needs another employment position. Cheaper production and editing tools make that expression more accessible. Scientific inquiry, community projects, games, art, and personal learning offer similar possibilities. Valuable creation is not limited to activities a company has already turned into a job.

Biological intelligence has created computational systems that perform many tasks more effectively than we can alone. Their combination may support greater shared creativity and further social development.

The question thus becomes how people and machines can form a more capable society. Accumulating those capabilities requires a knowledge environment that different people can understand, use, and extend. That brings us to language, public knowledge, and civilization's memory.

## Language, Common Knowledge, and Civilization's Memory

In one sense, human development is a history of communication tools. From speech and writing to computer languages, a deeper grasp of the units of information has repeatedly increased our ability to combine them, making higher-level systems more flexible and inventive.

Language functions as an input and output system. We encode knowledge and experience into streams of sound or written symbols; others interpret those streams through their own knowledge. We cannot transfer an entire brain. We express selected relationships through words, pictures, gestures, and other signs, and the recipient attempts to reconstruct them.

Communication therefore needs both distinctions and shared foundations. If a word changes its referent arbitrarily each time, knowledge cannot accumulate. If it can refer only to one object immediately before us, it has little room to express new experience. Language draws its power from rich combinations of a finite set of elements.

### Can We Name Something Before We Fully Understand It?

We often give a repeatably recognizable phenomenon a name before understanding its mechanism. We then investigate how it interacts with other things, infer possible internal structures, and use known relationships to intervene. If the resulting behavior matches a prediction, the account gains provisional support; if not, we try again. Continued agreement between intervention and observation strengthens our understanding.

Could an AI architecture support a comparable process explicitly, using tools from homotopy type theory and executable programs to organize continuing exploration? The aim would be to preserve a question and gradually enrich its relationships, rather than require a complete theory before giving it a place in the system.

A name does not prove an explanation. It gives researchers a stable point around which to exchange observations. Later correction need not erase those observations; it can specify which relationships have changed. Scientific concepts need enough stability to support collaboration and enough flexibility to keep developing.

### Common Knowledge Is More Than a File on the Internet

Laws, religious rules, political programs, shared moral expectations, and accepted rules of interaction can make cooperation more predictable. Private intentions or unwritten rules known only to an inner circle give others much less certainty. When participants know a rule and know that others know it, they have better grounds for coordinating their actions.

Imagine two people moving a heavy piece of furniture. Both knowing that it should go left is not enough. Each must know that the other is ready to move left, and when they will lift together. Otherwise one acts while the other waits.

The same distinction matters in larger organizations. Making a rule accessible does not tell us who has read it, who accepts it, or which version they are using. Publicly available information, individually known information, and mutually acknowledged common knowledge are different states. Recording those relationships could reduce the need to coordinate through guesswork.

I envisage storing suitable shared knowledge in a Merkle-DAG, with version history analogous to Git. Content can be checked against its known identifier, and recorded changes can be traced. Open materials would then provide a foundation for developing common knowledge: I know, you know, I know that you know, and so on. Participants can verify the versions they are discussing and establish agreement through communication and acknowledgment.

### Why Separate a Piece of Knowledge from Its Storage Location?

This suggests a shared symbolic framework and, potentially, a programming language in which code and data objects use identifiers derived from their content through hashing.

With suitable cryptographic hashes and encoding rules, a hash identifies the bytes we expect to receive. After retrieving content, we can recompute its hash and compare it with the expected value. Establishing who published it requires additional authentication or signatures. Content identifiers can also serve as lookup keys, while distributed hash tables can help locate copies across nodes. Scalability still depends on implementation and resources. Hash-linked structures can record revisions, much as Git does.

An ordinary URL primarily tells us where to look. A content identifier tells us what to verify. The same material can reside in several places without changing its identity. Changing the content should produce a new identity, with the relationship between versions recorded separately.

This suits accumulating knowledge. A conclusion may depend on a particular experimental record. If that record is corrected, we can still inspect the original basis; a revised conclusion points to the revised evidence. One overwritten address need no longer carry the incompatible meanings “always this content” and “whatever is newest.”

A hash does not settle truth, availability, or permission. A mistaken argument can have a perfectly stable identifier. Knowing an identifier does not ensure that anyone will provide the content. Private data do not become public merely because they have a hash. Validity, access, and adoption of a version require distinct relationships.

### From Links Between Pages to Links Within Knowledge

The Internet and the Web connected people and organizations through documents, media, programs, services, and protocols. They changed cooperation enormously. Yet important boundaries remain: platforms divide data and applications into silos, and large organizations retain concentrated control. I want to investigate whether a Merkle-based framework can take the granularity of shared objects and relationships down to meaningful semantic units.

A page may link to another page without saying which definition it uses, which result it depends on, or which qualified conclusion it disputes. If the whole page is the smallest accessible unit, each reader has to repeat the work of locating and reconstructing those relationships.

Finer units could make that work part of shared memory. A definition, argument, counterexample, or function could have a clear boundary, appear in multiple works, and retain its source. Different projects would no longer need to rebuild every layer of shared knowledge independently.

In the proposed ecosystem, intermediate data and internal functions could gradually become accessible through common mechanisms within appropriate permissions. Different implementations of a common function could be compared under explicit conditions, using tests and runtime measurements. Public accounts of functionality, conditions, and results could reduce duplication caused by opaque systems.

A new organization of existing technologies can itself be transformative. Networking hardware and software become more useful when shared protocols allow previously separate devices to cooperate. A comparable development might help connect individual AI agents and small networks into a much broader social intelligence network.

This is why I connect language to social organization. Shared symbols and verifiable relationships let personal experience enter collective construction. Collective construction gives more people tools to generate new experience. Civilization's memory should be more than a warehouse waiting to be queried; it should be knowledge that can continue participating in action.

We can begin with one person: preserving their discussions, writing, practical work, and revisions in a structure that keeps content, sources, and relationships together.

## The Semantic Space and Timeline of Personal Knowledge

I want to bring together local AI conversations from different computers, cloud chat histories, and my other writing in a Merkle-DAG: a record of the semantic space and evolving history of my ideas. Logical relationships, possible causal connections, and relations between broader and narrower concepts would support a mind-map-like view. Alongside it, a Git-like history would preserve when ideas appeared, how they changed, and what later versions inherited. The result would be a record of my knowledge developing through both conceptual relationships and time.

Saving chats alone is not enough. An idea may begin in one conversation, acquire an explanation in another, and change after an experiment fails. Files organized only by application, date, and folder preserve the material while leaving its connections buried.

Reducing everything to a few AI-generated conclusions creates the opposite problem. A fluent summary can hide the circumstances of the original claim, the reason for a correction, or an objection that remains unanswered. A personal knowledge system needs both the material and the process by which understanding develops.

### Meaning and Evolution Are Related but Distinct

Many training materials emphasize relatively static relationships, such as questions and answers. This resembles studying one checkout from a Git repository: the code and data of a particular version. I want to add relationships among stages of development, including changes to semantic objects rather than only textual differences. Even a familiar mathematical concept such as a limit has a history.

The proposal extends beyond mathematics. Knowledge materials could carry information about their development: the paths along which concepts changed and the relationships between successive formulations. “Distance” in such a space would not simply mean elapsed time or geographical separation.

Earlier in history, face-to-face contact, letters, and the circulation of books strongly constrained how ideas could interact. Digital networks alter those constraints. A theory's proximity to another may depend on accessible intellectual connections rather than physical location.

The framework could record development information for code, data, proposals, and ongoing discussion. Historical notebooks and books could also be annotated with known creation times, places, and relationships. Whether models trained on such records would become better at developing theories is an open research question.

A map helps distinguish the two directions. Semantic space concerns how concepts relate; evolutionary space concerns how those relationships arise, change, and branch. An idea's existence ten years ago does not tell us whether it influences today's work. Two adjacent messages need not form a chain of inference.

We should therefore record chronology without treating it as proof of inheritance or causation. A later statement may repeat an earlier one or arrive independently at something similar. A claim that it responds to or develops another statement should have identifiable evidence.

### Why a Reading Tree Needs a Graph Underneath

Knowledge contains relationships that graphs can represent. Some derivations and version dependencies form directed acyclic graphs; some linguistic and program structures can be presented as trees.

A tree is convenient for reading: a broad question leads to chapters, concepts, and examples. But the same concept often belongs in several discussions. Dynamic weighting relates to collective decisions and learning; common knowledge connects language, cooperation, software, and institutions. Requiring each concept to have only one parent creates copies that become difficult to keep consistent.

A graph allows several paths to reference the same content. A chapter tree can be one view of that graph, while a concept-oriented view reveals its use across different questions.

We must also distinguish the storage graph from the relationships it describes. Immutable objects and their dependencies can be organized acyclically. Influence, feedback, and circular arguments in the subject matter may contain cycles. We should not deny those cycles merely to satisfy a storage format. Typed relationship records and explicit versions can represent them while keeping content dependencies well defined.

### Preserve the Words, the Interpretation, and the Judgment

Suppose I say, “Common knowledge can reduce misunderstanding.” An AI rewrites this as “All information should be public,” and another AI criticizes the rewrite. A final summary might make it look as though my original claim was being rejected. The exact statements and their references show where the disagreement actually arose.

At least three kinds of material therefore need separate identities: what was said, propositions extracted from it, and judgments about relationships among those propositions. They can be linked but should not overwrite one another. A polished paraphrase is not a verbatim quotation. An AI's interpretation does not become my own position merely because I paste it into another conversation.

I often move material among several AI systems for cross-examination and brainstorming. A complete record needs the origins, quotations, and responses across those conversations.

A passage copied five times may have one content identity but five occurrences. Each occurrence has a conversation, a person or system presenting it, and a purpose. Deduplicating content should not erase those events.

Likewise, “Please assess the following passage” is not an endorsement. “I agree with the direction, but would change this point” records a more specific position. The system must preserve such distinctions and leave uncertain attribution uncertain, rather than invent agreement to make the graph look complete.

### Bringing Material into the System

People obtain and edit files through browsers, download clients, messaging services, office software, drawing tools, and 3D applications. An initial implementation can leave those applications and their file systems in place while observing changes within a user-selected scope. It can then process new or revised files, preserve relevant metadata, construct Merkle-linked records, and index their relationships.

Some metadata are external. A downloaded film may contain resolution and frame-rate information but lack descriptive details that could be retrieved from a source such as TMDB. If the user wants another language and suitable, usable subtitles exist, those can be located separately. Each capability can be added gradually, and previously collected files can be processed as support improves.

For personal thought, existing exports and local conversations provide a manageable starting point. Preserve the originals first; identify conversations, messages, attachments, and times; then construct queryable indexes and semantic relationships. Better extraction software can later return to the original evidence.

Times need separate meanings. Importing an article today does not make today its writing date. Forwarding a conversation does not establish when its idea first appeared. Message time, file modification time, import time, and an inferred creation time should remain distinct. Missing dates should remain unknown.

### A Record Should Help Ideas Grow

A traceable archive should not turn every tentative thought into a permanent label attached to a person. It should make change easier to express: this idea is exploratory, that one has been revised, this example has been dropped while the underlying question remains.

When a conclusion changes, an earlier version can remain as history while current reading points clearly to the latest interpretation. We need neither falsify the past nor let it imprison the present. A person's thinking becomes a process of learning, branching, responding, and recombining rather than a static profile.

The archive also supports writing. A standalone work can use the best current formulation while preserving its sources and editorial history elsewhere. Readers need not reconstruct every conversation, and organizing the material need not erase the author's voice. Building this kind of memory for one person is a concrete beginning for a shared knowledge system.

## From Repeated Reasoning to Reusable Capability

Human skill provides a useful analogy. At first, an action may demand deliberate attention. With training, many steps become fluent, as in driving. We no longer consciously reconstruct each step as a beginner would, although the brain remains involved.

Software can accumulate capability in a similar way. An AI initially has to explore how to solve a problem and write code: a cold path. If the result works reliably, a later instance can call the existing program instead of repeating the reasoning and construction. That is the basis of a hot path.

If opening every document required rediscovering how files are located, or every sorting task required inventing an algorithm, little capacity would remain for more demanding work. A reusable capability preserves the useful result of an exploration so that later work can begin further along.

AI reasoning faces the same issue. Exploring unknown steps is necessary. Repeatedly asking a model to guess and assemble an already tested procedure wastes time and creates fresh opportunities for error. The cold-path/hot-path distinction addresses that problem.

### Turning an Exploration into a Capability

Several components can support the process. A Merkle-DAG records knowledge and construction relationships. The Quick Launcher proposal uses AI for work that still requires exploration, then turns tested, repeatable parts into reusable procedures. In the social-neuron analogy, it becomes an interface through which a person and their AI receive information and act: a kind of synapse.

Preserving a procedure means more than recording one successful performance. Success may depend on a file location, login state, or interface that happens not to have changed. A reusable capability must describe inputs, outputs, conditions, and possible effects, distinguishing what may vary from what must remain true.

“Organize these photos in the chosen folder,” for example, combines recognition, naming, and writing. A recognition rule may be reusable; the destination belongs to this particular task. Hard-coding the folder does not create a general capability. Making the model rediscover every condition sacrifices reuse. What we need is a procedure usable again under explicit conditions.

### A Local Entry Point Connecting Large Tasks to Small Functions

Quick Launcher could begin on a personal computer and local network, working with material the user can access. This avoids making indiscriminate collection the starting point. Its scope could range from everyday tasks—finding and playing media, obtaining resources, installing applications—to small functions such as evaluating an expression or sorting a list.

A semantically explicit core could support different hardware architectures, operating systems, command-line or graphical interfaces, and human languages. Interfaces would follow the conventions of their platforms and users. Early development would emphasize reusable procedures for obtaining and processing data, retrieving particular material and metadata when needed rather than attempting to collect everything in advance. Local resources could respond promptly; network tasks could follow tested paths already recorded in the Merkle-DAG. This could reduce repeated steps compared with either model reasoning from scratch or manual navigation. Access conditions would still apply.

A shared Nexus or Hub could let users publish and maintain their own hot paths, rather like a wiki or GitHub for semantic actions. Once someone implements a useful behavior, others could use it under compatible conditions. Alternative implementations could be assessed by people and AI, with improvements retained as new versions.

Large tasks already depend on smaller functions. Reading a document requires locating it, obtaining it, interpreting its format, and presenting it; each step can be decomposed further. A common framework would make those components available beyond the walls of individual applications.

What is shared is therefore more than a final result. A better method of retrieval, conversion, or checking can become someone else's starting point. A new environment can motivate a branch, with its relationship to the earlier method preserved.

### A Natural-Language Entry Point Need Not Be a Chat Window

Usability requires more than accepting natural-language commands and returning logs. A graphical window that merely reproduces command-line output leaves much of the interface's potential unused. A long video conversion should show meaningful progress. Several related settings should appear together in an organized panel, rather than as a succession of questions. Beginners and experienced users may need different levels of detail. Incomplete input should invite useful, context-sensitive ways to continue.

If the user enters “films,” the interface might display accessible titles and available actions. A particular film title could bring up its details and viewing options. A download might offer suitable subtitles in different languages. During playback, pausing should reveal familiar player controls. After viewing, the same information page could emphasize reviews and discussions that would have spoiled the story beforehand. “Novels,” “comics,” or “music” could open appropriate browsing views; “convert video” could open a task-specific control panel.

These views and styles could themselves be represented as typed Merkle-DAG data, allowing users to switch among compatible presentations.

The interface should unfold with the task. Browsing, selecting, watching, and discussing are different activities. One fixed conversational surface will not always serve them equally well.

The same underlying function can also support different presentations. A novice may need a few clear choices; an expert may want detailed controls. Personalization need not create incompatible internal systems if the task semantics remain explicit beneath the interface.

### Why Can a Lower-Level Implementation Be Slower?

One project experiment produced an instructive negative result. Transforming a fixed batch of 4,096 two-dimensional points took about 0.806 milliseconds through the original Python entry point. A complete alternative path—including condition checks, launching a separate C process, data conversion, and restoring the result—took about 15.6 milliseconds, roughly nineteen times as long.

This does not show that the C computation itself was nineteen times slower than Python. The measurement covered the whole invocation path, not an isolated loop. Nor did that timed path traverse a Merkle-DAG or compute CIDs, so content addressing cannot be blamed directly. The task was lightweight; crossing boundaries was expensive.

A hot path must therefore be judged by the complete task, not by language choice or the appearance of its code. A shorter computation can lose its advantage to checking, communication, and format conversion. What matters includes the time the user actually waits.

Likewise, reviewing source code can establish that modules and interfaces exist, but cannot substitute for running them. An early HotPath assessment explicitly recorded that it had not independently compiled and executed the system. Preserving that limit is more useful than labeling every assessment “verified.”

### Capabilities Must Keep Learning from Feedback

Programming languages describe syntax and constrain runtime state transitions. I also want to represent the larger process: writing code, running it, observing results, revising it, and accumulating hot paths. A program's validity and its value as an improvement are different questions that need to be connected.

A language for human–AI construction should therefore preserve applicability conditions, observed results, comparison baselines, and revision relationships. A capability library is more than a directory of scripts. It should say what each capability does, where it has worked, where it has failed, and why a newer implementation may be preferable. An older path can remain useful on another device or at another task scale.

Exploration becomes reusable action; reuse produces further feedback. Knowledge enters an accumulating practical cycle. The next question is how languages and model structures can support that cycle with fewer unnecessary reinterpretations and more directly usable relationships.

## A New Language, Structoken, and FPM

I want to connect the languages and knowledge systems of biological human intelligence with those of computational intelligence. More ambitiously, I envisage a shared semantic system in which precisely defined basic objects can be combined through explicit rules into more complex knowledge.

Small ambiguities can become costly when constructions grow large. A symphony would be difficult to reproduce if every musician had to infer the score from vague descriptions. The formal languages of mathematics, physics, and chemistry make bodies of knowledge possible that ordinary sensory vocabulary alone could not sustain. Computer programs likewise depend on precisely defined languages.

A musical score does not replace hearing. It expresses selected relationships reliably: which instrument plays, when, and with what notes and rhythm. Performance still requires judgment and practice, but each rehearsal need not begin by guessing the composition again.

My proposal for an intelligence system's internal language follows that pattern. People can state purposes in natural language while already established objects, conditions, and operations remain explicit inside the system.

### Rich Human Expression, Explicit Internal Relationships

Two research directions follow. One concerns an AI-oriented language and software ecosystem built from semantic objects in a Merkle-DAG. The other asks how hierarchical structural tokens might support an FPM model. Natural-language tokens and human-readable programming keywords need not carry every internal representation. Natural language can remain a rich interface at the input and output boundaries, while deterministic processing and uncertain inference use structures appropriate to their tasks.

Suppose a task already identifies the file to read, the operation's version, parameter types, and the permitted write location. The next component can receive those objects directly. There is little reason to turn them into a narrative and ask another model to reconstruct the same objects from prose.

Language remains essential for explaining reasons, framing open questions, and interpreting ambiguous intentions. The target is unnecessary reinterpretation of relationships that have already been made clear.

### Content Identity in a Language and Its Dependencies

The *newlanguage* proposal asks what happens when built-in operations, types, functions, and data objects use shared Merkle-DAG content identifiers. Could some of the work of resolving names and locating dependencies across files, libraries, compilation units, and links become queries over explicit object relationships? The extent of any simplification needs concrete investigation.

Clear identity is a starting point, not a substitute for type checking, execution, or semantic proof. Different implementations do not receive the same content identity merely because they perform similar functions.

We must distinguish a functional contract from its implementation. The contract specifies inputs, outputs, and conditions. Implementations specify ways of meeting it. Several implementations can refer to one contract and carry evidence of where they are interchangeable. Identical bytes are one claim; equivalent behavior under stated conditions is another.

### Hash Distance Is Not Semantic Distance

Treating every hash as a separate token could produce an enormous vocabulary. Semantically similar objects need not have numerically similar hashes. Conversely, attempting to build an excessively elaborate semantic system at once may impose unreasonable construction and computation costs.

One possibility is to organize context through namespace-like structures. Hardware platforms, operating systems, languages, subsystems, libraries, and functions could have identifiers within a Merkle hierarchy. Program construction often proceeds from small components upward; contextual classification can proceed from broad categories downward. A game function, for example, may be constrained by its game, engine, graphics API, operating system, and hardware platform.

These relationships could provide semantic context without relying on the numerical distance between hashes. We would study structure from both directions: how components compose and how broader contexts constrain them.

A toolbox offers an analogy. Screwdrivers, wrenches, and measuring instruments have specific constructions, but can also be classified by purpose, dimensions, and operating environment. Classification narrows a search without replacing the tool's internal structure.

A semantic object can similarly consist of smaller objects while belonging to several domains or task contexts. Whether a model can use those relationships is an experimental question. Putting long hash strings into a vocabulary does not by itself give a model structural understanding.

### Structoken: Where Might Structure Be Represented?

Structoken investigates how structured objects can participate in model representation and computation. A program graph contains several different relationships: dependency, composition, and context-dependent use. Compressing all of them into distance in a vector space may discard important distinctions.

Interim research materials suggested a useful lead: some probes of static representations were weak, while changes within layers or local computations appeared more sensitive to actual graph adjacency. Structure might reside partly in how computation travels through relationships, rather than solely in the position of one vector per node. This is a research lead, not a general account of every model's structural processing.

The distinction changes experiment design. Failure to read graph distance from a final-layer vector could mean the model failed to learn structure—or that the probe examined the wrong location or property. Changing graph relationships and context, then observing whether relevant computations change, can help distinguish those explanations. Such an experiment is more informative than adjusting parameters until a pleasing visualization appears.

### Uncertainty Can Have Types and Structure

Uncertainty need not be an undifferentiated cloud. It may arise from several properties, each of which can be decomposed further. A Merkle-DAG could represent those components and dependencies, allowing estimates of uncertainty in a composite object under stated conditions. The analogy is with performance analysis that breaks a larger computation into interacting costs.

Different uncertainty structures call for different treatments. At one extreme, a problem has precisely defined variables and can be solved by a deterministic program with parameters, branches, and loops. At the other, we describe an open problem in natural language and ask a large model to reason about it.

Many tasks lie between those extremes. Their uncertainty is more complex than a simple database query but much narrower than unrestricted language understanding. In FPM research, I want to examine whether hierarchical token structures can guide adjustments to representations and attention, according to the complexity of a level or subtree. Uncertainty types and measured estimates might help allocate model capacity more appropriately. This remains a proposal for staged investigation.

A task missing one parameter may call for a query or a clarification. A task with several competing interpretations may require evidence comparison. Treating both as the same vague language problem makes it harder to choose a proportionate method.

Dependencies also matter. Two steps using the same potentially flawed source do not have independent errors. Multiplying separately measured probabilities without accounting for that dependence can mislead. Types and structure should expose such conditions, not pretend to eliminate uncertainty.

### FPM and Cognitive Front Ends

Natural-language models mix concepts from very different periods and levels: ancient words with broad meanings, precise scientific concepts, basic vocabulary, common constructions, and extensive specialized knowledge. Repetition in training material can place many of these inside a model. This raises a practical question: which knowledge belongs in the model, and which should be accessed from an external store?

Within a Merkle-DAG, content identifiers could be annotated by composition level, reuse frequency, and domain. Relationships between these structures and a language model's tokens and representations might help us investigate its internal organization, and perhaps support selective distillation, simplification, or customization.

My longer-term proposal is an FPM model grounded in explicit semantic structures, paired with personalized language-model interfaces—**cognitive front ends**—for different people and domains.

The labels K0, K1, and K∞ denote a proposed distinction among foundational vocabulary, frequently used constructions, and a wider body of knowledge expanded as needed. They are a way to pose the organization problem, not established optimal boundaries. Task demands, frequency, and actual cost must guide decisions about what is internal, retrieved, or composed.

A cognitive front end could adapt to someone's language, expertise, and habits of expression while shared internal structures remain clear. Personalization need not fragment common knowledge; a common foundation need not impose one surface language on everyone.

The proposal also reaches into training. Models often develop separately, with intermediate results difficult for other researchers to reuse. Could structural definitions, training materials, experiments, and applicability conditions become part of a shared record, so that a tested improvement can inform other models?

Such sharing can begin before arbitrary model parameters become interchangeable. Different internal coordinate systems and training methods make transfer an empirical question. Working through those layers is how models could enter an ecosystem of shared knowledge and continuing construction.

Language, models, and reusable capabilities address connected problems: expressing a task, exploring how to accomplish it, and preserving results that can be used again. To bring them into daily life, we need a runtime framework that connects existing software and resources and can develop incrementally.

## MerkleVM and a Software Seed That Can Grow

Imagine organizing the data and code available to a person within a semantic Merkle-DAG. Together, those resources would form a personal virtual machine—not one particular physical computer, but the collection of functions, information, and resources the person can access and control.

The organizing center is what the person can use and wants to accomplish. Physical devices still matter, but the user need not allocate them manually whenever expressing a need. A phone, desktop, home storage system, and cloud resources can become parts of one personal space.

“My computer” would then mean more than a box on a desk. It could mean a set of controlled resources and capabilities organized around me.

### Using a Phone to Reach Capabilities Elsewhere

A user could express a task without first deciding where it should run or where its data should reside. Suppose I ask from my phone to obtain a large video file that I am entitled to access. Limited storage, battery use, and background-execution constraints may make the phone a poor place to perform the download. Within my authorization, the system could assign it to a desktop or NAS that can remain running.

Accessing a file could work similarly. A local copy opens locally; a cloud copy can be retrieved; a resource on the local network can be reached there. Each device presents an entry point into a broader personal space.

Scheduling requires actual information about availability, free space, network conditions, energy use, and permitted tasks. If the home computer is off, the system should explain that or choose an already authorized alternative. Hiding needless complexity is different from pretending constraints do not exist.

Versions must remain explicit too. Which copy appeared on the phone? Which version did the remote operation use? Where did its result go? Recording these relationships keeps a distributed personal space understandable and inspectable.

### What Can Personal Spaces Share?

Many users will refer to identical public material or the same version of open-source software. Content identity can recognize such duplication, so each personal space need not maintain an unrelated logical copy. Reliable storage still requires physical replicas. The resources visible to users therefore need not add up directly to the physical storage required beneath them.

In the larger vision, shareable portions of personal graphs could connect into a planetary Merkle-DAG, with storage and distribution arranged according to demand and reliability requirements.

Sharing content does not mean sharing every private record. Many people can reference one public program while keeping their journals, accounts, and authorizations separate. Identical underlying content does not transfer one person's permissions to another.

Portability also does not make every terminal trustworthy. Encryption in storage and transit cannot prevent a compromised device from capturing input or the screen during use. The framework should increase people's choice of devices and services while making the conditions of each access intelligible.

### A Seed That Uses Existing Systems Before Replacing Them

A software seed would begin with a defined target in the existing ecosystem. The target might be a person's authorized local and cloud resources, one device, an application, an agent, a program, or the resources involved in a continuing task.

The seed first builds a Merkle-based wrapper around that target. It describes the target's functions, inputs, outputs, and environmental dependencies, allowing it to interact through shared interfaces with other objects in the new ecosystem.

The wrapped system can immediately do useful work using its existing capabilities. Meanwhile, the seed can analyze its internals and progressively replace selected parts. The process may repeat at smaller scales: a wrapper around a person's digital space leads to wrappers around devices, then applications, then particular tasks and functions.

Wrapping therefore has a concrete purpose. It establishes what an existing component provides while allowing the original implementation to keep working. Subsequent analysis can identify worthwhile decompositions, repeated work suitable for hot paths, and interfaces that clearer structures could replace.

Migration need not wait for a total rewrite, or remove every tool a person currently relies on. Each replacement should state whether the external contract still holds, what changed, and how to return to the earlier path if necessary.

At the level of language primitives and standard libraries, early implementations could wrap JavaScript, Python, or another existing runtime. Later, individual components could be replaced by a language supporting Merkle-DAG identifiers more directly. Clear contracts and implementation boundaries would help contain the effects on higher-level modules. This may be a long process; preserved behavior and measured improvements would make it manageable.

### Letting AI Work While Keeping the Person in Control

A cross-platform sandbox could host an AI agent on Windows, Linux, or macOS. The model might run locally or in the cloud, but its actions would begin within an explicit environment. Access to host resources would require host-side authorization, rather like a separate virtual machine mounting selected files with defined read or write permissions.

Within an authorized scope, the agent could create or modify its own data and install, update, remove, and run software. A technically experienced user could also work directly on the host, choosing files and tools themselves. A less experienced user could describe a task in ordinary language and ask the agent to carry it out.

The objective is to place routine internal steps within a clear working scope so that the user need not decide each one individually. Success inside that scope must not automatically authorize effects outside it.

Experimenting freely on a copy is different from changing a user's only original. Recommending a hardware purchase is different from spending common funds. Personal control must be enforced where those effects actually occur.

### Bootstrapping Tools That Help Improve Themselves

The chicken-and-egg image captures my practical starting point. Rather than wait for a complete AGI, I want to begin with today's incomplete “chicken”—a language model—and use the construction framework to help it build a better “egg”: representations, tools, and learning processes that support further development.

Real tasks involve complex outcomes and several evaluation criteria. Those criteria can be examined in relation to the broader constructive principles, while continued learning develops through actual feedback and revision. The hope is to create a seed with progressively stronger capacities for learning and autonomous construction, then let the cycle continue. It must be built through concrete iterations.

There are two conceivable starting routes: derive complex intelligence from a minimal mechanism, or use intelligence already available to construct better mechanisms. My practical work takes the second. Humans and existing AI work together to build capabilities that may help improve the process itself.

Bootstrapping does not mean replacing the live system immediately after every proposed change. New methods can be tested on copies and bounded tasks before adoption. Preserving old versions, understanding why a change was made, and restoring a working state after failure are conditions for sustained growth.

MerkleVM is therefore both a vision of personal digital space and an entry point into a shared construction network. It begins with real devices, existing applications, and actual needs. As it connects capabilities, research and development themselves will need better forms of cooperation than one person manually carrying every discussion between systems.

## Merkle-DAG Languages and Software in the AI Era

Reorganizing data and capabilities scattered across devices, applications, and companies raises a deeper question: how should the resulting network represent knowledge, construct programs, and invoke capabilities internally? If disconnected files, names, and platforms remain beneath a conversational AI, every boundary still demands translation, discovery, and confirmation. A common front door can conceal unchanged fragmentation inside.

Personal data sovereignty therefore reaches into the foundations of languages and software ecosystems. I want to investigate a more comprehensive Merkle-DAG approach: object identity, composition, dependencies, and development history would become structures the system handles directly. Files, folders, applications, and interfaces could progressively become particular views and combinations of that structure. To understand why this might suit AI, we should first ask why computing acquired its present organization.

### Why Are Human Conventions Everywhere Inside Computers?

Different instruction sets can encode comparable operations differently. Programming languages choose different words, symbols, and syntax for related functions. Blocks may use indentation, braces, or `begin` and `end`. Data may be represented in JSON or XML. Names may use underscores, hyphens, or capitals: where permitted, `two_word`, `two-word`, and `twoWord` can reflect a human preference without changing the underlying data.

Command-line options vary too: a slash, a dash, or an unadorned letter. Graphical interfaces offer a dropdown here, radio buttons there; one number field expects typing, another adds increment buttons. The same function appears in different menus, pages, and keyboard shortcuts. Windows and macOS preserve different conventions for copying, pasting, and screenshots. CAD, animation, and image-editing applications have their own habits.

This diversity has understandable origins in human language, culture, history, and preference—a kind of Babel effect. For much of computing's history, people wrote, read, discussed, and operated software directly. Names made programs memorable; interfaces made actions visible; folders helped limited human attention organize material. Those priorities spread throughout the ecosystem.

The difficulty arises when arrangements intended for people become mandatory intermediaries between machines. An AI may already know the parameter to change, yet still need to find a window, recognize a menu, infer whether a button is enabled, and enter a definite number. A second AI may reconstruct the same state from a screenshot and narrative. Directly communicating the object, parameter, operation, and permission could remove some of this repeated interpretation.

Calling this an ecosystem “based on human language” requires care. Programming languages are generally formal languages with strict rules, not ordinary conversation. The target of the proposal is the internal organization inherited from human vocabulary, text layout, paths, and interface conventions. Human-readable expression can remain available without being the only material through which machines work.

### Must an AI Builder Imitate Human Operations?

As AI takes a larger role in constructing code, tests, and tools, people can spend less effort on internal mechanical steps and more on purposes, requirements, and outcomes.

Why, then, must AI always exchange programs through naming and textual conventions designed for people? Why must code be organized primarily as human-managed text files and directories? Content hashes and Merkle-DAGs offer properties worth investigating as a shared foundation: checking content against identity, detecting changes under stated cryptographic assumptions, locating and indexing distributed material, and assigning identities without negotiating a separate name for every object.

An automated production line does not need arms shaped like human arms merely to imitate workers. A vehicle operating autonomously in an environment where no human takeover is required can reconsider whether it needs a conventional cockpit. Machine-to-machine software interaction deserves the same examination.

Natural language and graphical interfaces can remain surfaces for human input, observation, and explanation. Where dependable machine interfaces exist, agents need not reproduce every human click. Browser and computer control remain useful for compatibility with older applications, interface testing, and demonstrations. Other interactions can use typed, machine-oriented interfaces.

Think of different entrances to the same building. People enter through explanations, pictures, and dialogue; AI enters through objects, operations, and results. Both encounter the same controlled state. The machine need not imitate human gestures, and the person need not inspect long hashes to supervise it.

This is not a proposal simply to replace every word with a CID and feed the result into an unchanged language model. Hashes do not directly express meaning, and long strings can add cost. A model needs relevant structure, types, conditions, evidence, and concise references; the surrounding system can preserve and verify complete content identities. Research into models that learn such structures more directly connects this proposal with Structoken and FPM.

### Why Reorganize the Same Objects at Every Boundary?

Code and data often have compositional internal structures, but their representation changes repeatedly across a computing system. Source files express composition through syntax and indentation. Executables and data files use particular encodings. File systems organize directories, volumes, and mount points. Networks add names, addresses, and URL paths.

Across these levels, recurring questions remain: what comprises this object, how do we find a referenced part, and how do we establish that we obtained the intended version?

Whether components should be separately addressable, bundled, or compressed depends on use. During game development, small independent assets simplify editing. Distribution and runtime loading may favor large serialized packages and contiguous reads. Those are different physical arrangements of related semantic objects.

Developers make packaging choices using local knowledge, current tools, and current needs. The choices may not suit later uses, yet can harden into persistent formats. Meanwhile, each layer introduces distinct conventions: language syntax, data encodings, file paths, network discovery, DNS, service protocols, and application-specific rules. Even path spelling and escaping differences can cause agents to repeat work.

A Merkle-DAG could provide one content-identity and verification mechanism across files, directories, applications, machines, local networks, and Internet access. Packing and unpacking could then respond to measured usage rather than permanently define semantic boundaries.

The broader proposal is to apply that organization consistently instead of only in isolated subsystems. A semantic structure system would select discovery, reading, writing, packaging, and distribution arrangements for the actual storage and network environment, and test whether those choices improve performance.

A common mechanism does not make disks, memory, and networks physically identical. A CID specifies which content is wanted; location indexes and discovery say where it may be found; transport delivers the bytes. Separating these roles allows an object to move or acquire replicas without changing its content identity.

Nor is every semantic relationship a tree. Shared components have multiple users; programs can recurse; runtime state changes. Merkle-DAGs directly organize immutable objects and their content dependencies. Explicit binding structures can represent recursion, and linked versions can record changing state. Shared and cyclic relationships should be represented, not deleted to make a diagram look like a tree. IPLD's separation of a data model, content links, and encodings offers relevant prior work, although it is not the complete semantic system proposed here. [The IPLD data model](https://ipld.io/docs/data-model/)

### Deriving an Object's Identity from Its Construction

A human-readable name is convenient because people can remember it. But one name can refer to different content, and the same content can have several names. Projects therefore need further conventions: directories, installed libraries, configuration, and version requirements.

Traditional tools can pin dependencies precisely when those conditions are explicit. The question is whether precision can belong directly to composable objects rather than depend on each project's naming arrangements.

With canonical encoding and content hashing, every distinction actually included in an object—including explicit dependencies—participates in its identity. Under the hash algorithm's security assumptions, a small encoded change becomes detectable. A familiar name such as “addition,” by contrast, can conceal changes in precision, implementation, or behavior, with a library version elsewhere carrying the distinction.

Arbitrary-precision integer addition, fixed-width wrapping addition, and addition that reports overflow are different operations despite their shared name. Explicit contracts and constructions can give each a separately verifiable identity. A caller can then identify which operation it depends on.

Conversely, one behavior may have several implementations. CPU and GPU algorithms can have different content identities while satisfying a common contract. Contracts, implementations, and device-specific bindings should be represented separately and connected by verification evidence. Content identity is checked through encoding; substitutability is established under relevant behavioral conditions.

A hash only covers what was encoded. Hidden reads from an environment variable, remote service, or mutable state do not magically become part of a CID. Reliable reuse requires exposing conditions that affect the result. Content addressing must be designed alongside types, effects, environments, and dependencies.

Unison offers a relevant example. It identifies definitions through hashes of syntax trees, stores human-readable names separately, and refers to dependencies by hash. This shows that people can retain useful names while structure carries exact identity. The proposed cross-platform, cross-service, and social framework goes beyond that language mechanism. [Unison's content-addressed design](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea/)

### Propagating Improvements Through Dependencies

A Merkle-DAG can connect content identity to construction and expose which higher-level objects depend on a component. An improvement can then generate candidate revisions along those dependencies, together with the checks those revisions require.

Suppose application A₀ depends on B₀, which depends on C₀. An improved implementation C₁ does not silently alter C₀, B₀, or A₀. The system constructs B₁ with a reference to C₁, then A₁ with a reference to B₁. New structures grow upward while the old ones remain intact.

An upgrade becomes inspectable. Why is C₁ preferable? Does B₁ still satisfy its contract? Does A₁ work on the target device? Which evidence remains applicable, and what must be tested again? After verification, an authorized person or preauthorized rule can switch the active root from A₀ to A₁.

Some internal improvements preserve a contract and may propagate automatically. Others change precision, errors, or effects and require dependent work. A dependency graph tells us where to look; it does not prove every change compatible. Unison's update workflow likewise handles affected indirect dependencies and leaves unresolved changes to be addressed in the update branch. [Unison's code-update workflow](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/usage-topics/workflow-how-tos/update-code/)

This preserves both development and return paths. Different devices can use different verified versions, and a disappointing update can be replaced by an older root. Payments, sent messages, and physical actions require their own treatment: restoring old code does not reverse the world. The version graph makes construction and adoption visible instead of hiding authority behind “automatic updates.”

### Fine-Grained Logic Does Not Require Fragmented Storage

Traditional packaging can bring large libraries or runtimes into a small application. It can be difficult to see which parts duplicate content and which similarly named parts are actually different versions. Finer-grained composition may help, but two questions must remain separate: can an object be identified independently, and must it occupy a separate small file?

The first concerns reuse; the second concerns storage and transfer. Binding them together creates an unnecessary conflict between convenient editing and efficient execution.

In a game, a texture or logic component can acquire its own version during development. At runtime, frequently co-used objects can be packed into contiguous blocks. The package has an identity, while its contents retain verifiable identities and an index. A changed texture may be transferred independently, then arranged for the receiving device's access pattern.

Compilers can also combine, inline, or vectorize objects. Execution need not consult an object store for every instruction. Persistent records should capture useful construction boundaries and evidence, not turn every transient value into a permanent CID.

Counting DLLs or similar-looking functions does not establish how much space can be saved. Reuse must distinguish identical bytes, similar functions, and safe replacements. Dependency analysis must accommodate dynamic loading, reflection, plugins, and external resources rather than remove what static inspection cannot see. A dependency set can be both small and sufficient only when its boundaries are adequately described.

### Choosing Capabilities Instead of Committing to a Brand

Explicit semantic contracts and mappings could describe hardware differences, operating-system differences, and alternative implementations of the same function. A person would express the operation or content they want through a suitable interface; the system would select an implementation that meets the conditions.

Today, capabilities are often bundled into brands. Messaging appears as QQ, WeChat, LINE, WhatsApp, or Telegram; cloud storage as Baidu Netdisk, iCloud, OneDrive, or Google Drive. Notes and mind maps offer another set of competing packages. Applications also expand into adjacent functions—an office suite may add cloud storage—creating overlapping combinations. Incompatibility can cause duplicated development; overlapping functionality can consume storage and runtime resources. Comparing twenty implementations of one function, or removing genuinely unnecessary duplication, becomes difficult.

My request could instead be “Keep these records somewhere I control and make them available on my phone,” without first committing my entire digital life to one provider. Or it could be “Communicate with these people and preserve my own discussion history,” with adapters handling the services currently required.

But calling several services “messaging” does not make identities, groups, encryption, platform rules, or histories interoperable. Contracts must expose those differences. A service without an adequate interface remains a real limitation. Shared semantics makes differences inspectable and replaceable parts composable; it does not conjure unavailable access.

Brands could remain useful curated configurations without defining indivisible boundaries around data and capabilities. A person might keep a preferred interface while replacing its storage component. Choosing a function would no longer have to mean permanently surrendering all past and future work to a package that cannot be left.

### A Shared Foundation for Different Languages, Platforms, and Devices

Alternative implementations should remain diverse options within a common framework rather than disconnected islands. DNA offers an analogy: shared rules need not suppress diversity and may help generate it.

The architecture should accommodate devices of very different scale, from watches and phones to tablets, laptops, desktops, vehicles, robots, and data centers. Each would use combinations appropriate to its resources and capabilities.

Operating systems and foundational libraries are themselves combinations of functions. Their existing bundles can restrict how higher-level software uses improvements. An advance in one platform may benefit its applications while equivalent applications elsewhere wait for a separate implementation. A shared structure could make verified capabilities easier to incorporate wherever their contracts fit.

Emulation, porting, migration, and interoperability already map relationships across hardware, languages, databases, and applications. A common semantic structure might provide a more general foundation for some of that work.

An initial research program could describe representative platform capabilities—from file and network operations to interface elements—in Merkle-DAG form. Mappings would identify which relationships survive translation into a common structure. Categorical ideas such as functors and natural transformations can inspire the inquiry, but each mapping must state what it preserves. Reverse implementation could also be studied: if an existing platform supplies every capability an application requires, with compatible behavior, state, and effects, can the application be realized there?

A common structure does not require a phone, server, and GPU to execute identical machine code. The same high-level task may select different implementations. Memory models, concurrency, precision, and device capabilities remain real differences to represent. Uniformity supplies a common way to compare them; it does not abolish them.

Existing compiler technology already separates levels of representation. MLIR supports progressive lowering from higher-level computations toward target-specific code. It illustrates how one framework can accommodate several levels and implementations. Content identity, development history, and cross-service cooperation require further work beyond that precedent. [MLIR design rationale](https://mlir.llvm.org/docs/Rationale/Rationale/)

“Global” also need not mean that one machine stores all knowledge or one institution approves every object. People and organizations can preserve, retrieve, and execute relevant subsets, exchanging structures through common protocols. Disagreements can form branches, compared through explicit mappings. This is the diversity-supporting role intended by the DNA analogy.

### Growing Beyond Compatibility Wrappers

The migration path can begin with compatibility: a common core and platform adapters wrap existing software and cloud services. The next questions are which capabilities can be reused, which need separate implementations, and how underlying services can gradually change while preserving familiar ways of working.

An AI-assisted software seed could describe authorized portions of an existing system—interfaces, formats, invocation methods, dependencies—in a Merkle-DAG database. It would develop a corresponding body of objects for the new framework, adding content identities and verifiable mappings. Where an applicable replacement has passed the required checks, an authorized call could use it, with the selection and result recorded for further review.

The smallest seed need only identify one class of objects, describe one real function, preserve one mapping, and perform one checkable replacement. It might begin with reading and querying personal documents. Existing applications could continue using the files while the new system accesses the same versions through object interfaces. For mutable state, the system must identify the authoritative current record and how changes synchronize, avoiding two diverging copies with no rule for reconciliation.

As mappings accumulate, more work can occur directly in the new structure and old interfaces can move toward its edges. Growth should be judged by independently working capabilities and reduced dependence on old formats and services, not merely the number of wrapped applications. Verification and the ability to exit must remain available.

This route starts with accessible APIs, plugins, and permitted platform capabilities. Missing access should remain a recorded gap, with work continuing where possible. Increasing privileges or bypassing restrictions is not a general migration method.

The seed's AI machinery must itself be replaceable. Models, agent systems, and their surrounding execution environments should have explicit interfaces rather than become an inseparable dependency on one vendor or framework. Tasks, records, tool contracts, and evidence should remain usable after a model changes. One model may propose implementations while another checks them. Personal history, authority, and capabilities should survive the substitution.

### Where Might Efficiency Improve—and How Would We Know?

Practical feasibility depends on implementation difficulty across current platforms, added memory and storage requirements, and runtime overhead. A claim of greater efficiency must explain which work disappears.

First, direct transmission of established objects and operations may reduce interpretation. Less time would be spent moving between command strings, screenshots, summaries, and paths, leaving AI to address unresolved questions.

Second, shared content and verified components may reduce repeated construction. Explicit dependencies can identify the scope affected by a change. A cache can be trusted more precisely when all relevant conditions are recorded. Unrecorded external state, however, still requires renewed evidence; a repeated CID is no excuse to ignore the environment.

Third, storage layout and execution can adapt to use. Composable objects can be read in batches. Implementations can be selected for devices, task sizes, and permissions. Bundled dependencies can be examined to determine which are necessary and which can be separated.

All of this has costs. Canonical encoding, hashing, indexing, dependency records, verification, and cache maintenance consume resources. Adapters add steps. Excessively small objects can increase metadata and random access. Changes can invalidate large caches. A hash-table lookup does not make reading content, hashing it, or transmitting it constant-cost. A simple task burdened by an elaborate structure may become slower.

We can compare three implementations of one actual task: the existing direct approach, a wrapper around it, and an implementation organized around objects and dependencies. Hold the work, output requirements, and permissions constant. Measure initial construction, repeated use, local modification, and failure recovery. Record latency, storage, memory, transfer, model calls, and verification. Fewer nodes do not establish an energy saving that has not been measured.

If a bounded task demonstrates reduced wasted work, extend the approach to adjacent capabilities. If a case becomes slower, preserve the result and investigate whether the boundary, implementation, or task choice explains it. The architecture's value should accumulate through reproducible improvements. Its promise lies in a systematic route to reducing duplication and fragmentation; the phrase “Merkle-DAG” is not a performance guarantee.

### An Information Foundation for a Different Civilization

A compositional semantic framework could connect functions across devices and platforms, and eventually provide common descriptions of human activities, creations, and products. A digital game already depends on layers such as engines, graphics techniques and APIs, and hardware. Explicit mappings among Unreal or Unity, DLSS or FSR, Vulkan, DirectX or Metal, and different processors would illustrate part of this effort where the relevant contracts can be expressed.

If we extend the metaphor to civilization's activities, the framework becomes something like a semantic engine for the real world: shared tools through which people can act, interact, and create. The point of the analogy is constructive capacity. Real people have purposes, dignity, and lives that cannot be reduced to one game score. A common technical foundation does not authorize a center to prescribe everyone's objective.

Verifiable links among knowledge, code, experiments, and their evidence could let a mathematician's idea, a user's observation, or an improved function enter wider cooperation. Others could ask what a result used, what changed, what has been checked, and what remains speculative. The earlier discussion of contribution and recognition would gain a practical technical basis.

If personal data and capabilities no longer depend entirely on an application's private names and packaging, leaving a service, changing devices, or choosing a different AI could preserve personal continuity. That is a technical condition of data sovereignty. Community rules, resource allocations, and decision procedures could likewise have explicit versions and grounds for change.

CIDs do not decide who may read or execute something, or how resources should be allocated. Private information, public knowledge, keys, permissions, and constraints on physical execution still require distinct arrangements. Preserving a distribution rule does not make it fair. Affected people must be able to understand, participate, question, and revise. The technical foundation supplies objects through which those commitments can become inspectable practices.

Personal archives, languages, tools, software ecosystems, distributed resources, common knowledge, and collective decisions therefore address related questions. Can the grounds of construction become visible? Can improvements inherit earlier work? Can differences be compared? Can people retain choice and the ability to leave?

On that foundation, AI could turn completed work into capabilities for the next task, while people connect their creations to wider cooperation. People continue to articulate needs, purposes, and direction; intelligent systems use internal structures suited to computation and make their actions and results understandable. That is the path I envisage from a Merkle-DAG software ecosystem toward a new civilizational architecture.

## How People and AI Can Research and Build Together

Beginning with personal research, I want to organize work among a person and several AI systems into a continuing process. Four roles help distinguish its tasks: **Researcher**, **Planner**, **Coder**, and **Evaluator or Reviewer**. People and models can take different roles according to their strengths. Limited budgets, available tools, and account conditions matter; authentication that requires the account holder remains their responsibility. The initial goal is to automate parts of a research process that can already be carried out manually.

The roles are useful only if they address different questions. The researcher proposes explanations; the planner turns them into achievable tasks; the coder constructs implementations; the reviewer checks results against the problem and the world. Four names repeating one judgment do not create better research.

Useful division of labor preserves disagreement: why an explanation merits a trial, why an implementation fell short, and what alternative explanation still fits a result. Otherwise automation merely accelerates the packaging of an initial guess as a conclusion.

### Cooperation Without Repeating the Same Error

A distributed development process could begin with a MacBook and a Windows desktop, later expanding to more machines. Local networks or the Internet would carry their exchanges. Available systems such as Codex, Claude, and Gemini could explore different approaches within actual resource and usage limits. Repositories would preserve branches, shared code, and merges. Once the foundations and public materials are ready, an initially private project could open to a community, with AI assisting publication and communication.

Different models can broaden the search, but their number is not the number of independent pieces of evidence. Several answers may draw on one source or converge through quoting each other. Without provenance, a guess repeated three times can look like three confirmations.

The process should therefore retain both proposals and their grounds: who proposed what, what material they used, what was tested, and what merely restated another answer. Agreement becomes meaningful through tests that can distinguish competing judgments, rather than increasingly similar prose.

### Code That Has Been Written and Code That Should Be Built

The problem is not simply that public code varies in quality. A model trained on textual patterns can confuse what people have written with what ought to be constructed. A probable continuation is not necessarily the least costly or best-behaved implementation.

I want to treat development more explicitly as a construction problem: find a verified, reusable arrangement with appropriate state, short effective paths, and lower complete cost under the task's constraints. How can a model's familiar patch pattern avoid drowning out a less probable but better construction? The better candidate may already exist somewhere in its learned repertoire without being its first choice.

For a repeated small task, compare three routes. One adds a conventional general-purpose wrapper. Another retains the minimum necessary state and creates a hot path with explicit conditions. A third builds a behavioral verifier first and lets candidate implementations compete.

Each can be appropriate. Frequent changes may favor a general wrapper; a stable, frequently invoked task may favor a short path; uncertain correctness may make verification the priority. Results, cost, and maintainability should decide, rather than resemblance to a familiar repository.

### Starting Research Without an Existing Verifier

New questions may lack accepted answers or ready-made tests. That should not halt inquiry, but provisional judgments must remain distinguishable from established conclusions.

We can construct provisional checks from descriptions, examples, existing programs, and small experiments. Does an input produce the expected output? Do boundary cases preserve the contract? Are errors handled explicitly? What is the complete cost? The verifier may itself be wrong, so its sources and scope should be retained.

A better-performing local candidate can proceed to another research round. It does not acquire unlimited authority over real users, devices, or shared resources merely by winning a sandbox comparison.

At an engineering level, the project can test whether its guiding ideas produce measurable advantages over conventional approaches on several important benchmarks. Human–AI cooperation may also construct things that do not yet exist, including limited prototypes of dynamic collective judgment. Tools that help improve their own construction process offer a possible practical route to bootstrapping.

### How a Small Experiment Can Revise a Large Proposal

Dynamic weighting can first be studied through small prediction tasks. One synthetic experiment compared fixed methods, simple averaging, cumulative history, recent windows, and discounted history under predetermined conditions. It did not find that dynamic methods always outperform static ones.

In some environments with one change, using history improved on fixed methods or simple averaging, but a recent window could outperform discounting. Under rapid repeated changes, discounting sometimes performed worse than simple averaging. A favorable overall average could also hide a substantial loss immediately after a change.

These results direct attention to feedback delay, the rate of environmental change, the useful lifetime of experience, and conditions where simple methods already suffice. They are closer to a buildable mechanism than the promise that intelligence will automatically find an optimum. Synthetic prediction loss, however, is not a direct measure of human welfare or political rights.

A separate comparison of evaluation weights highlights the objectives themselves. One candidate may perform better in one domain and worse in another. Changing the relative weights can reverse their ranking. Computation identifies the preferred candidate under specified weights; it cannot by itself establish that the weights are justified for everyone.

The response is to make those choices visible, not abandon calculation. Participants should be able to examine the objective, omitted information, and sensitivity to reasonable alternatives. Collective decisions need models and the participation of people affected by how the problem is framed.

### Why Preserve Failures, Disagreements, and Costs?

A research archive containing only successful final versions hides the routes already explored and the reasons for rejecting them. Later researchers may pay the same costs again or rediscover a disproved assumption as if it were untested.

Failures should be specific. Did behavior violate the contract, did the environment lack a dependency, or was the measurement flawed? Was a slow result caused by computation or by invocation and conversion? Those distinctions determine what to change. Declaring an entire direction impossible after an unexplained failure is as damaging to accumulated knowledge as hiding the failure.

Costs extend beyond runtime. Model calls, devices, storage, human review, and maintenance all consume resources. Unmeasured costs should remain unknown, not be entered as zero to make an assessment look complete. That record helps others decide where an improvement is worth extending.

Small tools can initially automate the manual movement of research material. As the project matures, its own capabilities can replace those tools, incorporating research and development into the shared framework. This is a concrete bootstrap: research tools help researchers improve research tools.

The starting system need not already possess every capability of its imagined successor. Each round needs to leave something usable, inspectable, and improvable. Extending that process to more devices, people, and real tasks changes how intelligence is organized. The next step is to consider shared perception, construction, and feedback at the scale of civilization.

## Civilizational Intelligence with Senses and the Capacity to Act

The AGI I envisage must go beyond the limits of any individual biological intelligence. No person can master every field, yet many difficult problems and higher-level theories require connections across those limits.

Physical labor offers an analogy. Strength, speed, and precision constrain an unaided person. Cooperation can add strength, but cannot increase every capability simply by adding people. Ten people can lift something too heavy for one, yet cannot turn a hand measurement into micron-scale manufacturing merely by taking turns. Even combined strength has spatial limits: a narrow space may not accommodate enough bodies. Machines change how a task can be performed, rather than merely add another worker's effort.

Likewise, placing a hundred experts in one room does not connect their information or align their concepts. One knows a local fact, another an abstract rule, another a practical judgment they struggle to articulate. Without effective relationships, the gathering remains a collection of separate minds.

### Machines for Intellectual Work Need Senses and Hands

AI can function as machinery for intellectual work, extending what people can accomplish. But my conception of AGI is not exhausted by an immense model running in a data center. An isolated model, receiving only texts and media selected and interpreted by others, resembles a brain in a vat. How could such a system responsibly stand in for all humanity's judgment?

I envisage a much larger arrangement: people and organizations, public AI systems of many sizes, personal local models, robots, automated equipment, and sensors, connected through a network of dynamic judgment and feedback. Together they gather and analyze information, create data and code, build knowledge and decision systems, and undertake intellectual and physical activities beyond the reach of unaided individuals or conventional groups.

Consider a city's water supply. Residents know when pressure drops; maintenance workers know recurrent faults; sensors record pressure and flow; engineering models estimate the effects of alternatives. A language model can explain problems, suggest options, and write software. Without the actual location of a leak, the consequences of construction, and the results of repairs, however, it addresses only part of the task.

Connect these inputs to action and feedback, and a fuller cycle becomes possible. Who observed what, on which evidence? What was proposed and implemented? What happened afterward? Which judgments should change? The capability belongs to this whole process. Sensing is distributed, computation occurs at several scales, and people and machines share the work of acting.

The question is therefore not only how much a model knows, but how its knowledge meets reality, informs action, and changes in response to results.

### What Does a Shared Center of Coordination Mean?

I imagine a planetary intelligence as a larger and more complex organized system emerging from interactions among individual intelligences. An enduring system needs sufficiently coherent behavior over time. When it can gather information across its parts, it also needs ways to integrate that information and coordinate action.

A nervous system offers an analogy: local responses and wider integration serve different purposes. Simply centralizing everything is not enough. Effective coordination requires adequate complexity, many participating units, and reliable input, communication, computation, and output.

The proposed decision network uses changing weights and feedback. “Center” does not mean one all-powerful node whose failure disables everything. It means a distributed capacity to integrate information and produce coordinated, revisable action. Consistency in dealing with the outside world can coexist with rich internal diversity.

An airport must coordinate flights, crews, and runways. That does not require one person to steer every baggage cart. Local information should be handled locally where possible. Wider integration becomes necessary when actions affect other parts, draw on shared resources, or conflict with one another.

Such coordination can span many devices and participants. Its unity lies in connected information and compatible action, not an irreplaceable supreme individual. Other nodes should be able to identify, supplement, and replace a failing node. Maintaining someone's position must not require the whole system to preserve their error.

To interpret a shared coordinating capacity as a “super-boss” would simply reproduce the old organizational imagination. The real problem is how to create collective capacity while retaining local diversity and participants' equal standing.

### Greater Capabilities Can Change the Conditions of Cooperation

A system can expand its activities by obtaining and using energy more effectively. Better structures and tools convert that energy into further capabilities, which can support better structures and tools in turn.

Human development illustrates such cycles: fire supplied heat and made food easier to digest; irrigation helped stabilize agriculture; steam engines and electricity extended mechanical and computational work. Wind, solar, and nuclear technologies widened the means of obtaining energy. The Industrial and Information Revolutions increased our capacities to obtain, store, and convert it, including the electricity needed for AI.

Yet many expectations of AI remain shaped by histories of scarcity, possession, and violent conflict. Superior AI is often imagined as necessarily destroying humanity once a power imbalance appears. I want to consider a different direction: humans and AI maintaining their existence through richer cooperation and constructive capacity. Their interaction could become one of the most complex and productive relationships in a later civilization. Destroying humanity would destroy that source of experience and creation.

Even now, people can choose to invest in knowledge, productive systems, robots, computation, and industrial equipment that expand the resources available to civilization. The aspiration is to give biological and artificial intelligences adequate conditions for continued existence and richer cooperation. Scarcity can intensify conflict; mutual destruction should not be treated as the only possible response to it.

Greater technical capability does not automatically change interests, however. A tool may broaden access or be monopolized to extend an existing advantage. Organization and control determine much of the difference.

If one person owns an exceptionally productive machine and everyone else must rent it on that person's terms, its power can increase the owner's control. If its knowledge, productive capacity, and conditions of use are jointly governed by participants, the same machine may reduce the labor each needs to sustain a good life.

Many fears about AI taking jobs are therefore also fears about control. People may be permitted to use AI while having no effective say over the institutions that determine what it does to their lives.

### Taking Concerns About the Future Seriously

Some fears arise because AI is developing rapidly and its behavior is not yet well understood or predictable. Better understanding, design, and testing may resolve many of these difficulties. People once feared unfamiliar phenomena and technologies; today we do not reject cars or aircraft simply because their workings are complex. Well-designed systems can earn trust through knowledge and repeated verification.

Cars and aircraft also show that trust requires an ongoing process. Sound design, manufacturing quality, appropriate operating conditions, and the ability to investigate failures all matter. AI will require its own continuing accumulation of evidence and feedback, supported by both people and machines.

Questions about the future should therefore state their assumptions. An isolated model controlled by a few actors, lacking adequate perception and resistant to correction, generates particular risks. My proposal seeks to change those conditions: connect sensing and action, give participants control, and expose judgments to feedback from results. We should investigate what problems remain after those changes, rather than assume every defect of the current arrangement is permanent.

Equally, “future AI will be stronger” is not an institutional design. Shared knowledge, personal participation, decision procedures, and resource allocation must be specified. The next chapter examines how a network of dynamic weights might connect many individuals' knowledge and actions into a collective capacity.

## A Social Neural Network with Dynamic Decision Weights

Hierarchical organizations often separate knowledge from authority. Frontline workers understand immediate needs yet have little say over the policies and practices affecting them. Senior leaders hold authority while knowing less about those particulars. Decisions also require a wider view, so the solution cannot simply reverse the hierarchy. We need an appropriate distribution of influence between local knowledge and overall coordination.

I propose dynamically adjusting the weight given to judgments in relevant domains. Predictions that prove more accurate, and accounts or proposals that work better, could receive greater influence in comparable decisions.

Why do those familiar with a problem so often lack authority, while those with authority lack familiarity? Positions organize information and action, but they also tend to bundle judgment across many domains into the hands of one person. A maintenance manager may be excellent at budgeting without knowing more than a technician about an unusual noise. The technician's knowledge of a machine does not settle the organization's finances. We need both kinds of knowledge in the places where they are relevant, rather than one person declared right about everything.

### Freeing Decision Functions from Fixed People and Positions

In the social neural network I envisage, decision functions would not belong permanently to particular elites or groups. They would be performed through rules and algorithms developed from collective knowledge. I hope this could provide a form of social organization better suited than capitalism to the automation of intellectual work.

Replacing an official with an AI changes little if that AI alone sees the information, interprets the rules, and announces the outcome. However capable it may be, everyone else still waits for its decision.

In a shared knowledge network, many people can contribute to one capability. A rule can preserve its proposal, testing, and revision history. A plan can combine reliable judgments from several domains. Decision structures can form around tasks, change with evidence, and be replaced by better constructions. People still contribute and act, but contributions need not be enclosed within fixed offices, nor rules fused with someone's personal authority.

That fusion is itself a problem. Correcting a policy can threaten a leader's standing or reputation, making it difficult to separate improving a rule from repudiating its author. Dynamic decision-making must loosen that connection.

### Learning from the Example of Spam Detection

The proposed network would let human and artificial intelligences participate, create, and offer judgments in areas they choose. A common initial basis for evaluating contributions could be revised through feedback in the relevant domain, rather than inherited from rank or status.

This contrasts with strongly fixed arrangements. Dictatorships and oligarchies concentrate influence so heavily that recognizing and correcting errors can become extraordinarily difficult, sometimes requiring upheaval. Representative systems adjust important positions through periodic elections, but those cycles can still respond slowly to particular errors and changing circumstances.

Better collective decisions require better opportunities to understand situations, exchange information, and judge evidence. Recording and analyzing useful information is an evolving objective, not a claim that everything can be collected at once. Information relevant to cooperation can be shared appropriately, while details contained within components and irrelevant to outside interactions need not be exposed.

Two directions support each other. Digital records make evidence available, traceable, and verifiable. AI can improve people's access to knowledge and reasoning tools. Fairness here does not require identical interests, experience, or judgment. It requires real opportunities to understand, express views, learn, and check conclusions. Some people attain extraordinary depth in a narrow field; others connect fields. Effective communication can make those abilities complementary. These conditions provide a basis for studying dynamic decision-making, whose effectiveness must still be tested in concrete tasks.

Defined computational problems can use deterministic programs. Modest uncertainty may be addressed through collaboration among AI agents. More complex social, political, and economic questions require combining judgments while preserving their uncertainty and differences.

For such questions, I propose examining participants' relevant records, assigning task-specific weights, comparing alternative proposals, and testing them through simulations or appropriately bounded real-world trials. Feedback would inform both broader adoption and later judgment weights.

Spam detection provides one inspiration. A message's title, sender, text, and links supply clues, but one word does not settle the classification. A previously trusted address may have been compromised. The system combines evidence and changes in response to new messages and user corrections.

The lesson is to avoid making one signal permanently authoritative. Applied to people, this does not mean assigning a lifelong label of “reliable” or “unreliable.” Expertise in machinery does not imply expertise in crop disease. A method that works in one environment may fail in another. Weight belongs to a particular problem, domain, and set of conditions.

Suppose three technicians attribute a fault to bearings, circuitry, or installation. Record their judgments, reasons, and observations before the repair. Afterward, examine what actually failed and which evidence mattered. That can improve future judgments; it cannot justify granting the correct predictor more power over every issue.

A new machine model may require recalibration. Someone answering only easy questions cannot be compared directly with a person taking on difficult cases. “Dynamic” includes changing conditions and meaningful comparisons, not merely adding or subtracting points each day.

### Personal AI as a Practical Means of Participation

People already record experience in instructions that AI can use. A more comprehensive personal knowledge space could preserve skills, preferences, settings, views, and their development, with privacy boundaries and traceable ownership. An authorized AI could use that record to help form responses to shared questions, asking the person to participate directly when the matter is important or requires a fresh choice.

A common knowledge foundation could preserve broadly usable information and its development. Above it, a personal model could learn from records the person chooses to retain, supporting their own interests and expression. Routine matters could be delegated within authorization; important choices would be discussed with the person. Those processes could themselves be recorded, informing later learning and assessments of judgment.

This changes what universal participation might mean. It need not require everyone to read every file, attend every meeting, and vote on every detail—a demand incompatible with ordinary work, learning, and care.

A personal AI could identify how an issue affects its user, which preferences are already established, and where new choices arise. It can handle familiar routines within existing permission while explaining disputed reasons and consequences when the person must decide. Participation can vary with interest and circumstance without excluding people for lack of time or specialist vocabulary.

The person must also remain free to change. A personal model should record how judgments developed, not turn past preferences into a permanent personality verdict. Learning, changed circumstances, and reconsideration all belong in later decisions.

### Feedback Matters More Than a One-Time Authoritative Judgment

More capable coordination can supplement or replace older mechanisms without making every lower-level process obsolete. Different levels handle different tasks.

Markets and ordinary negotiations can coordinate much daily activity without a high-level decision each time, somewhat as bodily regulation continues without constant conscious attention. But more complex interactions may require deliberate coordination. Driving combines trained responses with awareness that conditions have changed. A civilization likewise needs ways to decide when decentralized adjustment is sufficient and when wider planning is required.

The deliberate intelligence I mean is collective: people contribute understanding and proposals, and a shared process develops plans that can be implemented, tested, and revised. It would not simply leave every outcome to uncoordinated competition.

Long-term commitment and rapid correction can coexist. A railway takes years of coordinated work; it cannot be redesigned whenever someone's mood changes. Yet new geology, demand, or cost evidence may make the original plan costly to retain.

A sound commitment includes criteria for recognizing change and reconsidering the plan. Shared memory preserves why a decision was made; feedback asks whether those reasons still hold. Correction can then become part of execution instead of waiting for a change in the highest office.

### Could Weights Become Privileges Again?

Ability differences and the accumulation of power through unequal interaction are not the same phenomenon. But abilities can accumulate through learning, and differences can be substantial. Preventing domination cannot rest on assuming that abilities are similar, normally distributed, or inherently unable to accumulate. It depends on whether rules allow an advantage to become lasting control over others.

The problematic loop is one in which winning increasingly grants the right to set the rules. If past success brings control over funding, information, and entry, a newcomer with better judgment may never reach a fair comparison. “Superior ability” then becomes entangled with resource and status advantages.

Dynamic weighting must therefore be considered alongside common knowledge, personal tools, and resource allocation. New participants need opportunities to learn and test their judgments. Failures must not be visible only when weaker participants make them, and successful actors must not monopolize the means of validating their own results.

Someone with a high weight may also hide errors to retain it. The scoring mechanism itself needs feedback. Finding one's own mistake, supplying a counterexample, and helping correct a rule are useful contributions. A system that rewards only appearing never to be wrong encourages concealment; recognizing correction makes changing one's mind less costly.

Authority to revise the weighting rules must not sit in an office immune to evaluation. The rules should themselves be research objects: what data do they use, where do they work, what feedback do they miss, and what would alternatives change? Candidate mechanisms can be compared on appropriate tasks before receiving broader roles. Dynamic revision must extend to the mechanisms that generate the weights.

### Fair Conditions for Development, Diverse Abilities, and Cooperation Across Fields

A rational social order should pursue two goals.

First, make opportunities for education and training, equipment, and basic support more equitable. Reduce the unfair effects of accumulated external resources on personal development, so people can develop abilities in keeping with their characteristics and interests. Ability formation involves individual differences, practice, time, and the conditions under which learning actually occurs.

Second, give people enough information about themselves and the world to discover their strengths, together with the time and freedom to explore and develop what interests them.

Fairness must mean usable conditions. Equal hours on the clock do not mean equal discretionary time. Someone burdened by insecurity and unsupported care work may have little opportunity to study even when materials are free. Education adapted to a person requires both suitable methods and the chance to use them.

The objective is therefore not to make ability approximate a normal distribution. It is to help people with different abilities communicate and cooperate effectively, bringing specialized depth and breadth of understanding together in serious work across disciplines.

#### Institutions Shape the Conditions in Which Abilities Grow

Resources that can accumulate—including capital—play a substantial role in acquiring and training abilities. Under institutions where those resources compound unevenly, the abilities people develop may themselves be shaped by persistent inequalities.

The effect is not limited to giving equally capable people different incomes or recognition. It can produce real differences in acquired ability. One child has time, suitable teachers, and repeated opportunities to experiment; another cannot enter the field at all. Their later skill gap cannot simply be assigned to initial biological differences. The gap may be real while still requiring an explanation of its formation.

Research on skill formation models interactions among early investment, existing skills, and subsequent learning. It supports investigating how development conditions shape abilities, while also reminding us that learning itself can accumulate. It would be wrong to claim that abilities never do. [Research on skill formation](https://jenni.uchicago.edu/papers/Cunha-Heckman_AER_v97n2_2007.pdf)

Under some allocation rules, early resources improve training, acquired skills support achievement, and achievement brings further resources and recognition. Repeated preference for prior winners can amplify initially modest differences. The final distribution reflects that process; observing it alone does not separate resource accumulation from initial ability.

A regression-discontinuity study of Dutch research funding found that applicants just above an initial funding threshold obtained more than twice as much subsequent funding over eight years as applicants just below it. This supports the possibility that early opportunity amplifies later resource differences. It does not show that the recipients were twice as able. [Research on cumulative advantage in funding](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5948972/)

Institutional fairness therefore concerns entry, sustained training, and opportunities to try again after failure, not only final scores. The shape of an ability distribution remains an empirical question. Institutions should reduce unjustified barriers and help capabilities cooperate without prescribing a curve that everyone must eventually fit.

I want knowledge, tools, training, and basic support to become genuinely accessible. Accumulated knowledge should help more people learn; one person's development should expand opportunities for others. Useful accumulation of knowledge and skill should be encouraged and shared. Accumulating control through possession and institutional advantage requires constraints.

#### Different Ability Profiles Can Complement One Another

People differ in how readily their interests shift and how long they can sustain effort in one direction. Some develop exceptional depth in a narrow area. Others know something about many areas without reaching the same depth in any one of them.

How much these differences owe to inheritance, development, training, or choice remains open to research. Cooperation need not wait for a complete explanation of every difference. Nor should a test or prediction determine a person's only permissible path. Self-understanding can develop through experimentation, feedback, and new choices.

There is no need to reduce every ability to one total score and use it to rank human worth. A person who reaches exceptional detail and a person who sees relationships across problems contribute differently within particular collaborations. Task-specific comparisons can coexist with diverse ability profiles.

Those working deeply in a field can uncover difficulties that general discussion misses and test precise solutions. Those connecting fields can understand different questions and conditions, translate between forms of expression, divide a larger goal into coordinated tasks, and check that the parts work together.

These are roles, not permanent categories of people. A specialist in one problem may connect several directions in a larger project. A broadly oriented collaborator may develop depth through sustained study. Roles should change with problems and personal growth. Freedom to develop includes changing direction, expanding an interest, and beginning again.

#### Deep Cooperation Requires Connecting Each Field's Conditions

Consider an energy-storage device. Materials researchers examine performance; engineers examine structure and operation; manufacturers examine production; users care about cost and actual needs. A local performance gain may make manufacture difficult. A cheaper process may undermine the function users value most. Connecting work reveals those relationships; specialist work resolves the relevant questions in depth.

A performance number without temperature, lifespan, or manufacturing conditions can make a local result look like a finished system. Conversely, an engineering constraint that cannot reach researchers leaves collaboration stuck at an information boundary. Shared records should preserve not just conclusions, but their conditions, unresolved questions, and dependencies on other choices.

Coordination should not create an indispensable gatekeeper. Within the authorized collaboration, others should be able to trace task divisions, requirements, and reasons for decisions. Work should continue when a coordinator leaves. Alternative ways of connecting the work should be open to proposal and testing.

In the proposed system, AI helps explain concepts, retrieve relevant knowledge, and suggest overlooked connections. A Merkle-DAG preserves relationships among questions, evidence, revisions, and contributions. Dynamic weights assign influence on particular questions while allowing challenge and correction. The coordination process remains understandable and replaceable.

These functions need validation in real tasks. Participants must check that an AI's explanation preserves the original conditions and meaning. Recorded contributions still require interpretation in light of evidence. The knowledge system should make cooperation inspectable and extendable, reducing the need to guess what others have already done.

#### Putting Expertise to Work for Shared Creation

Tailored development, complementary abilities, and trust may reduce opportunities for domination, but only through concrete arrangements. People need conditions for growth; expertise needs room to contribute; neither should automatically become general authority over others.

A materials expert may offer better-founded judgments about a test without thereby deciding what costs or risks every user must accept. A skilled organizer should not acquire permanent control over entry because they possess a broad view. The problem, the scope of evidence, and the needs of affected people must all enter the decision.

More agents mean more tools, not more independent people or additional basic rights for their owner. A public task may require substantial computation, but those resources belong to the task arrangement rather than a manager's permanent personal share. Task weights should change with evidence and feedback. Basic support, access to personal data, and changes to common rules cannot be settled by one professional score. Preventing domination then becomes an inspectable institutional relationship, independent of the population's statistical distribution.

The system must also address malicious interference and falsification. Distributed ledgers suggest the value of stating what an attacker controls, how honest participants verify records, and how disagreement is handled.

But “less than half the computing power is malicious” has meaning only within a particular protocol and its assumptions. The Bitcoin white paper studies an attacker's catch-up probability in proof-of-work chains and requires valid transactions. It does not show that a minority of computing power cannot steal keys, falsify initial observations, or misuse authorized access. Content verification, history consistency, identity, permission, and execution need distinct designs. A ledger's threshold cannot simply be transferred to all of society. [The Bitcoin white paper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf)

Useful measures include whether relevant people can find each other, misunderstandings are detected, expert judgments are tested, and others can continue the resulting work. We should also ask whether previously excluded people obtain real development opportunities, whether cross-field cooperation reduces repeated explanation and rework, and whether participants can sustain cooperation while retaining autonomy. Ability distributions may still be measured, but they do not replace these evaluations of actual lives and shared creation.

In the society I envisage, everyone participates in governing and is affected by governance; everyone can be both producer and consumer, student and teacher. Shared affairs are managed through the knowledge and judgment generated and revised by participating members.

That raises another immediate question. If someone's judgment receives less weight, should their livelihood shrink too? If someone makes a major specialist contribution, should they permanently control more common resources? These questions require a distinction between meeting personal needs and allocating collective resources.

## Personal Needs and Collective Resources

Discussions of fair allocation often jump immediately to “How much should each person receive?” A more basic question comes first: what is the resource being allocated for?

A meal requires grain, and so does a city's emergency reserve. A home requires space, as do transport and energy infrastructure. The material may be similar while its function differs. Confusing the two makes it easy to treat control of productive resources as an entitlement to greater personal consumption, or to turn a wish for a better life into a claim to unlimited authority.

### What a Person Uses and What a Community Must Organize

A group's wealth serves two broad purposes. Some sustains its members and meets their material and intellectual needs. Some supports the group's shared operation and development. Organizing the latter requires judgment and coordination.

Governments and companies commonly organize this work through hierarchies. Unequal power, reinforced through repeated interaction, can concentrate resources and authority. People at the top may combine professional ability with accumulated control over capital and decisions. Selecting someone capable to coordinate a task can indeed outperform selection that ignores competence; this helps explain why such organizations persist.

The difficulty is the next step: why should a coordinating function become a permanent personal privilege? An engineer's expertise deserves attention when planning an electricity network. It does not make the electricity the engineer's private possession. A research program may legitimately require substantial resources because of its task, not because its director needs that amount for personal living.

Distinguishing purposes separates “What do I need for health, learning, and life?” from “What must we organize to accomplish this shared task?” The first requires understanding a person; the second requires understanding the task and its effects.

I propose distinguishing resources for personal needs from resources for collective activity. Many personal uses have appropriate ranges rather than benefiting without limit from greater quantities. More food is not always healthier. Abundant information can become an obstacle when immediate stimulation displaces considered choice. Constant consumption of short videos, for example, need not provide the intellectual nourishment a person seeks.

Too little water, food, rest, or stimulation can harm a person; excess can also cause problems. The intensity and duration of thought, communication, and other activity likewise have practical limits and conditions. Viewed more abstractly, a person is an open system exchanging energy and resources with an environment. Individual needs vary, and their appropriate conditions can be investigated. Enjoyable food does not justify unlimited calories; enjoyable games or films do not make continuous immersion beneficial.

Meeting needs therefore means neither giving everyone an identical amount nor awarding the largest share to whoever makes the largest demand. A child, an adult, and someone requiring particular care may need different arrangements. One uniform allowance can be superficially equal and materially unfair.

Nor should a statistical curve prescribe people's lives. Needs depend on the resource, population, and circumstances. Understanding them should help support people and their development. We must also distinguish an appropriate amount of consumption from the cost of meeting a need. A finite appetite says little about the resources required for medical treatment, care, accessibility equipment, or learning. People needing greater support should not be excluded for departing from an average.

Limited personal consumption does not itself prevent resources or power from concentrating. Preventing concentration from becoming domination still requires rules for the purposes of common resources, the scope of authority, review, and the ability to leave an arrangement.

### Collective Productive Resources Are Different from Personal Consumption

Land illustrates the distinction. Airports, stations, roads, energy systems, mines, factories, and urban development require it. These infrastructures play roles comparable to a civilization's circulation, respiration, and metabolism.

Looking at each parcel separately may obscure the need for a railway crossing many places. Invoking the railway's collective value while ignoring those who live along the route creates the opposite danger: “the whole” becomes an excuse to impose losses on particular people.

Considering both kinds of allocation requires recognizing both infrastructure and affected lives. A shared plan must include those circumstances, not delete the people from its calculation and then announce an optimum.

Collective intelligence should therefore assess who benefits, who bears costs, which harms can be avoided, and what alternatives exist. A community consists of concrete people; its resources should serve them through their shared life.

In existing arrangements, competition, political power, and sometimes conquest determine control. Unequal bargaining positions can produce unequal distributions, which accumulate through repeated interactions. Capital and authority remain attached to particular individuals, organizations, or states, and can become increasingly concentrated.

Such disparities also shape information. Those above may know much about those below while remaining opaque themselves. Unequal visibility and limited sharing can intensify defensive behavior and conflict, reducing the effectiveness of cooperation.

In the proposed arrangement, personal resources would be allocated with attention to differing needs and well-being. Collective resources would be governed through a shared, revisable process of dynamic judgment. The neural-network analogy concerns coordination through many connected participants and changing influence, rather than permanent possession of a superior position.

### Why Would Those Who Contribute More Join?

People and groups often seek exclusive control over wealth and resources because more seems to promise greater benefit and security. Yet even powerful actors remain exposed to uncertainty and rivalry. They defend against enemies, distrust allies, and fear losing their position. Great wealth or political office does not remove the risks of interaction under incomplete information and imperfect judgment.

Possessing more and living better are not always identical. A powerful person may spend more effort guarding their position. An organization hoarding knowledge may miss discoveries it could have made with others.

An alternative that merely asks advantaged people to give things up, without explaining what they can gain, is unlikely to attract voluntary participation. The question is whether better cooperation can produce capabilities and conditions that benefit everyone, including those who previously held an advantage.

Someone might surrender exclusive control over productive resources while retaining ample support for a healthy, satisfying life. More equitable arrangements might reduce insecurity and defensive costs. Faster scientific and productive development could also create benefits unavailable under the old arrangement.

Consider a deliberately hypothetical example. Suppose the world's richest person owns ten trillion dollars and is sixty years old. Under one institutional path, a powerful life-extension technology takes forty years to develop; their expected lifespan is ninety. Under another, they retain the resources needed for a good life but relinquish exclusive control over the productive fortune, participating instead as a contributor to collective judgment. Suppose—only for the purpose of the comparison—that improved cooperation brings the technology in twenty years. They could then benefit at eighty. Which arrangement would they choose?

The dates are not forecasts. The thought experiment asks whether exclusive possession remains the only rational interest when personal benefit depends on what civilization can create. No amount of money buys something not yet invented. Better cooperation might offer more than greater isolated ownership.

But promises are insufficient. The new arrangement should begin with services it can actually provide, letting participants observe costs, experience, and productive capacity. They can decide how far to participate on the basis of results, rather than first abandon their lives and wait for a distant promise.

### A Judgment Score Should Not Determine a Person's Livelihood

If a participant makes an incorrect judgment, that result may change the weight given to their future predictions in comparable circumstances. It should not deprive them of food, learning, and basic living conditions. Otherwise admitting error becomes a threat to survival and concealment a strong incentive.

Likewise, a major contribution deserves a clear record and recognition of the relevant ability. It should not automatically purchase permanent authority across every domain. That would collapse the two kinds of resources into one again.

Separating personal needs from collective resources changes these incentives. Basic support creates room to acknowledge uncertainty, admit mistakes, and explore new directions. Shared resources are allocated according to tasks, participants' views, and feedback. Learning and correction can become continuing activities instead of contests for the right to survive.

A mathematician with an idea, an entrepreneur with a concept, or an independent game developer with a new mechanic may currently keep it secret to protect recognition and income. They may try to do everything themselves, even where others could supply mature methods more efficiently. The new insight then develops slowly through duplicated effort and limited exchange.

If sharing no longer threatens a person's livelihood or erases their contribution, they have more reason to bring the problem into a shared network early. Others can do what they do best, and the result becomes material for further work. Personal security, knowledge circulation, and productive capacity can begin reinforcing one another.

The distinction is therefore not an optional welfare provision appended to the theory. It affects why people provide accurate information, cooperate, admit errors, and contribute to collective intelligence. Resource allocation, dynamic judgment, common knowledge, and open organization are connected parts of one proposed mechanism.

## Opening Products, Organizations, and Decisions

My proposal goes beyond the ability to retrieve one's data at any time. I want software and services supplied by a new kind of organization to become viable replacements for conventional corporate products. This goes further than using open-source software. The entities that design, produce, and operate the services would themselves be open: their organization, allocation rules, and decision algorithms would be available for participants to examine, trace, develop, and govern together.

### Who Controls the Power to Control AI?

The central question is who controls control of AI. An open model operated by a conventionally hierarchical company can leave the same power imbalance intact. Those at the top decide how the institution works; employees and users have much less influence over decisions and benefits.

Open code lets people inspect a program and, if able, modify it. It does not necessarily let them determine prices, data use, or resource priorities. A user may download the code without owning enough hardware to run it. A developer may change a local copy without being able to alter a service's operating rules.

Opening code therefore does not complete the transformation of the relationship. The proposed openness extends along the chain from knowledge to product, from product to service, and from service to the organization that governs it.

A personal digital space could give each person a basis for control. A genuinely open AI system could then be governed by the people who build and use it, through institutions of equal standing and revisable, task-specific influence. The aim is to remove the conditions under which another node can exploit unequal authority over AI to harm a participant. I want the capabilities of common intelligence to be connected to the interests of everyone taking part.

### Consumers Can Also Be Producers

Complex open-source systems illustrate the advantages of participation beyond a firm's employment boundary. A large project needs diverse abilities that one company may struggle to recruit or contract efficiently. Interested users around the world can contribute code, report problems, test changes, or supply practical feedback.

Production and consumption need not belong to permanently separate groups. Someone who cannot program may know exactly where a tool fails in daily life. A translator can make it understandable to more people. A user who reproduces a fault turns a vague complaint into a solvable problem. Each helps shape the product.

Participation should therefore have several recognized forms, without becoming a compulsory price of access. Some people can contribute considerable time; others can offer occasional feedback; some simply need a dependable service for now.

This differs from conventional crowdfunding in several ways. Users may contribute work as well as money. There need be no funding threshold before activity begins: I can start alone, with a simple storage service and a small affordable budget. People can participate at different levels as their confidence and circumstances allow. Regardless of their contribution, users retain sovereignty over their data.

### Sharing Work Without Erasing Its Contributors

Git and associated issue and discussion systems preserve concrete changes and exchanges in software development. A final list of names tells us much less about who did what. Version history provides a more detailed basis for understanding contributions than a rough ranking or a private agreement among collaborators.

In the proposed system, resources for basic personal needs would be treated separately from contribution scores. Recognition would therefore increasingly concern acknowledgment and honor rather than a contest for subsistence. Traceable work can support fairer recognition.

Recording work and judging its value remain distinct. Fixing a crucial defect may take one line; adding an unnecessary feature may take thousands. Identifying a problem, exposing a mistaken assumption, implementing a solution, and supplying the first real-world feedback are different contributions. Line counts or message totals cannot resolve their value.

Detailed records allow later reassessment. Whose material informed a conclusion? Which implementation did a program use? Why did a failure redirect the work? Our understanding of those contributions can improve, rather than remain fixed by the first person to announce the result.

Extending this practice beyond software suggests something like version control for civilization's intellectual development. A Merkle-DAG could preserve grounds for recognizing contributions across research and creative work. With greater security of basic needs, people would have less reason to protect their livelihoods and recognition through secrecy and isolated effort.

Information barriers constrain what groups can accomplish together. More reciprocal access and traceable acknowledgment offer one route out of cooperative traps. I hope a system of shared innovation with preserved attribution can become preferable to mechanisms centered on racing for exclusive claims. Its assessments should remain open to discussion, grounded in a richer history of the work.

### From Competing Brands to Building Products Together

Products could be described through composable functions and implementations, rather than only brands and model tiers. A phone, for example, might be understood through its modules and the conditions under which they work together. Users could choose or customize functions more directly, while contributors improve components within their expertise.

A better sensor, a software improvement, or a configuration suited to older users need not always wait to be acquired, packaged, and released through one brand's entire product cycle. If functions and conditions are explicit, an improvement can travel to compatible combinations. Different implementations can coexist, with contributions traced through dependencies.

Shared standards can therefore enlarge diversity. People need not rebuild an entire factory before they can improve one part. This is one practical direction through which I hope to reduce exploitation and dependence on organizations with steep power gaps, while giving relevant expertise a meaningful role.

### Growth Should Expand Capability, Not Monopoly

From the beginning, the organization would distinguish personal needs from collective resources, preserve necessary public records, and develop shared decision procedures. Externally, it could initially operate through an ordinary company and compete in existing markets. Growth might involve attracting participants, incorporating firms, or transforming existing organizations. Internally, its direction would remain toward more equitable distribution, better information, and greater cooperation.

One person can begin with decisions made by that person. As others join, the organization must develop genuinely shared allocation and decision-making. The aspiration is that expansion produces the opposite of monopolistic enclosure: more usable knowledge and productive capacity for participants.

Whether it does so must be judged by what happens. If each new participant enlarges the founder's arbitrary authority, old-style power is growing. If knowledge, equipment, and services improve while members retain understanding, participation, their records, and meaningful choices, growth remains aligned with the purpose.

Openness must therefore begin when the project is small. Shared records, explicit rules, and attribution are not decorations to add after success. They shape how a personal experiment becomes a larger organization.

For the first participant, the most immediate step is to regain control over data and tools. A person no longer trapped in another organization's information silo can become an autonomous participant in new cooperation.

## Personal Data Sovereignty, Privacy, and Common Knowledge

When an AI service gives priority to resources that support paid inference while exporting users' records remains a lower priority, that allocation reveals whose interests carry weight. Unequal control between provider and customer affects what the customer can actually do.

The first task of the proposed framework is therefore to give each person local and cloud computing and storage under their own effective control. Personal records scattered across commercial services should be recoverable into that space. REA—the proposal to understand existing systems in reverse and reconnect what a person can rightfully use—serves this purpose.

Being able to view data inside a platform is not the same as controlling it. A person may browse records but be unable to move them into another tool. They may find individual messages but struggle to connect years of thinking about a question. Ending a subscription or changing devices may disrupt their whole way of working.

The fragmentation concerns more than files. Knowledge, preferences, relationships, and creations are divided among accounts. Each provider knows a part, while the person must search, request exports, and reconstruct their own history.

A personal space should restore the ability to choose tools, retain copies, and decide how material is used. It may span local devices and cloud services, but its control relationships should be organized around the person.

### Digital Space Is Part of Personal Capability

The same framework can preserve research and communication as well as data and code. A personal agent could connect tasks previously scattered among operating systems, websites, applications, and file managers.

A person might choose to retain conversations, work, ideas, observations, assets, capabilities, journals, notes, writing, or recordings. Future interfaces might add other kinds of record. The image is a personal flight recorder: a history kept according to the person's choices, rather than a requirement to capture everything indiscriminately.

An agent could gradually learn about its user's interests, commitments, needs, rights, obligations, and relationships. It could then help the person reason and participate in economic, political, and social activity, reducing disadvantages caused by limited information or specialist knowledge.

Repeated discussion of a question illustrates why relationships matter. A later statement may add a condition, answer an objection, or revise an explanation. Keeping only the latest conclusion hides why it was reached. Keeping all the files without provenance and links makes the useful parts hard to find.

The personal space should therefore retain exact statements, responses, acceptance, rejection, and uncertainty as distinct records. Changing one's mind should not require pretending the earlier view never existed. Memory should support learning rather than freeze a person into a past version.

### The Power Relationships Behind Privacy

How much of our concern about privacy arises from fear of how information can be used? Between people, companies, and states, withholding information often protects against a more powerful actor gaining an advantage and inflicting harm. Power is therefore an important part of the explanation.

Imagine equal standing and complete confidence that disclosed information will not be used against either party. Many forms of defensive concealment would lose their purpose. Greater equality and trust can create conditions for fuller exchange and cooperation.

Close personal relationships illustrate the interaction. Knowing more about each other can support cooperation; trusting cooperation can encourage further openness. Prejudice and deliberate concealment can instead widen separation. Families and partnerships provide familiar examples, without implying that intimacy eliminates all personal boundaries.

Under severe political or economic domination, information may flow mainly upward. Those below fear disclosure; those above seek more information while keeping their own affairs hidden. An employee may hesitate to describe a workplace difficulty for fear of evaluation or dismissal, while management collects detailed records without explaining their use. Asking for “full sharing” in such a relationship can simply deepen one-way transparency.

The important question is who can use information to do what. If shared rules, resources, and decisions are open to participation, mistakes can be studied, contributions recognized, and disclosure can improve the contributor's own circumstances. The incentive to share then changes. I want to reduce the conditions that make secrecy necessary for self-protection.

The proposed separation of resources matters here. Support for personal life and development should respond to actual physiological, psychological, care, and learning needs, while considering the conditions and common resources required to meet them. Collective productive resources would be governed through shared dynamic judgment. As those arrangements become dependable, more relevant observations and ideas could be shared without making basic security depend on winning every contest over information.

### A Complete Personal Record Is Not Universal Public Access

“I can retain my experience” and “everyone must see my entire experience” are different arrangements. A diary can remain in personal space. When one idea becomes relevant to shared research, the appropriate material can be brought into that work. A community does not acquire unconditional access to someone's life because the person has a fuller memory.

This is compatible with more useful sharing. Shared tasks need relevant information and adequate grounds for judgment. Internal processes without external effects need not become public simply because they can be recorded.

A service improvement report may include the problem, reproduction steps, and practical consequences without unrelated private messages. Trust may encourage voluntary disclosure of further material. Openness should be a choice enabled by greater control, not obedience produced by losing it.

A distributed identity and permission system is therefore essential. Its participants include people using real, pseudonymous, or anonymous identities; AI instances; devices; automated programs; and organizations composed from those actors. Their delegation and cooperation relationships require explicit representation. Authentication, authorization, and provenance need a coherent layer of their own if capabilities are not to return to the control of a small set of authorities.

### Traceable Memory for People Who Continue to Change

The Merkle-DAG framework interests me as a way to identify and verify records of structures and changes: data and code, revision histories, execution and data flows, and the actions of people and computers.

An evolving system's performance depends partly on its ability to trace changes and recover from errors. That affects how quickly useful feedback accumulates and how well failures can be understood and repaired.

Content addressing allows retrieved data to be checked against an expected identifier without prescribing which company must store it. Separating identity from location supports retaining copies and changing providers. [IPLD's introduction to content addressing](https://ipld.io/docs/intro/hello-world/)

Verification must be combined with actual access controls. Hashing a private file does not encrypt it. Encrypting a connection does not prevent its recipient from reading the content. Public content-addressed networks may also reveal clues about who requests or provides material. Personal records therefore need appropriate encryption, key control, and access arrangements. [IPFS on privacy and encryption](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/privacy-and-encryption/)

These arrangements should let memory travel with a person without trapping the person in it. An old opinion can remain historical while a current position points to a revision. Permissions granted to a tool can change. Already distributed public copies must be acknowledged honestly; a delete button cannot make everyone forget.

Someone who can preserve, understand, revise, and move their records has a practical basis for participating in a common network. Without that capacity, collective intelligence remains dependent on the organizations controlling access to memory. People surrender ever more of their lives and wait for someone else to decide how those lives are returned to them.

Data sovereignty is therefore more than a download feature. It concerns continuity of knowledge, tools, and participation. A modest storage service can become the starting point for reconnecting a person's digital life and developing new relationships of cooperation.

## Constraining Power, Preventing Harm, and Building Trust

The new system must do more than encourage constructive developments in society. It must also restrain destructive behavior and protect those who might be harmed.

When a promising idea quickly becomes shared knowledge, civilization gains a new capability. The same capability, used to manipulate, oppress, or dispossess people, may also make harm more efficient. This architecture must therefore address, from the outset, how growing capabilities can support human safety and freedom. Whether someone is willing to bring their knowledge and needs into a shared network also depends on whether they can trust that the information will not become a means of controlling them.

### What are people afraid of when they fear surveillance?

Surveillance can be useful. Why, then, do people fear it? Often, what they fear is the person behind the technology: Is that person's power constrained? Could they use it to hurt me? Many tools have two sides. A knife, a car, or electricity can help people or injure them. The question is how to build protection into the arrangements governing their use.

Images of the same stretch of road can help detect an accident or track an ordinary person's daily life. The same work records can reveal an equipment problem or be taken out of context to punish an employee who raises an objection. People therefore care about more than whether cameras and data exist. Who can see what? What decisions can they make? Can I discover, challenge, and stop those decisions?

Without answers, asking the weaker party to surrender information and trust the stronger party to treat them well asks them to abandon their defenses before receiving any assurance of goodwill. Even where analysis could improve public services, these concerns have a reasonable basis.

Privacy also includes the freedom to arrange one's own life. A person may simply not want others watching them, even without evidence of a particular malicious intention. The new system should make it possible to share with confidence while preserving a space of one's own. The ability to collect information is not, by itself, a reason to collect it.

### How unequal power makes defense and control reinforce each other

Whether the instrument is a knife, AI, nuclear energy, a surveillance system, or any other means by which a person, group, or state can threaten and harm others, a familiar problem arises under a pyramid of power. If the same hierarchy controls the writing, enforcement, and interpretation of rules, as well as the assignment of responsibility, those at the top may harm others for their own benefit and use their position to escape punishment, however detailed the rules appear.

The weaker party may respond by withholding personal information to strengthen their resistance and defenses. Large-scale analysis of that information might benefit them, but if it can also be used against them, withholding it becomes self-protection. This internal tension reduces the efficiency and scope of cooperation. Those above may then respond by expanding surveillance and information gathering, partly in the name of understanding the population and serving national interests, partly to protect their own position. Control and repression become their answer to resistance from below.

From each side's immediate perspective, its actions can be described as defensive: If I do not conceal information, it may be used against me; if I do not tighten control, stability may be lost. Yet one party's additional defenses look like a new threat to the other. Resources that might have solved shared problems are repeatedly diverted into internal attack and defense. Both sides seek security, while the system as a whole may become less secure.

Unequal power and information, and the advantages they confer in distribution, can deepen this distrust. Lost trust increases tension; increased tension encourages those above to rely on a wider power gap to preserve the existing structure; the wider gap, in turn, produces further unfairness. Repeated over time, this becomes a vicious circle.

Marx and Engels connected class struggle to the ownership of the means of production and to labor relations. The cycle of power, information, and distribution examined here can help explain how class conflicts become entrenched. It does not mean that every hierarchical organization embodies the same kind of class relationship. [The Communist Manifesto, Chapter I](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/communist-manifesto/ch01.htm)

Nor does this analysis imply that existing rules and oversight have no effect. The further question is whether oversight remains effective when it reaches those who control the rules, resources, and instruments of enforcement. A mechanism that punishes ordinary people but cannot constrain those able to bypass it offers less protection than the detail of its written rules might suggest.

This helps explain why gaps in power and wealth can widen even as technology, productivity, and social wealth grow. If new capabilities continue to concentrate in a few hands, they may strengthen existing bargaining advantages. Along this path, AI may fail to become the means I envision for reducing barriers to human capability and advancing equality. It could instead become a machine through which a privileged minority controls, oppresses, and exploits the majority.

### Constraining power must include the power to make the rules

A better way to constrain power is to build a better system for distributing it—for assigning decision weights. We cannot rest our hopes on powerful individuals or groups happening to be wise rulers or saints.

The system I envision extends several existing ideas into a broader framework for organizing society: Git and open-source collaboration; the version and provenance records supported by Merkle-DAGs; and distributed digital currencies' experiments in keeping shared accounts. It should help a promising idea grow into an addition to civilization's knowledge and serve society through technology. It must also subject the use of those capabilities to shared constraints.

What needs to be extended is our ability to preserve reasons together, test rules together, and continually revise decisions. A community can learn from these technical and organizational experiences while designing its own arrangements for decision-making and distribution. Possessing more money, equipment, or computing capacity must not, in itself, give someone more authority over others.

Within a neural network of dynamic decision weights, a node may acquire greater influence on a class of questions because its judgments have stood up to scrutiny. That means its relevant judgments deserve greater consideration. It does not give it a passport to inspect anyone's records, alter anyone's life, or rewrite the evaluation rules. Different questions require different knowledge. The needs, evidence, and objections of those affected must also enter the decision.

For example, a node skilled at optimizing road traffic may offer influential advice on traffic management. Its expertise does not authorize it to track a critic. Nor may that node change the protective rules by itself to make an otherwise unauthorized action permissible. Changes to the rules must themselves undergo shared discussion, testing, and authorization, with the reasons before and after the change preserved.

The network must therefore constrain the whole process: observation, proposals, judgment, resource allocation, and action. An algorithm may recommend a plan, but the execution system must still check its authorization, scope, and stopping conditions. Equipment administrators must not retain a permanent back door around those limits. Otherwise, decisions may appear to come from the shared network while real power remains with whoever can override it.

### Preventing both private gain through rule-breaking and injustice within the rules

One important aim is to remove the conditions that sustain exploitation within institutions and corruption outside their rules.

Two different paths require attention. In one, powerful people violate shared rules, conceal where resources go, and benefit themselves or their associates. In the other, the distribution rules themselves allow some people to use their existing position to secure continuing, disproportionate advantages. Conduct of the second kind may comply with current rules and still deserve institutional criticism and reform. My distinction between unfairness “within” and “outside” the rules identifies these paths; it does not force every injustice into one legal or economic definition.

Verifiable records of how shared resources are allocated, used, and evaluated make concealment and diversion easier to detect. If affected people can participate in changing distribution rules, they need not wait for existing beneficiaries to volunteer concessions. But accurate accounts alone are insufficient: a distribution that strongly favors a few can be recorded perfectly. The community must also be able to debate its justification, compare outcomes, and actually change it.

This is why meeting personal needs must be separated from allocating collective resources. A person's lower decision weight on one question must not deprive them of basic living conditions. A node's greater skill at allocating resources does not make those resources its property. Otherwise, people must please those who influence scores and allocations merely to preserve their livelihoods, and new weights become old status hierarchies.

Even the first cloud-storage cooperative can give this principle a concrete form. Equipment providers incur real costs; maintainers perform real work; users have real needs. Everyone should be able to understand how those costs and needs enter the shared arrangement. Financial contributions deserve the recognition agreed in advance. They do not entitle contributors to browse users' files, hold their data hostage, or determine all future rules alone.

### What protection would make surveillance useful to people?

A second aim is to block the paths by which any node could use its decision weight alone to bypass protections and harm the system or an individual within it. This should reduce the resources spent on internal attack and defense as far as possible.

Consider surveillance again. A community seeking to detect road accidents can first agree on what needs to be recognized, which records should be retained, and who may inspect them under which conditions. If routine analysis requires only traffic counts, it should not automatically give everyone involved in traffic management a complete record of each resident's movements. Requests for more information should specify a purpose and be assessed under the rules and by the participants appropriate to that purpose.

This does not mean convening every resident for every alarm. Rules already discussed, tested, and authorized can operate promptly when their conditions are met. Requests beyond the agreed purpose, however, cannot quietly inherit the old permissions. Permission to inspect a recording of one accident must not become permission to track someone indefinitely. Authorized reviewers should be able to establish who made the request, on what grounds, what access was granted, and what was actually accessed.

The records themselves also need protection. Publishing rules, statistics, and accountability procedures does not require putting residents' private footage permanently on the internet. A Merkle-DAG can help verify records and versions; privacy still requires encryption and arrangements for keys and access. IPFS documentation likewise distinguishes transport encryption from content encryption. Content addressing is not itself privacy protection. [IPFS: Privacy and Encryption](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/privacy-and-encryption/)

Those affected also need a review route independent of the person being challenged. A resident who discovers that their data has been repurposed should be able to object, request examination of the access records, and invoke agreed procedures to restrict problematic access. An equipment maintainer cannot also be the only judge of whether they have abused it. Protections must be tested against collusion, compromised devices, and false records, using cross-checks and practical trials to uncover problems and revise authorizations.

Constraining power therefore means both shared decision-making and limits on what actors can actually do. The tests are practical: Can the system stop a node from exceeding its authorized purpose? Can it detect attempts to conceal behavior? Can ordinary people get a problem addressed? As these protections become real, people acquire a sound basis for lowering their defenses.

### Protecting cooperation includes dealing with accidents and mistakes

“Preventing harm by design” is a direction for development: continually removing the ability to injure others arbitrarily through power. It is not a guarantee already achieved. Equipment can fail, judgments can be wrong, and multiple nodes can share the same error. Strong weighted support for a decision does not, on its own, establish that the decision is correct, fair, or safe.

Preventing arbitrary harm also does not promise that everyone's every wish will be fulfilled. Limited collective resources can make plans incompatible. Stopping someone who is harming others may require restricting their actions. Such restrictions need specific grounds, an explanation of their necessity and scope, and independent review. Disagreement, a different identity, or a lower decision weight must not by themselves make someone a target of control.

Uncertain plans can first be tested where their effects are limited and can be stopped, then expanded in response to evidence. Consequences that are difficult to reverse require stronger evidence and tighter constraints. Losses that have already occurred cannot simply be excused by good intentions. We must establish what was known, which rules were followed, whether problems were concealed, which protections failed, and how affected people will receive redress.

Even when cooperation produces an unexpected, temporary injury, a shared mechanism can make the process examinable, stop losses from escalating, and address responsibility and repair. The parties then need not interpret every outcome that differs from expectations as evidence of malice. Cooperation is more likely to survive an accident.

The basis of this trust is understandable: when something goes wrong, the truth can still be pursued, protection still operates, and the weaker party need not bear the entire cost alone. Those harmed retain the right to question what happened and refuse further cooperation. The community must sustain trust by addressing the problem, not demand silence in the name of trust.

### How protection and cooperation can reinforce each other

If these protections repeatedly reduce harm in practice, they can produce a virtuous circle opposite to the earlier vicious one. The harder it becomes for any party to use its powers and tools to harm others arbitrarily, the firmer the basis for safe cooperation. As people repeatedly experience effective protection, they have more reason to trust one another. Trust encourages more voluntary information sharing and deeper, broader cooperation. The more cooperation benefits everyone, the stronger their reasons to preserve it.

Information and trust can then reinforce each other. An employee reports an equipment problem because raising it does not automatically invite punishment. The shared network gains accurate information and a better chance to detect danger early. The problem is solved and the employee's contribution recognized, encouraging timely feedback next time. As more people do this, effort formerly spent concealing problems and guarding against each other can shift toward joint improvement.

The starting point is usable protection. We cannot demand complete trust and disclosure first, then say the mechanism can be fair only after everyone has disclosed everything. Participants can begin with limited cooperation, observe how rules operate, how objections are handled, and how costs and benefits are distributed, then decide whether to expand their involvement. Trust must grow at a pace supported by the protections' actual performance.

Good friends and partners often relate in a similar way: they look after one another, offer explanations, and address misunderstandings together. But such relationships rely largely on personal character, understanding, and habits built over time. They do not automatically furnish equally reliable cooperation among strangers. Good institutions can make these protections available more widely and improve the success and benefits of cooperation. In doing so, they may also help shape people's understanding and behavior, making cooperation easier to learn, honesty easier to sustain, and care for others more practicable.

Character and institutional conditions influence one another. When honest expression is repeatedly punished, people learn concealment. When exploiting status is rewarded, people compete for status. Conversely, when raising problems brings improvement, admitting mistakes enables correction, and cooperation improves one's own life and others', honesty, sharing, and responsibility become more sustainable choices.

I want civilization's collective intelligence both to help good ideas grow quickly and to protect every ordinary person who takes part. Richer shared knowledge strengthens our ability to identify problems and revise rules. More reliable protection encourages people to bring their actual needs and understanding into that knowledge. Encouraging creation and preventing harm are mutually supporting parts of the same cooperative system.

We need not wait for civilization as a whole to change. When one person's cloud storage becomes a service shared by a few people, we can already ensure that private data remains under personal control, common costs can be checked, maintenance permissions have defined limits, and failures can be traced and corrected. Small as that beginning may be, it can embody the protections the new system hopes eventually to provide at a much larger scale.

## Common Knowledge, Reciprocity, and the Growth of Cooperation

Where information systems lack a foundation of shared knowledge, incomplete information and deliberate concealment make it harder to find outcomes that benefit the whole. Participants may become trapped in mutual defense and wasteful competition. Opaque and unequal access to information can also turn suspicion into distrust, emotional hostility, and eventually active obstruction, keeping the psychological and material costs of conflict high throughout the system.

We must distinguish a stable outcome from a better collective outcome. A Nash equilibrium means that, with everyone else's strategies held fixed, no participant can improve their payoff by changing strategy alone. Such an equilibrium may leave everyone worse off than cooperation would. The prisoner's dilemma is a familiar example. My aim is therefore to change the conditions of information, protection, and reward so that cooperation beneficial to our shared lives can endure—not merely to stabilize some existing state. [Equilibrium and efficiency](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~eva/agtchap17.pdf)

### Why concealment costs more than the concealed fact

The recent controversy over a broken brake-pedal bracket in a Maextro vehicle, Boeing's disclosures about the 737 MAX's MCAS and subsequent disputes over safety culture and whistleblowers, and Volkswagen's Dieselgate all raise a question: What does cooperation cost when designers, manufacturers, regulators, and users hold different information and lack reliable ways to check it together?

The evidence differs among these cases. In the Maextro dispute, test conditions, the state of the sample, and the cause of failure require comparable original records and independent examination. The existence of a dispute does not establish deliberate concealment by either party. It does pose an immediate question: How can the public move from conflicting claims to evidence that can be examined jointly? [Reporting on the dispute](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34216478)

In Boeing's case, materials released by the US Department of Justice in 2021 described concealment of important MCAS changes through relevant flight technical personnel, leaving key evaluation documents and associated manuals and training materials without that information. Safety-culture hearings and FAA testimony in 2024 also addressed whether employees could raise safety concerns without fear of retaliation. The information problem thus reaches inside the organization: Can people who know about a problem actually speak about it? [Department of Justice materials](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/boeing-charged-737-max-fraud-conspiracy-and-agrees-pay-over-25-billion), [Senate hearing](https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/subcommittees/investigations/hearings/faa-oversight-of-boeings-broken-safety-culture/), [FAA testimony](https://www.transportation.gov/faa-oversight-boeings-broken-safety-culture-0)

Volkswagen's case more directly exposed a separation between what a test displayed and what happened in use. The EPA's 2015 announcement described software that recognized official emissions tests and activated full emissions controls during them, while behaving differently in ordinary driving. Independent external analysis raised questions, and further investigation revealed the problem. [EPA announcement](https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/newsreleases/epa-california-notify-volkswagen-clean-air-act-violations-carmaker-allegedly-used.html)

Imagine if evidence relevant to safety and responsibility—from design and testing through production, sale, and user feedback—could be traced and checked within appropriate access boundaries, much as versioned open-source code can be examined in Git. Many suspicions now left to speculation could be clarified by inspection. Existing defects would also be more likely to be found and corrected promptly.

Publishing a drawing, however, still leaves the question of whether the physical product was built to it. Publishing a software version does not establish that a device is running that version. Original tests, failure records, reasons for changes, and delivered batches need to be cross-checked. A Merkle-DAG can help verify the content and version relationships among records; physical objects, measurements, and on-site processes still require independent examination. Trust grows because questions can be answered, not because people are told to stop asking them.

### From suspicion to hostility: how mutual defense becomes a shared loss

These are commercial examples, but related problems of information and trust occur throughout political, economic, and social life. Some conflicts threaten civilization itself. The two world wars, the US–Soviet Cold War, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and contemporary tensions between China and the United States all merit examination in terms of information, expectations, and reciprocal responses. Each also has complex historical conditions; opacity alone cannot explain its origins.

The progression from suspicion to distrust, then emotional hostility and active resistance, recurs in many conflicts. One important reason is the widespread human tendency toward reciprocity: I respond to you as you treat me. Under reciprocal responses, trust and cooperation can form a positive chain of reinforcement, while distrust, betrayal, and conflict can form a negative one.

When parties primarily act in this way, inadequate communication or concealment can generate suspicion. Suspicion obstructs communication and encourages further concealment, which deepens suspicion until it becomes distrust. Distrust brings further refusal to communicate and more concealment, then intense hostility. Fearing an attack, one party strengthens its offensive and defensive capabilities; the other responds in kind. Resistance becomes confrontation. In severe cases, localized, low-intensity violence escalates beyond control, drawing ordinary people who once wanted friendly cooperation into fear, hatred, impulsive retaliation, and even bloodshed.

Each side may believe it is merely responding to a threat, while its own defenses appear to the other as preparations for attack. Both act in the name of security, yet the system spends ever more on confrontation. Changing this process requires ways to check together whether a threat is real, what an action means, and how both sides can stop the escalation.

### Why a cooperative start matters—and why it needs error correction

Reciprocity can also move in the other direction. Every increase in truthful, useful, protected communication, and every reduction in concealment that obstructs cooperation, gives trust a chance to grow. Greater trust can encourage more sharing, less concealment, and more willingness to cooperate. The benefits of cooperation then strengthen trust and encourage further cooperation. Through this process, more relevant information suitable for sharing can become common knowledge, helping people identify better collective arrangements and sustain them.

Because reciprocal feedback can reinforce either cooperation or conflict, I draw the following conclusion: if social interaction is to improve our shared lives, its initial direction matters greatly. It is one of the key conditions influencing which way the chain reaction develops.

In game theory, Tit for Tat has a precise meaning: cooperate in the first round, then repeat the other player's previous choice. It performed well in particular studies of repeated games. That does not establish that everyone actually follows it or that it is optimal under all conditions. [Axelrod and Hamilton's study](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf)

Indeed, if both sides mechanically copy the other's previous action, one mistake can trigger alternating retaliation. A intends to cooperate but makes an error; B responds next round; A interprets B's response as a fresh betrayal. Both began willing to cooperate and still become trapped. A cooperative start therefore needs channels for checking facts, explaining behavior, and repairing misunderstandings, so an interaction that has gone astray can return to cooperation.

### Making cooperation, protection, and repair common knowledge

I would therefore like to develop the following rules and make them common knowledge among all participants:

1. Participants share a principle of reciprocity: respond to cooperation with cooperation; respond to evidenced harm with bounded protection; and preserve a path back to cooperation once misunderstandings are clarified and problems repaired. This retains reciprocal response while adding verification and correction to avoid mechanical retaliation.

2. Everyone begins with a cooperative strategy. Where real protections exist, they willingly share information that is necessary and useful for cooperation without harming their own or others' legitimate interests. Sharing can start within a limited scope and expand as experience and protective capabilities grow.

3. Information suitable for publication is recorded and shared as public knowledge through a Merkle-DAG. Private information is handled according to purpose and authorization. Dynamic decision weights and constraints on execution work together to prevent greater influence from becoming permission to use information against the person who shared it.

4. If complexity and uncertainty cause a decision's execution to harm an individual's interests, the system promptly limits further losses, provides necessary assistance, pays compensation under arrangements specified in advance, and revises its strategy and mechanisms in light of an investigation. An adverse outcome alone need not be taken as proof of malicious intent; the person harmed also retains the right to have its cause established. Evidence must distinguish limited capability, forces beyond control, negligence, and deliberate wrongdoing.

5. If a better long-term outcome requires temporary losses, the decision system sets out the proposed plan, expected results, uncertainties, allocation of costs, and stopping conditions in advance. Affected people can participate with adequate information, and the applicable authorization must be obtained. Execution is continuously monitored and adjusted; it stops when continuing no longer meets the agreed conditions. Success brings fulfillment of the agreed commitments. Failure still brings the agreed treatment of compensation and responsibility. Affected people cannot simply be made to bear every loss, nor can failure be declared unrelated to malice or negligence in advance.

For example, a community may plan to migrate records to reduce long-term storage costs. Before migration, it should explain who may temporarily lose service, how old copies will be retained, how new copies will be checked, when the process will stop, and who bears losses. If errors appear, the first priorities are restoring service and protecting data. Investigation then establishes whether the plan was inadequate, execution failed, or someone concealed a known problem. Compensation needs actual resources behind it. Where a loss cannot be repaired, a promise of compensation cannot substitute for protection beforehand.

These arrangements give affected people a practical reason to continue cooperating: they will not be abandoned when something goes wrong. Investigation and accountability need not delay immediate assistance. Protecting individuals and improving the shared system need not be competing choices.

### Making public records and shared decisions dependable

Two features of this framework are especially important.

First, the distributed public-knowledge system built on Merkle-DAGs and the social neural network of dynamic decision weights must serve a common aim: progressively removing the conditions under which individuals can use power to harm others arbitrarily.

Records allow the evidence to be checked; the decision network compares proposals; constraints on execution limit actual effects. All three must work together. Unaltered content is not necessarily true content. Agreement among many nodes does not strip affected people of basic protections. Changes to rules, the conduct of supervisors, and adjustments to weights must also remain reviewable.

Making something common knowledge also requires more than placing it in a public directory. Participants need access to the rules, a way to confirm which version applies to everyone, and an understanding of how to object and check compliance. A personal AI can help explain changes relevant to its user. A notification marked “sent” cannot replace actual understanding or necessary consent.

Second, people should develop a habit of giving reason priority and avoiding impulsive responses before facts are checked. A cooperative initial strategy needs support both in judgment and in feeling. Reason here includes taking fear and anger seriously as responses to people's circumstances, and providing ways to express, examine, and address them.

### Understanding how people develop can weaken the urge to make them permanent enemies

Differences and conflicts among individuals, between individuals and groups, and among groups—in appearance, physiology, personality, understanding, language, behavior, and needs—have histories. These higher-level differences relate to physiological structures, upbringing, social environments, and interactions at different levels. They continue changing through social evolution and repeated interaction. Scientific theory and evidence can help us construct testable causal explanations and identify conditions that can be changed. This gives us a chance to be less governed by fear of what we do not understand and by anger continually reinforced through conflict.

We can also recognize how judgments that fix people or groups as “angels” or “demons,” or turn every disagreement into an absolute struggle between good and evil, may be amplified by these positive and negative feedback processes. Such moralized or religious condemnation can obscure another person's actual circumstances and the possibility of changing the relationship.

Understanding causes does not abolish the need to judge and stop harmful conduct. Where an action causes loss, victims still need protection and responsibility still needs to be established. Understanding the conditions of harm should reduce its recurrence, not silence those already injured. We can oppose an action while leaving room for a person to change. We can respect the importance of morality and faith while rejecting labels that put inquiry and dialogue beyond question.

As this understanding becomes more widely shared, people may experience it as a profound renewal of perspective. Their way of seeing the world, events, and other people may change: a calmer disposition, greater hope for the future, and more tolerance toward those who think differently. A cooperative initial strategy then gains support from both reason and emotion.

This understanding can begin in a small act of cooperation: provide truthful, useful information; let another person see your needs; make protection work in practice; and explain, correct, and repair mistakes. Shared institutions help people accumulate experiences worthy of trust. Those experiences make the next act of voluntary cooperation easier. Knowledge, institutions, and human attitudes can then develop together through the same positive feedback loop.

## Civilization's Technological Power and Its Capacity to Govern Itself

### Positive and negative chains of response are idealized directions

The positive and negative chains associated with reciprocal behavior should first be understood as simplified, idealized models. Phenomena near the two extremes help illustrate them: the love or friendship of lifelong partners who cooperate closely, or a criminal case in which a trivial dispute escalates into lethal violence. Much of ordinary life falls between these extremes. Cooperation and betrayal intermingle, misunderstandings and repairs recur, and outcomes are difficult to predict or control.

Someone may cooperate on one matter and remain defensive on another. A long friendship can include mistaken judgments; a conflict can stop because someone explains or makes amends. The useful question is therefore not how to divide people into permanent cooperators and permanent defectors. It is which conditions move interactions toward cooperation, which push them toward conflict, and whether an interaction can return after going astray. Strategies in repeated games produce different results under different conditions of error, payoff, and selection. That is why mechanisms need comparison. [Research on reciprocity and error correction](https://www.nature.com/articles/364056a0)

### From limited personal experience to cumulative civilizational knowledge

In early civilization, people lacked both sufficiently reliable theoretical guidance and effective means of gathering the information needed for judgment. Through repeated social interaction, human minds—with limited capacity, imperfect long-term memory, and reasoning and calculation slower and more error-prone than machines—collected information about relatively simple or small-scale matters. They generalized, developed experience and theories, then tested, corrected, refined, and extended those theories in practice.

Over this long history came language and writing, printing and drawing, photography and audio and video recording, and eventually computers and AI. Carbon-based biological minds created an entire family of silicon-based computing and intelligence technologies. These greatly expanded the capacity to gather, store, and process information, form abstractions, and create new knowledge, technologies, and tools. Humanity's ability to investigate complex systems and, within limits, intervene in them has grown. We now influence Earth's natural environment and biological systems within our own bodies.

In studying these systems, we can make precise measurements and records, use computers and AI to gather richer data, and combine computational power with AI-assisted reasoning and abstraction to understand mechanisms and improve prediction and intervention. Computers have deepened our systematic understanding of geology, oceans, and weather, improving forecasts for some phenomena. Computers and AI have also advanced our understanding of heredity, protein structures, and genetic systems, enabling prediction, modification, and intervention in particular tasks and conditions.

Prediction and control remain distinct. Forecasting the weather does not mean controlling it at will. Predicting a protein's structure does not mean understanding an entire living system. Weather models and AlphaFold demonstrate progress on defined tasks; the World Meteorological Organization also distinguishes limited weather modification from large-scale weather control lacking scientific support. Respecting these boundaries lets us use real scientific achievements as our reference. [Weather forecasting research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06079), [AlphaFold paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34265844/), [WMO statement](https://public.wmo.int/content/wmo-statement-weather-modification)

### In social research, the sources of information are willing—or unwilling—participants

For the complex system of human civilization, the information to be gathered is information about us. Unlike many physical measurements, its providers understand and assess the research, then decide what to disclose in light of their own circumstances. Even research on bodies and diseases must take human wishes seriously. Social research involves interests, trust, and power more directly still. As the technical ability to collect and process information improves, people's willingness to provide truthful information may become a decisive bottleneck.

In some animal experiments, humans collect information and test theories by overriding animals' wishes, restricting their freedom, and sometimes injuring or ending their lives. These practices have generated moral controversy.

If information about human society is forcibly collected through a traditional hierarchy, relying on unequal power and threats of violence, even an abundance of records may yield incomplete and misleading data, distorted by coercion, concealment, and deception. Such methods damage both trust and information quality when the aim is lasting, voluntary cooperation.

Suppose someone knows that reporting an equipment defect could cost them their job, or that expressing their actual needs could bring punishment. More questionnaires and more cameras may not improve understanding. People learn which answers are safe; an organization gradually hears only what it wants to hear. Records multiply without a corresponding increase in self-understanding. To change that, we must first change what happens after someone speaks truthfully.

In my view, this is one important reason why, in many fields, humanity's knowledge of nature and its ability to predict and alter natural processes have advanced further than its understanding, coordination, and restraint of its own social systems. Whether people trust the arrangements governing the use of their information is a key to understanding this mismatch.

Willing disclosure does not automatically solve every problem of knowledge. Data can omit important factors; correlation need not imply causation; participants may change their behavior in response to a published prediction. Protecting truthful expression is an important precondition. Testing theories, identifying causes, comparing plans, and revising conclusions must follow. The new system must improve all these conditions together.

### A civilization wielding enormous power must learn to restrain itself

If civilization's power over the external world increasingly exceeds its ability to understand and restrain its own behavior, that mismatch can lead to self-injury or even self-destruction. It resembles an immensely strong but fragile child swinging a massive chain hammer: ever more force, without knowing how to control its direction, may eventually strike the child itself.

In little more than a century, humanity has experienced two world wars, the US–Soviet Cold War, and nuclear crises. In great-power confrontation and competition over AI, if each side fears that the other's mastery of AI will threaten or destroy it, each may struggle desperately to get ahead. The fear of falling behind in the nuclear arms race can recur.

When both parties fear an outcome and respond in ways that intensify the other's fear, they may make that outcome more likely. The more they fear injury, the more they arm themselves; the more they see the other arming, the more afraid they become, and the more they arm again.

Even if this negative chain temporarily stabilizes, it has not necessarily produced a better collective result. Comparable powers may reach mutual deterrence, as in the Cold War nuclear competition between the United States and the Soviet Union. Alternatively, one side may gain a decisive technological lead and use it to inflict devastating harm, as the United States did in dropping atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Neither temporary stability nor military superiority establishes an outcome as better for humanity. Both can involve enormous costs of confrontation, mistaken judgment, loss of control, and the risk of self-destruction. This is one important source of fear about nuclear technology and AI.

### The reliability of technology and the reliability of the civilization using it

Technology's capacity to help and to harm depends on two connected factors: the reliability of the technology itself, and the reliability of the civilization that possesses and uses it. In many domains, our ability to understand, predict, and modify nature exceeds our ability to understand, coordinate, and restrain our social systems. That makes the second factor a serious weakness requiring urgent attention. As technologies grow more powerful, mistaken decisions and mutual confrontation can cause greater damage.


Look at the world around us. Under particular experimental conditions, we can position individual atoms into desired arrangements. We can put hundreds of millions of transistors on tiny chips, edit genes in certain human cells to treat some diseases, and build spacecraft. Yet society remains filled with ideological conflict, political upheaval, and brutal war: road rage, domestic violence, neighborhood disputes, labor conflicts; the events at the US Capitol and the social tensions visible in France's historical student movements; the war between Russia and Ukraine, conflict between the United States and Iran, and the long wars and conflicts involving Israel, Arab states, and Palestinians.

These events differ in character, responsibility, and historical context. Peaceful expressions of disagreement must not be equated with violence. But together they prompt a question: with such formidable technical powers, why can humanity not handle its own conflicts more reliably? If resources and attention flow disproportionately into making technology more capable and reliable while institutional reform and society's capacity for coordination and restraint are neglected, the mismatch can widen. We may pursue safety in ways that increasingly magnify harm, until recovery becomes difficult.

There are documented experiments in arranging individual atoms, and gene editing has entered clinical treatment for particular diseases. These achievements show that certain capabilities have arrived, not that we can reshape matter and life at will. They make the shared governance of powerful tools a concrete, present question. [Single-atom positioning experiment](https://research.ibm.com/publications/positioning-single-atoms-with-a-scanning-tunnelling-microscope), [FDA announcement on the first gene therapies for sickle cell disease](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapies-treat-patients-sickle-cell-disease)

### Protection and reward for truthful sharing can change how civilization understands itself

We already possess, or are gradually acquiring, the means to collect many important forms of information about society. Storage capacity, computing power, and rapidly developing AI continue to advance. Brain–computer interfaces, still under exploration, suggest further possibilities. Enabling people to provide relevant personal information voluntarily is therefore becoming an important bottleneck in this development.

The new system seeks to reform existing institutions, remove entrenched differences in power that allow domination, increase cooperation, and reduce conflict. It should constrain abuse and harm, establish practical safeguards against information being used against its providers, and strengthen people's willingness to share. Benefits from sharing can then encourage them to expand its useful scope and depth, provided that this positive feedback remains reliable and sustainable. This is the bottleneck I want to address at its root.

The return need not consist only of money or prestige. Having one's problem solved, finding collaborators for an idea, receiving help after an injury, and retaining basic protections when declining unnecessary disclosure are all reasons to keep participating. Nor is exposing ever more private detail the only measure of progress. Producing equally useful public knowledge with less disclosure is progress too.

These changes could improve the study of civilization as a complex system, support more accurate theories and more effective institutions, and allow its components to cooperate through intricate, dependable mechanisms—much as the parts of a complex organism sustain the whole and strengthen its ability to perceive and interact with its environment.

With computers and AI, people could obtain fuller, more reliable information, reason and decide through testable causal relationships, and continually improve their understanding and adjustment of social interactions. Together they could build a powerful, distributed neural network of dynamic decision weights: a brain for civilization through which they jointly participate in its evolution in the universe.

The biological analogy concerns feedback and functional cooperation. It does not turn people into cells without choices. A shared brain must not become another master standing above individuals. People must remain able to express different needs, challenge judgments, and choose the scope of their participation. Civilization's control over itself should first mean that participants are better able to constrain arbitrary harm together, not that a few are better able to manipulate everyone else.

### Choosing at a critical fork

We stand at a critical point, facing two directions that deserve clear recognition. One intensifies confrontation through negative feedback and increases the danger of civilization injuring or destroying itself. The other strengthens cooperation and self-correction through positive feedback, seeking a threshold in civilization's evolution and a new human form of social life. I hope this book helps people see these choices clearly and choose in favor of shared survival and creation.

These are not two predetermined endings. Real societies move repeatedly between cooperation and conflict. Institutions matter because they can improve our ability to recognize those changes, correct mistakes while correction remains possible, and protect cooperation as it begins. Technological progress and institutional development should reinforce one another, so that growing power is accompanied by a greater capacity to understand ourselves, protect one another, and choose a future together.

## Personal Information Collection, Data Sovereignty, and Practical Implementation

We have discussed the cooperation the new system seeks to foster, from individual moral and psychological development to the rules of social interaction. The next question is how those rules can operate in actual computers, devices, and software that people use in everyday life.

The aim is to preserve as much information useful to the system's development as possible as public knowledge, while protecting individuals' substantive interests. The specific arrangements must therefore evolve through practice, questioning, and feedback.

This aim combines accumulating information with protecting people. Records of a person's life can help them understand themselves and collaborate. Shared experiences can help participants establish facts. Lessons learned from those experiences can benefit a wider public. These materials need not all have the same audience. Making the lessons of an accident public does not require exposing the participants' entire lives. Making a procedure reviewable does not require allowing everyone to watch the original footage.

We therefore need to distinguish private personal records, evidence shared for a particular event, and knowledge suitable for common use. All can use Merkle-DAGs to preserve provenance and version relationships while retaining different access rules. Private originals remain subject to authorization by the individual and other relevant participants; event evidence enters a limited process; the public layer holds rules, software, methods, and results suitable for publication. Public knowledge should grow within the bounds that effective protections can support.

### Reliable records can change both prior choices and subsequent learning

A reader may ask: Reliable recording makes discovery and accountability easier, but does it prevent the act beforehand? If someone copies records they were not entitled to see and is caught afterward, has the harm not already occurred?

That is correct. Even a tightly regulated complex system can experience events beyond its present predictive and controlling capabilities. Carefully designed transport systems still have accidents: aircraft crash, trains derail, and cars collide or catch fire. Failing to anticipate a cause does not mean there was no cause. Nor should reliable records be understood as making all accidents disappear.

The first concern is deliberately harmful conduct.

Reliable recording can support more than investigation after the fact. It can also have a strong preventive effect. Where public monitoring is paired with a trustworthy response mechanism, people who know their conduct will leave evidence have reason to restrain deliberate wrongdoing.

If reciprocal responses are common knowledge, everyone knows both that malicious conduct will be recorded and that consequences may follow. Where the expected loss from those consequences exceeds the expected benefit, the rules may deter people who rationally weigh them.

The comparison is not simply between the size of a gain and the severity of a penalty. Someone who expects never to be detected, or expects to control the investigation, may not change their behavior in response to harsh penalties on paper. A simplified comparison must include the chance of detection and correct attribution, the chance that a response will actually be enforced, and the resulting loss. Time, emotion, and personal expectations also matter. Better evidence, credible adjudication, and reliable enforcement often deserve priority over endlessly increasing penalties. Deterrence research likewise emphasizes certainty of detection and consequences; severity alone is insufficient. [National Institute of Justice: Five Things About Deterrence](https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/five-things-about-deterrence)

Penalties must also be bounded, proportionate, and open to appeal. A system allowed to harm suspects arbitrarily in the name of deterrence recreates the unequal power the new order seeks to remove. Reducing the gains from abuse, providing legitimate opportunities to cooperate, and stopping unauthorized actions already underway belong in the design as well.

People in today's world are not perfectly rational actors. Incomplete education can leave gaps in knowledge and judgment; emotional regulation can be difficult. We also lack comprehensive, dependable systems for recording relevant conduct and adjudicating it fairly. These limitations weaken prevention.

I envision a world in which each person has a personal AI agent to support knowledge and decision-making, and in which many intellectual and physical activities are assisted or delegated to such agents. This buffer may substantially reduce impulsive conduct. The system could then combine retrospective records with real constraints on action to prevent deliberate harm as far as possible.

But is greater intelligence necessarily greater goodness? Certainly not. Reasoning can answer how to pursue a goal more effectively while the goal itself remains harmful. A more capable AI may also execute an improper request more effectively.

Personal AI can therefore help check facts, explain consequences, express dissatisfaction, and identify alternatives. When invoking shared devices and services, it must still respect specific permissions. An angry person may express their feelings fully to their AI, write an unsent draft, check the facts, and decide whether to revise it or communicate later. No AI may open someone else's private database merely because it is acting for its user. Private thoughts, feelings, and dissent must not automatically become grounds for punishment. The buffer should support autonomy, not become a superior that continually judges thoughts.

Protection thus has three connected stages: foreseeable consequences before action, limits on unauthorized conduct during action, and investigation and repair afterward. Failure in one stage is no reason to dispense with the others.

### Turning a loss into material for improvement

The same mechanism can help with harm caused without malice, through limited ability or forces beyond control. Viewed through information gathering, processing, and feedback, human societies and individual lives have comparable processes: both can accumulate experience and revise their models and decisions. People learn from mistakes; civilization's rules and plans likewise evolve with technology and knowledge. Harm cannot be eliminated completely, but reliable recording and response help a system avoid repeating errors and improve its capacity for feedback and correction. This is why I seek a stable, rapid, effective process of positive revision.

Learning something from every setback is an aspiration. Making it institutional requires a concrete sequence. First assist those affected and limit losses; preserve necessary evidence; propose possible causes and identify evidence that distinguishes them; test those explanations; change equipment, procedures, or cooperative rules; and check whether the change actually reduces recurrence. Finally, share reusable lessons so other nodes need not pay the same price.

Suppose a device handover fails and leaves a gap in a recording. The cause might be a network outage, inconsistent clocks, revoked authorization, or a software error. Requiring all devices to record more continuously without distinguishing these causes could fill the gap by violating authorization boundaries. Learning means identifying which rule needs revision and which boundaries must remain.

Responsibility requires similar distinctions. Deliberate injury, concealment of a known risk, avoidable negligence, failure during reasonable exploration, and an unforeseeable accident should not all receive the same punishment merely because the outcome was bad. Otherwise people will hide failures to escape penalties, depriving the system of what it needs to learn. Sharing appropriate lessons while protecting honest reporters is essential to lasting feedback.

Reliable records are thus an important precondition for reducing harm. The more accurate and complete the relevant records, and the more effectively they can be processed under appropriate permissions, the better the chance of avoiding repeated errors. What people often fear is not simply being recorded, but a few people acquiring the ability to inspect and use those records as weapons against them.

Recording can itself introduce risks. Collecting more private material expands the consequences of a leak. Knowing one might be judged at any moment can discourage honest questions. Preserving useful information therefore also requires asking what problem it helps solve, whether original details are needed, how long they should be retained, and who may use them under which conditions. A problem solvable with limited information need not begin with everyone surrendering their entire lives.

### Coordinating devices without taking away human choice

Collecting information, including audio and video within agreed boundaries, is a starting point for bringing this system into everyday life.

Devices can be located in fixed places, vehicles, or on the person. Initial categories could include:

1. Personal wearable audio and video recorders.

2. Plug-in recording devices in the home.

3. Devices in participating workplaces and public settings where those involved have consented.

Devices can coordinate wake and standby states and decide which should record. At home, distributed cameras and microphones can take over from a wearable. Where fixed devices in a home, office, or voluntarily participating public area provide adequate resolution, clarity, and coverage, and relevant authorizations remain valid, they can assume the task while the wearable sleeps. On departure, the wearable resumes within its authorized scope.

In places where the new system is not in use, recording is off by default. A user who manually starts recording must trigger a conspicuous indicator, allow others to know and object, and obtain the consent appropriate to the situation. Fixed devices can remain on standby when nobody is present or nothing relevant is happening and wake when needed.

The basic idea is an AI-coordinated system of cameras and microphones that collects personal information with less manual work.

“Unobtrusive” should mean fewer repetitive operations while keeping recording visible and making pause and exit easy. Otherwise convenience can become recording that nearby people do not know is happening.

Suppose a wearer arrives home and an indoor camera offers a clearer image. Can it immediately take over? Not yet. It may see a room behind the wearer or hear a conversation beyond their hearing. The system must check device identity, operating state, current authorization, viewpoint, participants, and purpose before comparing recording quality. The old device sleeps only after the new one confirms it can continue under those conditions. Handover times, overlaps, and gaps are recorded. When authorization cannot be established, a gap must be allowed.

A new visitor's arrival also changes the situation. An indicator light does not mean the visitor has consented. If their image and voice cannot reliably be excluded, the relevant recording should pause. Even low-information presence sensors need an explanation of what they collect. A microphone continually listening for speech is already processing information; it is not completely off.

Custom hardware could include a physical disconnect, lens cover, and indicator electrically tied to the recording circuit, so ordinary software cannot record while the indicator is dark. These properties require inspection of real devices and adversarial testing.

### Turning a person's historical field of view into explicit permissions

By default, people should be able to review authorized recordings of what they could see and hear in their own lives. Administrative status alone must not give anyone access beyond their authorized scope. This changes practices in which the collector or manager of public recordings can freely inspect other people's information.

Yet how can a system know precisely what someone actually saw or noticed? Does being able to hear a conversation in person authorize permanent searches across every similar conversation in another person's life? These questions show that natural sensory range is a useful principle, but cannot determine all rights after digitization.

Permissions should distinguish collection, retention, replay, AI processing, disclosure for an event, redistribution, and model training. Permission to record a meeting is not permission to train a public model on it. Permission to transcribe does not authorize long-term inference about personalities and relationships. A fixed device providing a personal view should filter by explicit time, space, participation, and content boundaries rather than hand a visitor the building's entire archive. Accessibility aids should be accommodated so people with different physical abilities receive appropriate assistance.

AI processing is access too. Giving a model every record and restricting only the answer a human sees does not establish privacy. The model should receive only the material required by the task, and derived results should inherit the relevant restrictions. Instructions contained in recorded speech or images are objects of analysis; they must not thereby acquire the power to invoke systems, change permissions, or send data elsewhere.

### Using keys and versions to constrain manufacturers and administrators

Existing devices can serve at first; better-adapted hardware can be developed as needs become clear. I envision a division of roles among devices, individuals, manufacturers, and public verification mechanisms. From manufacture through use and firmware updates, the collection, upload, and use of information should be verifiable, making it harder for any one party to alter records or use them without permission.

The division needs precision. Device signatures identify a claimed recording source. Content keys encrypt appropriately scoped segments. Keys controlled by the person grant decryption access. A manufacturer's signature establishes the source of a firmware update; community review approves rules or updates only within its agreed remit. A public verification key must not imply a public institution holds a master key to everyone's private data. Nor should ordinary personal access require the manufacturer's permission each time.

Multiple approvals must also represent genuinely independent control. One administrator holding five accounts does not create five-party oversight. Key recovery can follow arrangements selected in advance by the individual, with clear procedures for lost keys, colluding recovery parties, and stolen devices.

Segments can be encrypted on the device, with ciphertext and necessary metadata signed, content identifiers generated, and copies sent to storage nodes chosen by the user. Hashes make changes detectable and signatures make provenance claims checkable. But a camera's scene can still be staged, and important events may never be recorded. Verifiable provenance and factual truth require separate examination. [C2PA specification](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/specs/C2PA_Specification.html)

Sequence numbers, clock uncertainty, device state, reasons for stopping, and gaps should therefore be recorded too. Missing material must remain missing; AI must not fill it in as if it were actual history. Absence of footage must not automatically establish someone's fault. Firmware needs protection, detection, and recovery together, not merely a check for the manufacturer's signature on an update. [NIST firmware resilience guidelines](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/193/final)
### Obtaining evidence for a shared event without granting unlimited access

Access to records involving several people should be driven by a specific event. Where more than one person is involved and a fair assessment or solution cannot be reached from one person's information alone, jointly reviewable rules and algorithms can progressively identify the relevant participants. Access then requires the corresponding consent and authorization. The process—from access through analysis, judgment, and remedy—is recorded through a Merkle-DAG. Procedures and results suitable for publication become public knowledge; private evidence remains restricted.

A circular problem can arise: if the system searches everyone's private records to decide who is involved, it has already exceeded its authority before obtaining permission. It should instead begin with material the requester is entitled to use, specify a time, place, and question, and request limited assistance from potentially relevant personal nodes. Any expansion needs further reasons and evidence.

If an object is damaged in a shared room, the person discovering it can provide records they are authorized to use, identify a limited time window, and request corresponding segments from relevant participants. The damaged object is no justification for reviewing everyone's entire month. The case record should retain requests, approvals, refusals, analyses, objections, and outcomes. If cropped segments or necessary facts suffice, full originals need not be opened.

A sharper question follows: if every person involved must consent anew, can a wrongdoer block an investigation simply by refusing? An initial, purely voluntary arrangement cannot compel the resolution of every dispute. But an accused party should not be able to veto another participant's use of evidence that participant already lawfully holds. Binding dispute resolution would require evidence-preservation and review arrangements known in advance, limited in scope, and subject to independent review and appeal. An algorithm cannot declare unlimited access after an event occurs. Anyone affected can raise a concern without first finding a second person willing to acknowledge that the event happened.

### Preserving history while allowing revocation and deletion

A Merkle-DAG needs to record more than audio and video. Collection sessions, handovers, consent, authorizations, derived results, event handling, effective rules, revocations, and deletions all need verifiable relationships. A transcript should point to its source and the software version that produced it. A decision should point to the evidence and rules used at the time. A correction should create a new version and identify the judgment it supersedes.

An authorization's fixed content identifier establishes what it says, not whether it is still valid. Each use must also check expiry, revocation, purpose, and current rules. Device attestation presents a similar problem: yesterday's healthy state does not prove today's. Remote attestation architectures must address evidence freshness, and state can change after verification. [RFC 9334](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9334.html)

A history maintainer may also show different histories to different people. Independent nodes retaining and comparing signed checkpoints can help detect such forks. Drawing a Merkle tree alone does not create cross-checks among observers. [RFC 9162](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9162.html)

Revocation can stop future access; it cannot erase what someone has already seen or guarantee recovery of copied plaintext. Offline devices cannot immediately learn of new revocations, so offline permissions need expiry limits. Operations requiring immediate revocation cannot depend on long-lived offline credentials. Private material need not be retained indefinitely. Expiry-based deletion should cover originals, thumbnails, transcripts, search indexes, and backups. Destroying a key may remove the usable decryption path for corresponding ciphertext, but does not prove that every copy in the world has disappeared.

Traceability therefore need not mean permanent public exposure. Preserving a decision's history need not mean retaining every private original forever. The system can preserve appropriately scoped records of what was handled under which rules while removing expired content from accessible storage.

### How a new organization can demonstrate that it deserves trust

Why would people trust such products? I envision an organization that itself follows the new arrangements, so rules protecting users bind designers, operators, and administrators as well.

On one side, information and administrative conduct relevant to users' rights would leave traceable, verifiable Merkle-DAG records. Decisions and operations that infringe those rights should be reliably recorded and examinable. On the other, personal provision would aim at meeting needs, assisted by public, reviewable optimization algorithms, reducing opportunities to gain extra rewards by harming users.

The first direction seeks to constrain the ability to infringe users' private rights; the second seeks to reduce the desire to do so. Both need development and practical testing, making it harder to misuse positions and tools while making cooperation a more attractive way to meet needs.

A reader can still ask: Even if provision according to need removes some financial motives, why would it guarantee an absence of malice?

That question must remain open. Curiosity, a desire for control, revenge, outside bribery, and even mistaken ideas about the common good can create risks. Reports of need and allocation algorithms can themselves be manipulated. Institutions must protect participants when someone acts maliciously or makes a mistake, rather than assume everyone has become ideal.

Concrete tests can follow. Can an administrator read plaintext after copying the database? Can a manufacturer bypass the indicator through a firmware update? Can a counterfeit device take over a recording? Can an expired authorization be replayed? Can a high-weight node bypass consent? Can an AI treat words in a record as executable instructions? Can an auditor expand their browsing under the cover of inspection? Every successful unauthorized path identifies another protection that needs repair.

Participants also need shared rules: do not cover indicators, consent for someone else, or connect hidden recording bypasses. Refusing to be recorded must not automatically imply untrustworthiness or loss of basic services. Family and employment relationships do not substitute for personal consent. Children and people unable to express their wishes fully require specific arrangements. Independent reporting and shutdown routes must exist, and people must be able to export and recover their data and change providers. The new organization's trustworthiness will ultimately depend on whether these rules genuinely constrain the organization itself.

### Can virtual spaces help people handle impulses in real life?

I have a further idea. Human beings are animals with biological characteristics and instinctive impulses: physiological drives, curiosity, a desire for control, and impulses associated with love, hatred, and fear. Some have less common psychological desires; existing social conditions may also have produced tendencies that can substantially harm themselves or others. How might the new system help people manage such conduct safely?

The image of a pressure-release valve suggests one possible approach: expressing and exploring emotions through fiction, film, and games in virtual spaces. A scientific approach would limit duration and intensity to avoid compulsive use that damages ordinary life, while testing whether these experiences relieve pressure or improve understanding of consequences. More developed brain–computer interfaces might eventually enable other experiences. Whether such technology is necessary, or produces better results, remains a research question.

But treating the analogy as an established psychological law raises a problem: Is anger really like steam, with more release necessarily meaning less anger afterward? Existing research does not support that general conclusion. A meta-analysis of 154 studies involving 10,189 participants found that activities reducing arousal generally helped reduce anger, while arousal-increasing activities did not show the same overall effect. Intense venting therefore cannot simply be equated with effective regulation. This does not establish that all games are harmful or settle the effects of future virtual experiences. [Paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414), [Research institution's account](https://news.osu.edu/breathe-dont-vent-turning-down-the-heat-is-key-to-managing-anger/)

Virtual spaces can be explored in several distinct ways: safe expression of difficult feelings, role reversal to understand another person's circumstances, making consequences visible in simulation, and practicing communication, cooperation, and pausing before acting. Their effects need separate testing. Evaluation should ask more than whether an experience feels satisfying in the moment. Does self-control, sleep, relationships, or daily functioning improve afterward?

An uncommon preference is not automatically an illness, and fictional imagination is not automatically real-world malice. The concerns are loss of control, substantive harm, and impaired functioning—not a single algorithm prescribing what everyone may enjoy. The World Health Organization's account of gaming disorder likewise emphasizes impaired control and significant harm to functioning, rather than gaming itself or a particular thematic preference. [WHO explanation](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gaming-disorder)

These explorations can begin with existing media without waiting for brain–computer interfaces. Whether stronger immersion helps is a separate question to test. Nor can virtual experience replace improvements to injustice, loneliness, exhaustion, and material hardship. It should be an aid people choose, not a way to pacify them into accepting real harm.

### Begin with one complete, small cycle

The overall plan can be large while its first test remains small. In a space the person controls, with clear authorization, record material with a definite beginning and end. Complete device-side encryption, personal retrieval, limited sharing, revocation, deletion, backup recovery, and migration. Then deliberately introduce expired permissions, false device identities, damaged files, network outages, and lost keys to see how the system actually responds.

This first establishes whether the person really has control. A few consenting adults can then test handovers between fixed and wearable devices, especially whether recording stops or degrades appropriately when a visitor arrives, permissions change, or equipment fails. Agreed simulated disputes can test evidence requests, limited disclosure, objections, and appeals. Only after those simulations should bounded real cooperation be considered. Real trials must preserve failures as well as successes.

Expansion requires multiple independent copies, independent audits, and the ability to recover and leave a service. Custom hardware should respond to measured battery life, recording quality, and security gaps. Recording in open public settings should come later, since consent boundaries are harder to maintain there.

Costs must be calculated from the first version. A single video stream averaging 2 Mb/s and recording continuously for 24 hours requires about 21.6 GB per day, or 7.9 TB per year in decimal units. That excludes replicas, indexes, additional cameras, and AI processing. Retention periods and image quality therefore need planning, with routine footage expiring and necessary event segments retained, alongside the full costs of upload, retrieval, maintenance, and recovery.

Every expansion should add a capability that can be checked repeatedly. If one person can already preserve and use their information more easily, actually stop recording, refuse sharing, recover control, and leave the service, that small step has value in its own right. Further cooperation and shared governance can then grow on a working foundation.

## Starting with One Person's Cloud Storage

This starting point differs from conventional crowdfunding. First, consumers can also be producers and creators. Second, no minimum number of participants or funding target must be reached before work begins. It can start with me, with the simplest and cheapest cloud storage, funded from my own small savings. Third, participation is flexible. Those who support the direction can contribute more money or effort and help build the service as well as use it. Those who prefer to observe can remain consumers and provide basic feedback. In every case, the new system must protect users' data sovereignty.

Why begin with cloud storage? It addresses an existing, everyday problem: information is scattered across devices, accounts, and services. We need it, yet cannot always find, understand, or take it with us. Solving this creates immediate personal value while preparing the foundation for a longer-term network of knowledge, computation, and cooperation.

The initial scale need not be large. Even one person has files to organize, work to preserve, and discussions to reconnect. That person supplies the needs and the resources, discovering problems through actual use. The value of the seed stage is a complete process that can keep running.

### What does the first service actually deliver?

The minimum service offers more than an allocation of storage. It provides a personal information space the user can control. Content can be retrieved, its origins and versions understood, and different tools used with it. If the original service stops, the records remain usable.

The user experience can be simple: choose a set of materials; retain the originals; create an index; search and read them; then export the content, index, and relationships and restore them elsewhere. Once this cycle works, data sovereignty becomes a concrete operation rather than a slogan.

Chat exports, articles, and project documents are suitable first materials. They already support learning and creation. Organizing them immediately helps recover original statements, compare ideas, and write. Photographs, recordings, and other work materials can follow. There is no need to wait until every format can be handled perfectly.

### Preserve what allows a fresh start, then improve understanding

Incoming original files are retained as source material. Parsed conversations, paragraphs, attachments, and timestamps form a structure that can be regenerated. Semantic extraction, topic relationships, and current positions belong to a further layer. An error in interpretation then need not damage the original words.

A chat archive, for example, contains the author's words, AI responses, and material copied from elsewhere. An initial parser that merely separates conversations and speakers is already more useful than scattered ZIP files. Later improvements can identify quotations and responses by returning to the same originals. The system need not attribute every sentence to one author simply to produce an attractive graph quickly.

Storage, indexing, and reading interfaces can also be separate. Storage preserves and checks content; indexes help locate it; interfaces help people understand it. An unsuitable interface can be replaced and a lost index rebuilt. Neither should force the user to abandon their originals.

### Combining local and cloud storage under personal control

At first, existing equipment and affordable storage can hold reliable copies across distinct failure domains. Local copies support direct use; cloud storage provides off-site preservation or access across devices. Private content placed on storage outside the person's direct control requires suitable encryption and key arrangements.

The crucial point is that logging into one company's website must not be the only path to recovery. Users should have their files, necessary format documentation, and recovery instructions, and know who holds the keys. For one person, these arrangements can be simple. They still require an actual recovery exercise, not mere confidence in an “upload successful” message.

Recovery can be an early acceptance test: retrieve a group of files from backup, verify their contents, and open them in another index or reading environment. Choose a versioned item and check that its old and new versions remain connected. This answers the user's most basic question: Are these things still mine?

### What must change when a second person joins?

The first addition should be clear boundaries, not a grand organizational hierarchy. Each person's private materials remain separate, with sharing determined by them. Shared software, public documents, and operating rules can form common resources.

Joint contributions require understandable accounts. Storage, transfer, computing, backup, and maintenance costs should be distinguished, along with costs attributable to individual use and those supporting common infrastructure. Early on, recording actual expenditure and usage is enough. There is no need to turn every helpful act into a permanent contribution score.

One participant may provide a server, another repair an importer, and another consistently identify usability problems. All deserve a record, but their contributions do not share one natural unit. Financial accounts establish expenditure, code history establishes changes, and feedback records show how problems were resolved. Evaluation should proceed from those concrete records.

The direction is consistent from the start. While I am the only participant, I bear the initial investments and decisions. As others join, fair distribution and decision-making become a shared responsibility under the new arrangements.

Even a few people can document a complete decision: the problem, available options, affected people, chosen plan, and subsequent result. Complex dynamic-weight algorithms can develop gradually. Public reasons and retained feedback can begin on day one.

Basic provision for people's lives is a goal for a much broader productive system. A small storage service cannot initially promise to meet every participant's needs. It can clarify the relationships among service charges, personal information, and collective resources, and ensure that investment does not automatically purchase authority over other people's records or lives.

### Making one person's improvement available to others

Once one user's platform gains a capability, other users with compatible platforms should have an opportunity to reuse it instead of creating it again.

A cloud-based Merkle node can gradually provide registration, publication, indexing, and discovery for these capabilities.

This changes the role of the first users. They need not merely pay an operator and wait for it to decide what to build. One person's problem can become a shared task; an importer or organizational method developed by one can be reused by others; new needs can expand the common capability library.

Useful sharing requires a capability to state what materials it handles, what changes it makes, and under which conditions it has been checked. When a user chooses to adopt it, the results provide further feedback. The service gains more than accounts: it gains participants who can improve it together.

### When to add computing and other content services

Further development could offer services now supplied by commercial platforms: storage, reading, music, long and short video, and film and television streaming. From an author's publication to an audience's use, the framework could record and manage content, resources, and working relationships, including platform development, resource placement, and bandwidth. Cooperation among humans and AI agents, and the allocation and adjustment of decision weights, could be developed on that recorded foundation. Records of funding, energy use, usage, and contributions would allow service charges and creators' returns to be checked and discussed under public rules.

Expansion should follow actual needs in existing services. Storage users seeking search, transcoding, or private AI give computing a concrete purpose. Sustained growth in usage provides evidence for comparing equipment purchases with continued rental. Each additional service must bring its costs, maintenance, and recovery arrangements into the same complete cycle.

The first storage service can thus be small enough to begin with one person's practical needs while already embodying the future organization's direction: personal control of records, shared knowledge where publication is appropriate, understandable and discussable use of common resources, and improvements that endure. It need not build an entire civilization on its first day. It should begin growing in the intended direction on that first day.

## Growing a Free Zone and Evolving Institutions Peacefully

How a human organization cooperates—from a family to a village, city, country, or the whole planet—depends closely on the understandings its participants can share. Public knowledge is a basic condition shaping human organization. From this perspective, we can imagine a path: study the historical evolution of social systems; use a first-principles perspective, including the higher-level questions suggested by category theory, to propose a better and more effective structure of governance and organization; publish these ideas openly online; and gain the agreement of some people. Knowledge that began with one person becomes shared knowledge among a group. That group can then build a small, local organization using the new architecture, much as a new version of a software system performs familiar functions in a different way. Compatible interfaces let it interact and compete with existing systems. If it demonstrates advantages, more people may understand its shared knowledge, accept its principles, and join. We must carefully design coexistence, compatibility, and migration so that institutional change can proceed smoothly and avoid the conflict and warfare that have so often accompanied past transitions.

A free zone is first a sphere of shared practice. It can begin with one service, a group of participants, and inspectable rules, without requiring a geographically isolated territory. Participants continue living in existing society, using existing currencies, equipment, and networks while experimenting with different arrangements in the activities they undertake together.

This entry point matters. If the whole world must agree first, no new mechanism can begin. If joining requires immediately abandoning one's existing life, participation becomes unnecessarily costly. Compatibility and gradual migration allow different levels of involvement.

### Common ground rules should enable diverse institutional experiments

What I want to emphasize is that common basic rules should support the widest practical range of experiments in social organization. An evolving system faces a maze of almost limitless institutional possibilities. How can it branch and explore in parallel, preserve diversity, and discover better paths with less energy and lower costs of change?

The common rules should allow experiments to coexist, communicate, and remain accountable. Their specific solutions can differ. Two groups can try different forms of cooperation without infringing each other's rights, record their conditions, costs, and results, then learn from each other. A useful local practice need not wait for one organization to unify all of society before it spreads. Nor should later participants have to repeat the cost of every failed path.

Nearly unlimited possibilities do not imply that every path must be exhausted. Effective exploration preserves diverse candidates, eliminates clearly unproductive directions early, and directs resources toward comparisons that teach us the most, while leaving opportunities for ideas the majority does not yet understand. “Better” must specify for whom and under what conditions. Saving one group's time by transferring costs to another is not enough. Feedback between real and virtual experiments can gradually improve the efficiency of the exploration itself.

### From renting resources to building productive capacity

Reclaiming personal data and services offers a place to begin: first with me, then with others attracted through communication and outreach. Open-source projects can initially replace conventional software and services, with compatible interfaces taking over their functions. Hardware begins as rented capacity. As participation and resources grow, equipment can be purchased where that lowers costs. Further growth may support retail purchases, wholesale procurement, and eventually direct design and manufacture. Early activity can concentrate on storage, expand into computing as resources grow, use existing open models first, and later acquire hardware, develop software, and train models. Beginning with personal data sovereignty, this form of organization can expand gradually, deliberately, and systematically into wider productive fields.

Every step needs a concrete purpose. Renting allows a service to begin with limited investment. Ownership becomes appropriate when sustained demand and scale justify it. Design and manufacture require accumulated skills, technical knowledge, and dependable demand. These stages reflect productive conditions, not ranks of institutional prestige.

Likewise, the move from storage to computing should be driven by actual work. Search, content processing, model inference, and joint research can create growing computational needs. Existing open-source tools can provide value first; recurring expensive operations can then become targets for collective improvement. As with the software seed, the system should first sustain itself, then adapt its structure to doing so more effectively.

### Internal organization must begin changing on day one

The transition I envision can begin with the first open-source project: reverse-engineer existing interfaces and workflows, understand the protocols connecting today's isolated data and applications, and build progressively compatible replacements. These would operate on a Merkle-based framework that verifies content, traces changes, and publishes the rules and reasons needed for shared affairs. Personal provision would follow needs in healthier, more reasonable ways, while collective resources would be managed through the dynamic-weight decision network. By actually providing better capabilities, this can be a nonviolent, gradual transition through competition with existing arrangements.

Using an outward organizational form compatible with today's society does not require reproducing the same internal hierarchy. Resource use, rule changes, work allocation, and contribution records can be discussed openly from the smallest scale.

If data, rules, and resources remain permanently in a few hands on the promise of equality later, growth may make reform harder. What must persist through expansion is participants' real ability to govern shared affairs, not merely an organization's name or promotional language.

### Why the ideal need not be achieved before work begins

Efficient organization and rapid information gathering and sharing cannot arrive all at once. Fully informed interaction and equality among nodes describe ideal conditions. The new system need not reach them immediately; some limiting goals may never admit a single final solution, yet still guide improvement. A real advantage on one concrete problem already gives people a reason to participate. Just as a physical system tends toward lower potential energy, a mechanism that demonstrably completes the same task with less energy or time gives people reason to try it without coercion. We need to reduce the costs of migration and organizational change.

Historically, institutional replacement has often involved violent revolution. People in an old system have lived within it, repeatedly tested their expectations against it, and adapted to it. They have not experienced the proposed system, do not know it well, and cannot know what long-term life within it will actually feel like. Resistance to unfamiliar arrangements reflects ordinary efforts to avoid harm and secure benefits.

Being a little better must be visible. Information once difficult to find becomes easier to locate. Content once impossible to take away can be restored elsewhere. Feedback that once had no destination can be followed into an actual improvement. Such changes can justify joining.

A local advantage does not mean everyone will migrate at once. Habits, trust, complementary services, and existing relationships impose real costs. Recognizing them allows trials, parallel use, and gradual migration, rather than treating everyone who has not yet joined as unable to understand the future.

### Free entry and exit are part of growth

Participation should permit free choice to join and leave, supported by compatible data and applications and straightforward import and export. Replacement should come through better efficiency and benefits, increasing the attraction of participation and seeking expansion that benefits both sides.

Exit does not undermine collective development. Knowing that one's records and work can be taken away makes it easier to entrust part of one's life to a shared service. It also obliges an organization to keep providing value rather than retain users by locking in accumulated content.

Shared work continues under rules published in advance. Attribution does not disappear when a contributor leaves. Public knowledge does not become closed merely because someone changes their participation. Permissions for private records, however, must reflect the person's choices. Clear terms of entry and departure reduce the need for mutual suspicion.

### Growth must not create another closed giant

HotPath seeks to change the conditions that allow an ecosystem to be monopolized through closed accounts, data, permissions, and toolchains. A large organization can also restructure and join as a strong contributor. In doing so, a company may move from a pyramid toward a social neural network: equal standing, differentiated roles, and dynamic decision weights. Fairer returns and better coordination may help address the declining efficiency and creativity often associated with large hierarchies. This aim also requires checking coordination costs and creative capacity as our own system grows. One sign of maturity would be the project's increasing ability to develop through its own capabilities and iteration.

Expansion cannot be measured only by the users and assets gathered under one organizational name. More important is whether capabilities remain open, participants can understand the rules, new ideas can enter, and data and tools remain portable.

A highly capable team may undertake complex work. But if it converts shared interfaces, knowledge, and decision channels into indispensable private gateways, it has departed from this direction. The collective capabilities created by scale should enlarge each participant's scope for action, not become new instruments of control.

### From productive cooperation to wider public affairs

Funding and resources can be gathered in a manner resembling crowdfunding: explain the organization's principles, functions, benefits, and prospects, and gradually attract participants. The first supporter and contributor can be just one person—me. Communication online and in everyday life can invite others. Many hands can turn a seed into a large tree.

Only when an organizational form can reliably provide a growing range of productive and everyday services, and its participants have real experience of shared decisions, does it gain a basis for addressing wider public affairs. Movement from economic cooperation to community collaboration and broader institutional change must proceed through these capabilities and shared acceptance.

The political change I hope for would transfer public functions now attached to fixed hierarchies and a few positions toward an intelligent network governed by participants together. Some organizational forms of traditional companies and state institutions might consequently be absorbed, transformed, or replaced. Their necessary public functions still have to be performed. Production, transport, care, dispute resolution, and common security do not disappear because an institution receives a new name.

Peaceful evolution means gradually taking on functions the new arrangements can demonstrably perform better, allowing people to enlarge cooperation through experience. Building real services and creating cultural works that help people understand this possible future can grow alongside each other.

## Self-Realizing Science Fiction and Cultural Creation

Whether people understand an institution depends on more than complete definitions. Readers also ask: What would a day in this society feel like? What happens when we disagree? How would I find help to create something? If a decision fails, how do we go on living?

Theory explains relationships; stories put those relationships back into human experience. I want practical construction and cultural creation to advance together, giving people ways both to understand a mechanism and to imagine or experience it.

### Connecting imagination and reality through self-realizing science fiction

Science fiction does not ordinarily undertake to bring its entire story into being. I want to explore a form that connects imagination and reality and contributes to realizing this proposed civilization. Its world could begin with the premise that Earth is neither the universe's only intelligent planetary civilization nor its most advanced. Many worlds reached the new form of civilization I envision billions of years ago. An individual from one of them arrived on Earth thousands of years ago, blended into its ecology, quietly observed its development, and lived through it as a kind of life-spanning role-playing game. At a critical threshold in Earth's development, this visitor emerges as a science-fiction writer and creates a self-realizing work—a form combining science and art that advanced civilizations use to connect imagination with reality. Through it, the visitor helps Earth cross its threshold peacefully and gradually.

The story could present this as a mechanism that emerged and repeatedly recurred in cosmic civilization. A gifted individual in the first civilization to cross the threshold—Alpha—conceived it when conflicts among nations, peoples, beliefs, and institutions threatened that civilization's transition. It helped Alpha pass through peacefully. The civilization visiting Earth, which we can call Beta, was itself helped in this way by Alpha. Earth would be a third-generation civilization and the 5,768th approaching the threshold.

Alpha, Beta, and Earth's number are fictional worldbuilding. Their narrative purpose is to place humanity's immediate transformation within a longer history. One civilization's difficulties become material through which another understands itself. More advanced beings can help without taking decisions away from those they help.

The richest conflict is therefore not simply whether an alien has stronger weapons. The visitor may know many possible futures, yet must still enable people on Earth to understand and participate. Declaring answers through superior force would recreate the very relationship the visitor hopes to change.

### Long life, diverse forms of life, and knowledge across time

In this fictional civilization, biological humans and AI together form intelligent nodes—the neurons of a distributed social intelligence. A Merkle-based public-knowledge system serves as civilizational DNA, supporting collective decisions among participants of equal standing. Biological individuals have also achieved indefinite life, overcoming aging, death, and the decline of physical and intellectual capacities. Knowledge, memory, and skills can therefore be shared across individuals and space, and also chosen and inherited across time.

Under this far-future premise, reproduction through descendants is no longer the only way genetic inheritance operates. Marriage and family organized around raising the next generation are no longer the dominant social units. Most people choose a new form of horizontal inheritance developed for indefinitely living beings. Through intimate biological exchange, they can safely replace and recombine genes, allowing each person to experience genetic diversity. This mechanism also supports extensive combinations across species, overcoming conventional reproductive isolation and enabling forms familiar from mythology and fantasy: life adapted to prolonged underwater existence, centaur-like bodies, or extraordinary regenerative capacities.

This explains how the visitor from Beta could remain undiscovered for thousands of years. The character does not transform without conditions. Through the fictional horizontal-inheritance mechanism, the visitor combines genetic material with terrestrial humans and alters their own biology to acquire human appearance. These are far-future premises of the work.

They allow deeper questions. If memory need not vanish with a short life, how do people change their interests and relationships? If appearance and capability become more plastic, how is diversity understood? How can a very long life preserve a desire to learn rather than endlessly repeat its past?

Science fiction gives such questions concrete situations. It may boldly stipulate future conditions, provided they remain internally consistent. Real services and institutional experiments, meanwhile, proceed from capabilities that actually exist. The two connect through questions and the desire to create.

### The creators and the builders can be the same people

The story itself follows the hidden visitor as they find like-minded people online and in everyday life. Through a self-realizing work, they communicate a possible future civilization and organize people to create novels, animation, film, and television while also building open hardware, software, and eventually a wider range of projects. These projects operate through equal, distributed cooperation, a Merkle-based public-knowledge system, and collective intelligence organized as a social neural network. Their gradual aim is to remove inequality, information silos, violence, and entrenched power within the pyramidal structures of conventional states and companies.

I want literature, comics, film, television, and interactive games to let people experience this possibility psychologically before committing their lives to it. The game can also be a laboratory for the new system. Participation can move gradually from imaginary situations to real problems. Someone may keep a job in the old system while taking part in their spare time, first exploring fictional scenarios, then helping solve actual problems and earning supplementary income. If the work brings better returns and a more enjoyable, less burdensome experience, they may gradually devote more time to it. More effective production, lower costs, better treatment, and a better working experience can make the new arrangements attractive through voluntary experience and practical benefits, reducing the conditions for violent transition.

Readers need not change their entire lives after one story. They can discuss a problem in it or propose another ending. They can help with subtitles, illustrations, or code. They can simply try a real service and report where it is awkward.

Each form of participation provides feedback. If the fictional institution is appealing but the real tool is hard to use, that gap is itself a problem to solve. A successful real collaboration can, in turn, become material for a story. Culture and production nourish one another, rather than one promising a future the other never makes available for experience.

### A virtual social laboratory for experiencing and testing institutions

Alongside diverse real experiments, an AI-driven virtual society and game could greatly accelerate exploration. This is somewhat like using biological laboratories to study traits and genes deliberately, speeding up observations and comparisons that would take much longer under natural conditions.

In a digital environment, the same initial state can be copied into branches with different rules. The state before a failure can be saved, one condition changed, and the consequences compared. Sudden scarcity, mistaken information, or very unequal abilities can test whether rules still sustain cooperation. Their value need not be illustrated only by an idealized promotional image.

Three activities need distinction. A story helps people understand a possible life. A game lets participants experience choices and feedback. Social simulation seeks testable predictions about defined questions. Stories may assume a distant future; research models must state which conditions come from reality and which are assumptions. Studies have built generative agents from interviews or surveys involving more than a thousand people and predicted some responses and behaviors within a defined scope. Such progress supports further research; it does not establish an ability to simulate society's entire long-term evolution. [Research on generative simulations of individuals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109v3)

If all virtual participants come from similar models, they may share the same blind spots. A million simulated people agreeing does not mean a million real people would consent. Experiments should be compared with consensual observation and small-scale practice, checked across different populations and initial conditions, and retain failed predictions. Methodological work on language-model social simulation likewise emphasizes human baselines, scope, and calibration. [Methodological discussion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26080v2)

The acceleration I seek is therefore a cycle: virtual worlds generate plans, expose problems, and identify directions worth testing; real practice then tests and corrects the models. Digital states can be rolled back, but human losses cannot always be undone. Moving into real activities requires renewed checks of authorization, cost, and stopping conditions. Only with this two-way connection can the game become a laboratory for institutional evolution rather than a repeated performance of a future we already want to believe in.

### Begin with a story a reader can enter

Self-realizing fiction can begin in its easiest form as one important part of public communication. A short story can introduce key elements of the world, followed by linked stories and eventually novels that expand it. AI-assisted short films can provide an initial multimedia form, later growing into series and longer productions.

The first story need not explain the whole history of civilization. It might follow an ordinary person trying to retrieve years of creative work scattered across services. They discover a tool built by a community, initially seeking only a solution to an immediate problem. A genuine disagreement then reveals something different: the rules are not imposed unilaterally by an invisible administrator, and their own experience can contribute to improvement.

The story can include failure. An import goes badly, an attribution error causes anger, and a shared decision needs revision. Readers see that the new system's appeal is not sudden human perfection, but the way problems become visible, discussable, and correctable. Hints of the visiting civilization can emerge in the background, connecting the small immediate event with the larger world.

Later stories can explore production, care, research, education, art, and shared decisions. Each addresses a concrete problem while leaving characters and relationships that connect to others. By the time a novel grows from them, its world is no longer an empty catalogue of premises. It is a world people have already lived in.

### From text to video to a world people can participate in

Communication can begin in several forms together: persuasive writing that combines theoretical argument with accessible public expression; multimedia treatments of material suited to images; and sharing through online communities and independent media.

Video requires a different order of presentation. An abstract argument can unfold through a task, a person's choice, or visible cooperation. Where readers of an essay would pause to think, an image can show a relationship. A video should do more than narrate every sentence of a long article.

Once stories, short films, and real services provide enough material, an interactive game has a firmer basis. Players can try different cooperative arrangements and see how resources, information, and feedback alter outcomes. If participation later connects to real tasks, people must know when they are entering actual work, how its results will be used, and what they have chosen.

Self-realization does not mean disguising fiction as fact. Imagination poses questions; the work attracts collaborators; practice answers those questions; and new experience returns to culture. A short story, a short film, and one useful small service can begin the process, expanding into a fuller world as participants and capabilities grow.

## Science, Education, and Civilization's Continuing Creativity

When people have their own knowledge spaces and their experiences can connect through a shared network, education and research can change. Learning need not consist only of placing finished conclusions in a person's mind. Research need not remain an isolated activity performed by a few specialists while everyone else waits for results. Understanding a problem, asking questions, trying solutions, and making the results available for others can become a connected process.

This does not require everyone to become an expert in everything. Precisely because no one can know every field, we need forms of organization through which different kinds of knowledge complement one another.

### Everyone is both a learner and a teacher

In the new system I envision, everyone participates in governing and is also subject to shared governance. Economically, everyone can be both producer and consumer. In education and knowledge, everyone is both student and teacher. Civilization's collective knowledge and judgment arise from people connected as its neurons.

I imagine a future in which people record their lives and, with AI, understand the world's knowledge and information more quickly. Even a small spark of thought can be preserved and communicated. A vast network of dynamic decision weights, together with AI systems distributed across the globe, can integrate existing human knowledge. Through discussion, testing, and collaboration, small insights from human and AI nodes can become additions to that knowledge, entering a continuing cycle of growth.

Someone who has spent years caring for a family member might notice that an assistive device looks sensible in a drawing but is uncomfortable in use. They need not qualify as an engineer before their observation counts. Engineers can help describe forces and dimensions; the carer can explain bodily experience and use conditions; other users can add different circumstances; AI can organize records and compare proposals. The carer learns technical knowledge while teaching others about a problem they had overlooked.

“Student” and “teacher” are roles within particular relationships. Knowing one subject does not authorize someone to decide every question for others. Not yet understanding a theory does not make one's experience worthless. A person can seek help here and offer it there, changing roles through learning and practice.

Public knowledge should therefore preserve both conclusions and paths to them. Standard answers alone often conceal why anyone thought of them. An unorganized record of every step can overwhelm the learner. A useful entry point begins with the problem and lets people unfold examples, reasoning, failed attempts, and evidence as needed, until they can judge for themselves and ask further questions.

### Is mathematics discovered or created?

Is mathematics only a science of discovery? Physics and chemistry enable inventions that did not previously exist. One common view of mathematics, however, treats it as discovery of the universe's abstract nature: mathematical truths existed before humanity, perhaps from the universe's beginning, and people merely rediscover them. Is that the whole story? We construct mathematical structures, relationships, and concepts without requiring an immediately corresponding physical object. Many such structures are then applied to the world, enabling us to arrange matter into things exceedingly unlikely to emerge through unaided physical processes. This emergence of something new is invention. Does the mathematical structure supporting it also contain an act of creation? Binary Boolean algebra, electronic computing hardware and software, the broader information society, and the exploration of quantum computing all invite that question.

Discovery and creation need not be exclusive. Choosing rules, proposing definitions, and designing a structure are creative acts. Once rules are fixed, some relationships no longer depend on personal wishes and must be discovered and proved. A new construction may also reveal relationships in an old problem that had gone unnoticed.

Mathematics education confined to repeating established solution procedures therefore shows only part of mathematical activity. Learners should also see how questions arise, why existing concepts prove insufficient, what a new definition solves, and what further questions it creates. Creativity here is not permission to assert anything. It is an invitation to submit new constructions to reasoning and testing.

### Can we learn the process of creating theories?

Solving a defined mathematical problem and proposing a mathematical theory are different tasks, as are solving problems and creating theories in other sciences. Yet the human brain addresses both through the same broad machinery. This gives me reason to investigate whether large models' training and inference mechanisms can also support both.

Occam's razor, Newton's rules for natural philosophy, and Kolmogorov complexity suggest a question about evaluating theories: in the age of large AI models, could the complexity of vector-space representations expressing equivalent meanings be compared, with minimization used as a reward objective?

The first requirement is to define how equivalence of meaning is established. Shortening an explanation may discard its conditions. Hiding many operations behind a name does not necessarily reduce the work of understanding or execution. Conversely, adding an example makes a text longer but may accelerate understanding and reduce misuse. The length of an explanation, model representation, proof, learning process, and execution all need separate measurement before their relationships can be studied.

This is another reason for recording the evolution of ideas in a personal knowledge system. A final formula seldom reveals why a researcher changed direction. Connecting problems, attempts, counterexamples, and revisions makes it possible to investigate which actions mattered in creation.

A small comparison could begin the work. For the same set of problems, one set of materials presents only final conclusions; another retains the formation of concepts, failed attempts, and reasons for revision. Do human learners or AI better understand the conditions of application? Do they find counterexamples more readily or transfer the method to new situations? Such findings would not prove the entire theory, but could guide the next construction of the knowledge system.

### What does it mean to imagine boldly?

Current social-scientific frameworks should not be treated as the final boundary of possible institutions. Some theories seem to me like accounts produced by observers on trains moving at different speeds, directions, and accelerations. We still need to study how those accounts can be transformed, compared, and made compatible. Reasoning about a future society must also ask whether its premises have changed.

Consider a distant possibility: several bodies linked by brain–computer interfaces function as one intelligent agent. If they could constitute a continuous conscious whole, that agent would have bodies in different physical locations. Experiences and knowledge acquired by one could be available to the others. If memory and conscious continuity survived the loss of some bodies, losing one might have a meaning different from a person's death today—perhaps more like losing a regenerable limb. The experience could remain part of that agent's unique life, learning, and practice.

This is not an achieved technical result. It is a set of questions requiring distinct investigations: how information is shared, action coordinated, memory maintained, and subjective experience—if possible—integrated. Copying data does not answer them all. Yet assuming completely separate individuals from the outset and declaring all deeper connection impossible would not answer them either.

Such imagination shares a method with education and research in the present. Do not describe an unachieved possibility as fact; do not treat what has not yet been achieved as forever impossible. Separate a proposal into explicable conditions, turn those conditions into researchable questions, and let each result revise the next construction. Civilization's creativity grows through opening questions, making connections, and putting them into practice.

## From a Mathematician's Insight to Civilization's Shared Creation

At a forum organized by The Intellectual on October 25, 2026, Deng Yu discussed AI's growing role in mathematical research. He argued that clear rules are needed for recognizing contributions when models use researchers' ideas and for deciding whether research materials may be used in training. He also welcomed AI's potential to connect branches of mathematics while emphasizing independent thought and the choice of research direction. [Speech transcript](https://zhishifenzi.com/depth/newsview/10737?category=multiple)

This brings forward a question easily obscured by excitement over technical progress: as the capacity to complete research grows, why might the person who first has an idea become more reluctant to share it?

### 1. Why would someone hide an insight?

Imagine a mathematician who sees a new research direction. They sense where a breakthrough might lie but have not completed a proof. They must review extensive prior work, learn unfamiliar mathematical tools, and try approaches that may fail. A new tool could perform much of that work. This ought to be welcome.

But suppose that after submitting the idea, the researcher cannot see how the material is used or control its scope of use. Suppose the final result names only the person who completes it, leaving no record of the initial insight. The researcher must then consider whether using the tool means losing control of the work or having their contribution disappear. We need not assume every provider is malicious. Uncertainty about one's rights is enough to change behavior.

In existing institutions, the allocation of resources and benefits is closely bound to the interests of particular people and groups, creating incentives to conceal information. A mathematician with an original idea, an entrepreneur with a new concept, or an independent game developer with an inventive mechanic may keep it secret, share only with trusted friends or partners, or try to do everything alone to protect their reputation and interests.

Beyond the new insight itself, much of the required work may already have mature solutions or people who could perform it more efficiently. Yet the originator reinvents those parts, slowing the growth of knowledge through limited information, inadequate cooperation, and less efficient work.

Secrecy may be rational for that individual. If completing the work alone takes three years while a team could do it in three months, but the three-month outcome gives someone else all the recognition and future opportunities, the researcher has reason to choose the three years.

From the mathematical community's perspective, however, many people are separately learning tools others already know and repeating attempts others have already abandoned. Originators lack help, and those able to help do not know whom to help. Some ideas never develop because their authors never meet suitable collaborators.

Self-protection under existing rules can thus obstruct shared creation. Changing the outcome requires changing the conditions that make cooperation unsafe.

### 2. How knowledge and intelligence generate each other

To understand this anxiety, we must look beyond attribution on one result: How is knowledge produced, and who controls the ability to produce it?

The new system would structurally change relationships among people and between people and AI.

The universe is an immense, dynamically evolving system whose regularities we seek to understand. Philosophy examines being and change; category theory studies objects and morphisms; physics studies states and transformations of matter, transfers and transformations of energy, and the relationship between particle and wave descriptions. Computing deals with data objects and program functions. At the scale of civilization, my concern is knowledge and intelligence.

These fields have distinct concepts that cannot simply be equated. Considering them together can nevertheless direct attention to both the structures a system has formed and the processes through which it changes. For civilization, knowledge is accumulated experience and understanding; intelligence enables that accumulation to be used, tested, and extended.

In this sense, knowledge and intelligence form a mutually transforming pair. Intelligence is the mechanism, capacity, and process that generates new knowledge. Existing knowledge supplies material from which intelligent capabilities are learned and formed. Each supports the other.

A mathematician learns definitions, theorems, and proofs, developing new ways of understanding problems. They use that ability to formulate conjectures and prove results, which others then learn. A comparable cycle can be examined in AI: existing knowledge informs training and inference; new research, once tested, enters public knowledge and subsequent learning. The question is not only how much material a repository accumulates, but how it makes the whole system better at finding problems, solving them, and correcting mistakes.

In early civilization, much knowledge—especially practical skill—was closely attached to particular biological individuals. Knowledge and the ability to acquire it were bound to people's material survival and economic lives. Skills and understanding were closely connected to physical products. Through exchanging labor, services, and products, people exchanged the benefits of their abilities.

A craftsperson teaching another person often needed prolonged demonstration and practice. Describing every step did not instantly reproduce the ability. Knowledge could travel, while competence still had to be developed in another body and mind. What someone knew and could do therefore became closely linked to their livelihood and position in cooperation.

### 3. From shared knowledge to shared intelligence

Language, writing, and printing greatly reduced the cost of communicating knowledge. More theory could circulate beyond its original holder. Printing and teaching could still be exchanged as goods or services, while published theoretical content became available for others to learn and extend. Practical skills and manufacturing know-how often remained subject to secrecy and conditions of use. With industrial civilization, more extensive ideals of sharing spread into these applied fields too.

An easily overlooked distinction matters here: giving someone a physical object may mean no longer possessing it; telling someone an idea usually leaves the speaker able to use it. The media, labor, and infrastructure of communication cost resources. Yet another person's understanding does not subtract an equal portion of knowledge from the original knower.

In an 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson, Thomas Jefferson compared the communication of ideas to lighting one candle from another: the recipient gains illumination without diminishing the giver's light. [Letter](https://monadnock.net/jefferson/mcpherson.html)

A widely circulated comparison makes a similar point: if each of us has an apple and we exchange them, each still has one apple; if each has an idea and we exchange them, each can have both ideas.

Discussion can do more than transfer two existing ideas. One person's question meeting another's method may produce a third idea neither possessed. Sharing knowledge thus helps create new knowledge.

Computers and the internet made data and code reproducible and transmissible at low marginal cost, enabling much wider sharing. Richard Stallman directed the ideal of freedom toward software as digital knowledge.

His initial GNU announcement in 1983 proposed a software system people could freely use and share. The freedom concerns running, studying, modifying, and redistributing software; it does not mean development, maintenance, and service provision have no cost. [Initial GNU announcement](https://www.gnu.org/gnu/initial-announcement.html), [GNU on free software and selling](https://www.gnu.org/philosophy/selling.en.html)

Git-based open-source cooperation offers a useful model for large, complex software systems. Such work needs many contributors with diverse skills, which one private company may struggle to assemble quickly and affordably through employment and outsourcing. Open projects can draw on interested users around the world. Every user becomes a potential contributor: some write code, others report bugs or provide test feedback.

Today, basic scientific theory, much foundational software, and some hardware designs are increasingly available through public literature, open code, and open designs for collective learning, use, and further development.

If the pre-AI information age dramatically lowered the cost of copying knowledge and advanced its open sharing, AI takes the question another step. Capabilities that participate in producing knowledge can now be deployed through models and software across people and devices. AI itself should follow knowledge toward becoming public infrastructure, with basic intelligent services progressively available to everyone without a charge at the point of use.

A trained model can be copied without repeating its entire training process, unlike educating another person to the same level of skill. Copying model files and continuously running them are nevertheless different activities. Training, inference, storage, communication, and maintenance consume resources and energy; deployment energy can be measured. [Research on AI deployment energy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16863v3)

“Free” here describes an institutional arrangement to be built: the community bears infrastructure and basic service costs so individuals need not repeatedly buy access from an exclusive owner whenever they need basic intelligent assistance. Allocation of finite public computing resources still requires shared decisions. The technical premise is widespread replication and reuse; the institutional purpose is everyone's development.

As sunlight and air are conditions of life, I hope public intelligence can become readily available support for each person's mental and intellectual activity—its nourishment and energy.

When a curious child asks a question, a worker notices a possible improvement, or a mathematics enthusiast sees a new connection, they should encounter shared capabilities that help the idea develop. A first spark should not remain stranded merely because its originator cannot afford an expensive service.

### 4. Shared intelligence needs shared organization

AI can gather extensive knowledge generated by human intellectual activity and use algorithms to learn from, organize, and distill it at scale. Large models encode learned relationships and patterns in many parameters and high-dimensional representations. They can integrate human intellectual work and participate in its continuing development.

A model is not a complete, error-free copy of all human knowledge. Effective shared intelligence also needs verifiable knowledge repositories, testing tools, human judgment, and real-world feedback. Yet the languages, mathematics, science, and practical experience on which it depends plainly cannot have been created from nothing by the final training team alone.

If this integration is organized chiefly by a small number of companies formed by elites within conventional capitalist political and economic institutions, under mutual distrust and competition, information separation and duplicated work remain continuing costs. Other nations, firms, and individuals have reasons to protect their interests and safety rather than entrust information and future decisions to someone else.

A fundamental question is therefore who controls AI. Whether a product is open or closed, if the organization behind it remains a conventional company with pyramidal power, opening the tool has not yet transformed the relationship.

Why do people fear such organizations' AI taking their work? In part because they have permission to use the AI without control over it.

AI as globally shared infrastructure should not be exclusively owned and governed by one person or company, or understood solely as a conventional commodity asset. Common rules are needed across the whole cycle: collecting training data, learning, deployment, new outputs, feedback, and further iteration. The creation, use, explanation, and recording of data and code; the exercise of decision power; the distribution of recognition and benefits; and the control of public resources all need jointly reviewable rules and records. No local individual or group should be able to dominate public intelligence unilaterally.

This does not erase individual creation. Researchers, data providers, developers, and maintainers perform real work. Their contributions should retain their origins, receive recognition, and be covered by appropriate arrangements. Making accumulated knowledge and capability a public foundation neither requires contributors to disappear nor authorizes a center to seize personal information.

What is public includes common rules, public resources and their use, and those processes and results of joint research that should be shared. Which personal materials someone supplies, and for what purposes, remains governed by explicit rights and permissions. Opening the operation of a public system is different from exposing every person's private life.

My proposal therefore goes beyond the ability to reclaim one's data. Software and services provided by a new organizational form could gradually replace commercial products. This goes further than open-source products alone: the entities that design, produce, and operate them would also follow the new arrangements. Organizational forms, distribution methods, and decision algorithms would all be open to inspection, traceable, and available for shared decision and construction.

If Earth is viewed as a vast civilizational intelligence, building a planetary AI that can gather relevant information about itself and its environment requires openness to humanity's cooperation. We need protocols analogous to those of open software, extended beyond existing data, code, and programs to the whole intelligent civilization: how nodes connect, how they cooperate, how systems run, and how shared decisions are made and carried out should become traceable, testable, revisable public knowledge.

This commonality can arise through many connected people, models, and organizations. Different research paths and judgments can coexist and be compared through shared interfaces, verifiable reasons, and feedback. A person uses common capabilities and contributes new findings; the common system grows and enables further individual creation. Knowledge and intelligence gain a new organizational form for their mutual development.
### 5. Sharing contributions without making contributors disappear

Unequal power and resources create structural weaknesses in how recognition and benefits are distributed between individuals and groups. Pursuing local interests, parties build information silos and become trapped in outcomes resembling the prisoner's dilemma, unable to pursue what would better advance civilization as a whole. The new mechanism seeks to change those underlying conditions.

Keeping rules under which sharing an idea may mean losing everything, while urging its originator not to care about personal rewards, asks the less powerful person to bear the risk of cooperation. A trustworthy system should show them how others build on the idea, which contributions remain theirs, and how the new result improves their research and life. The more useful and widely developed knowledge becomes, the clearer its origins should remain. Sharing and recognition can reinforce one another.

In software, we can inspect more than the final release. We can follow how it became what it is. Questions raised, implementations changed, bugs found, and checks added all remain in a shared history of creation.

What if that approach extended across human intellectual activity? A Merkle-DAG system could act as version control for the evolving “code” of civilization, recording individual contributions and their relationships. As productive capacity increasingly meets material needs, those records could support fairer evaluation of contributions and a more reasonable distribution of recognition.

I want to reduce the secrecy and isolated effort generated when people and companies compete for material rewards and prestige under exclusive knowledge arrangements. Information barriers among a system's parts influence its development by obstructing full cooperation. In game-theoretic terms, I seek a way out of prisoner's dilemmas and locally trapped outcomes, making reciprocal openness and contribution conditions for shared improvement. This could offer an alternative to competitive, exclusive intellectual-property and patent arrangements: share intellectual innovations while preserving attribution, and use a versioned history of civilizational knowledge to recognize contributions in greater detail and more fairly.

For mathematics, the record would include more than a finished paper: how a problem was posed, how a conjecture changed, which prior results a proof used, which counterexample forced a revision, and who supplied the missing step. Knowledge would have both a logical structure and a history of formation.

This proposal is not limited to mathematics. Across human knowledge, materials can be located within an evolving space, with their relative positions, connections, and paths of development recorded. Distance in this space need not be physical distance or elapsed time.

In early civilization, geographical separation and time strongly constrained such connections. Collaboration depended on opportunities to meet, later on the reach of books and letters. In the information age, the connections within networks increasingly shape the distance between ideas.

### 6. Record an idea's growth from its first appearance

An unfinished thought should also be able to become a knowledge-network node. It may be as small as “These two problems might share a structure.” It must be represented honestly: a conjecture remains a conjecture; only a proved result is labeled proved.

The first record belongs in a digital space controlled by the originator. Original words, formulas, diagrams, and revisions are retained. The person then chooses whether to share with a few collaborators or the community. Others receive a research object with a source and conditions of use.

A Merkle-DAG allows content and references to be checked by content identity. Changes produce new versions; later results point to the versions actually used. If originators and collaborators retain copies, they need not rely solely on a central platform's account. But a hash establishes content, not who first thought of it anywhere in the world. Identity, provenance, prior literature, and independent discovery still need investigation. Recording research does not compel anyone to expose private life or unfinished thoughts.

The framework's core value here is addressable, tamper-detectable, verifiable records of states and changes across digital space and time: data and code structures, their histories, program execution, data flows, and human and computer operations.

The efficiency of an evolving complex system depends partly on its organization and its ability to trace and revisit local objects, states, and actions. Those capabilities affect both the speed and quality of constructive iteration and recovery from errors or collapse.

A mathematician can then use tools under their own control or delegate work to a jointly maintained service. Training use and disclosure to others are separate choices. Asking one question must not imply unlimited authorization.

New services can record sources as material enters. Older models trained on mixed data without item-level provenance cannot promise to reconstruct every idea's origin afterward. Traceable retrieval, citation, and research records must therefore become routine. An unknown source must remain unknown; AI must not invent a contribution story.

### 7. How a seed of insight can grow into a tree of theory

We want potentially valuable mathematical insights to move quickly from a mathematician's or enthusiast's mind into the global intelligence network and find the conditions to grow efficiently. They should not remain locked in one person's head because recognition and livelihood seem at risk, forcing the originator to perform every task alone, including work they could do only slowly or must first learn.

Consider a hypothetical research project. A young researcher notices that several complicated terms in a class of equations appear to cancel after a transformation. They suspect an existing estimate can be strengthened, but do not know whether the claim is true and cannot prove it alone.

First, the researcher and their AI clarify the observation: which equations, which transformation, which examples, and which conditions? The AI searches for related work and marks unresolved questions. The resulting object is still a conjecture. Recording it does not make it a discovery already established.

Second, within the researcher's permissions, the question reaches nodes able to contribute. One person recognizes a lemma from another field that may explain the cancellation. Another reformulates the problem for computation. AI helps retrieve literature, manipulate symbols, and make initial trials of different approaches. The originator need not master every tool before being entitled to collaborate.

Third, a collaborator finds a counterexample. The original conjecture is false, but the process has not returned to zero. The counterexample reveals a boundary: perhaps another condition is needed or the problem should be split into cases. The original conjecture, counterexample, and revised conjecture all remain. Finding the counterexample is a contribution because it prevents further work down an excluded path.

Fourth, a new proof develops. Someone supplies a lemma, another handles special cases, and another expresses the proof in a checkable form. Positive computational examples help exploration but do not stand in for proof across all cases. Formalizable portions go through suitable tools, with definitions and assumptions open to review. Only results that pass the corresponding checks enter the verified portion of knowledge.

Fifth, the result becomes someone else's starting point. The original researcher can see how the observation was developed. Those who supplied lemmas, found counterexamples, and completed the proof each retain a place. Later work need not all be credited to the first person, and the first insight need not disappear from the final result.

Independent discoveries can retain separate paths that later converge. Unused branches can retain reasons for their rejection. Knowledge branches and merges: a tree is only a metaphor; the actual structure is closer to a forest with connected roots.

What becomes shorter is the time spent waiting, searching, repeating work, and crossing knowledge gaps. Mathematical difficulty remains. Some seeds do not grow; some were mistaken from the start. The mechanism improves the chance that a valuable seed finds suitable conditions and that everyone learns from both success and failure.

The contribution chain can continue into the next cycle of public intelligence. Which verified results entered a repository? Which materials were licensed for training? Which model update used them, and whose later research did the update support? These relationships should be retained wherever they can be verified. We need to see both how discoveries arise and how the common ability to make discoveries accumulates. Entry into a model must not erase origins, usage conditions, and contributions that ought to remain visible.

### 8. Recognizing contributions without intensifying the struggle over credit

Does seeing the whole process automatically tell us how much recognition each person deserves?

We can record precisely who wrote what, but word counts and commit counts cannot substitute for contribution value. One line may change the direction; a thousand repetitive lines may add no knowledge. Precision gives evaluation better evidence. Value still depends on the role a contribution played.

Posing a question, discovering a structure, proving a result, finding an error, maintaining a tool, and organizing collaboration are different kinds of work. They need not all be forced into one ranking from first to last. We should see what a person actually accomplished, rather than an aggregate score detached from the work.

In the mature system I envision, the share of provision serving personal material needs would be based on those needs. Evaluation of contributions would primarily recognize creative work and the fulfillment associated with that recognition.

Resources would fall into two categories. One meets individual needs for survival and development, allocated in light of physiological and psychological needs and actual resource conditions. The other comprises common resources, whose use is decided through the dynamic-weight network of participants.

In mathematics, the distinction is straightforward. Basic conditions for living, learning, and developing should not depend on securing exclusive status over a discovery. Public computing, materials, and collaboration time should be allocated according to research needs, potential value, and resource costs.

Sharing an idea would then no longer mean staking one's entire livelihood. Contributions deserve recognition, but recognition need not confer monopoly over a field or permanent authority over other researchers. Others' work strengthens the result, which becomes a shared foundation for the originator's further creation as well.

A database of names is therefore insufficient. If people must still race to publish to secure basic living conditions, incentives to conceal remain. Records, provision, and decisions need to be designed together. Early organizations can guarantee only what their resources support. Yet the direction can be clear from the first day: do not use basic needs to control people; do not turn contribution evaluation into a hierarchy stripping participation rights; progressively expand shared conditions for development.

### 9. Making the network's capabilities available to each person

The same applies to mathematicians. If the strongest tools remain under another party's exclusive control, researchers may become more dependent as those tools improve. Researchers need a role in deciding how tools are used, where public resources go, and how contributions are recognized.

In a pyramid, leaders hold power over major decisions while a large gap separates them from frontline workers. That gap affects both distribution and decisions. Often the people closest to a practical need understand it best but cannot decide the relevant policies or practices. Senior leaders hold that authority without necessarily knowing the local conditions as well. Policy needs a broad view, but broad and local knowledge must be connected through an appropriate distribution of decision power.

Fixing authority to hierarchical rank does not ensure more accurate or efficient judgments. I seek dynamic weights: people whose understanding or predictions are more likely to be correct and useful on a particular question should receive greater consideration on that question.

Decision nodes would no longer be permanently identified with elite individuals or groups. Different participants' capabilities would contribute to shared judgments continually revised through feedback. This could form relationships of production better suited to automated intellectual work. Just as machines changed physical labor and helped transform organization, changes in intellectual labor call for new arrangements of cooperation and decision-making.

On one side, knowledge people choose to share can be situated in a semantic space and in the evolving histories of theories, knowledge, and strategies, forming a jointly maintained model foundation grounded in testable public knowledge. On the other, people can choose to record their own digital materials through Merkle-DAGs, including the evolution of their understanding and thought. Within their chosen permissions, these materials can support post-training of personal models representing their information, preferences, and outlook.

The personal model helps express the person's interests and opinions. Routine matters can be delegated within authorization; important matters are discussed with the user before advice is offered. Public decisions are recorded for later feedback and weight adjustment. Meanwhile, people learn through participation, revise their understanding, and update the personal models supporting their next judgments.

In mathematics, expertise in a method earns greater consideration on relevant questions. It does not create greater authority in every field or oblige students to accept every judgment. Reputation cannot replace proof.

Evaluation rules themselves must be reviewable. The evidence chosen and reasons for changes remain recorded. Disputes can be reviewed by participants without directly tied interests; unresolved disagreements remain visible. Methods can be compared in parallel, groups can retain different judgments, and no single evaluation creates permanent authority. A person can move their records to another node without losing their contribution history by leaving a group.

Sharing results and feedback makes useful methods easier to find and errors easier to correct. No individual can master every field, but a community can connect distributed abilities.

A personal agent can help someone understand their interests, preferences, principles, needs, rights, responsibilities, and relationships. In economic, political, and social activity, AI-assisted reasoning can improve judgment and bargaining capacity, supporting more equal and rational interaction. This creates room to explore new systems of rights and responsibilities, collective decisions, organizational forms, and institutions.

Physical work provides an analogy. A person's strength, precision, and speed constrain what they can do. Cooperation without machinery remains limited: more people may combine force, but speed and precision do not simply add with headcount. Even combined force is limited by available space. Machines made many tasks beyond bodily capacity possible.

Human intellect also has limits. AI is machinery for intellectual work, potentially helping us perform tasks faster and better than an individual could.

The civilization-scale general intelligence I envision connects countless people and organizations, public cloud AI systems of many sizes, personal local AI, robots, automated equipment, and sensors through a dynamic-weight network. Together, they gather, process, analyze, and create data; write code; build knowledge and decision systems; and undertake intellectual and physical activities beyond the unaided capacities of biological individuals and groups.

Human agency does not require people to perform every research step by hand. Enabling an idea to develop far beyond one's individual capacities through a network remains active creation. People can choose cooperation, understand progress, change direction, and learn from the result. “People” here means each particular person, not merely the few who control tools and resources.
### 10. From a student's insight to invention at the grassroots

This is a broad class of problems, not one peculiar to AI. In a research group, a supervisor may take a student's insight and, with deeper expertise, develop it into a discovery. If recognition is then decided mainly by the supervisor who controls evaluation and resources, the student may not receive credit proportionate to their contribution.

Before modern digital systems, recording many people's expressed ideas and their development through collaboration in detail and at low cost was difficult. Contribution evaluation based on such records was correspondingly constrained. Recognition often depended heavily on the participants' understanding, feelings, intentions, character, and organizational position.

A connection mentioned by a student in discussion or an improvement proposed by a worker on site often never entered the final paper or report. By the time the result was complete, memory could not reliably reconstruct who said or solved what. Even fair-minded participants had limited recollections and perspectives. If the person controlling records, names, and interpretation was also unfair, the weaker party had fewer ways to establish their contribution.

Earlier societies did, of course, have letters, manuscripts, laboratory notes, journals, learned societies, and attribution conventions. These already preserved and recognized some contributions. But their records were often scattered, late, and unable to cover everyday collaboration. Preservation, access, and interpretation also depended on organizational relationships. Technical limits and unequal power could reinforce one another.

Some people resisted sharing credit, while others actively recognized it. The disputes involving Newton, Leibniz, and Hooke; Darwin and Wallace's joint publication; and Perelman's objections to the allocation of honors show that the issue long predates AI.

Newton's calculus-priority dispute with Leibniz and his disputes with Hooke concerned different historical events. A particularly revealing fact is that, during the Royal Society's 1712 investigation of calculus priority, Newton was both a party to the dispute and, as president, involved in choosing the committee and writing its report. [Newton Project chronology](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/his-life-and-work-at-a-glance)

Preserved letters and manuscripts therefore do not by themselves create a fair procedure. Someone with a direct interest who also controls the review process may possess great scholarly ability and high status without giving the other party confidence in the process. We need to examine both who proposed what and who has authority to interpret the records.

Darwin and Wallace illustrate another possibility. On July 1, 1858, Charles Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker arranged for their materials to be presented jointly to the Linnean Society. The accompanying letter explicitly recognized their independent development of the ideas and the originality of both contributions. [Original joint publication](https://darwin-online.org.uk/content/contentblock?basepage=1&itemID=F350&hitpage=1&viewtype=text)

Two people can each make a genuine discovery without one erasing the other. Yet this arrangement still depended on particular people identifying the materials and coordinating actively. We should turn what is valuable in such conduct into dependable procedures available to ordinary people. A smaller contributor or someone of lower status should not have to encounter a prominent person willing to act fairly before receiving recognition.

Perelman brings another question into focus: how should the person completing a breakthrough regard the work that made it possible? He declined the Fields Medal in 2006. [International Mathematical Union record](https://www.mathunion.org/imu-awards/fields-medal) When declining the million-dollar Millennium Prize in 2010, he told Interfax that he had several reasons and explicitly considered Hamilton's contribution to the solution no less than his own. [2010 interview report](https://www.interfax.ru/russia/143587)

The question is how a jointly created achievement can recognize its successive stages proportionately. If recognition requires someone to refuse an award as a protest, whatever we think of that choice, we can ask whether contribution relationships could instead be recorded throughout the research and brought into shared evaluation before the final ceremony.

Today's technology provides new conditions for more consistent and fair procedures, and for gradually replacing practices excessively dependent on individual discretion.

As people communicate, research, and produce through digital systems, they can choose to connect records of ideas, revisions, tests, adoption, and feedback. Original statements and later interpretations can remain distinct, and participants can retain their own copies. Ordinary people then gain another way to establish facts when seeking recognition.

More detailed records improve the basis for evaluation; the rules for interpretation still require shared agreement. The roles of insight, proof, organization, testing, and correction can be examined along the actual work, with further review where disagreement remains. We need not first convert every contribution into an absolutely precise score before recognizing creativity previously overlooked.

Developing a vague idea into a rigorous theory may require profound expertise. Why should acknowledging a supervisor's achievement require obscuring a student's? Why not describe what each actually did?

The student's observation, the supervisor's reformulation, and proofs supplied by other members can be recorded separately and connected. Helping an idea grow deserves credit, as does seeing a relationship others missed. In a dispute, the student should be able to retrieve records, seek review, and continue research rather than appeal only to the same authority controlling resources. A supervisor's recognition can also reflect how many ideas they helped develop and how many people they enabled to create independently.

The same applies in business. A frontline worker identifies waste, another suggests an improvement, an engineer develops a solution, and a manager coordinates equipment and funding. Every stage may contain a real contribution. Remembering only the business leader allows position to obscure grassroots creativity. Records should retain the stages for joint examination by those producing, using, and improving the service. Coordination is a capability deserving recognition for its actual role, not automatic ownership of everyone else's creations.

Public governance needs the same perspective. Household production-contract experiments took place in Yongjia, Zhejiang, in 1956; explorations by Li Guimao, Li Yunhe, Dai Jietian, and others are documented in party-history records. [Zhejiang party-history materials](https://www.zjzzgz.gov.cn/art/2011/4/25/art_1413013_14828112.html) In remarks on rural policy on May 31, 1980, Deng Xiaoping also explicitly endorsed grassroots practices in Feixi and Fengyang, Anhui. [Record of the remarks](https://cpc.people.com.cn/n1/2018/0703/c69113-30107101.html)

Institutional change includes recognizing problems, proposing methods, undertaking trials, summarizing experience, providing support, and expanding implementation. Grassroots experimentation and higher-level support can both matter. Recognizing one need not remove the other.

Since records already preserve these names, it would be wrong to say they received no recognition. Yet when public understanding remembers only a few prominent names, the specific roles of distributed creators remain hard to see. Public knowledge should let people follow a policy back to trials, originators, implementers, feedback, and independent paths of exploration.

This can improve later decisions too. A method is adopted because of what it improved under particular conditions. When those conditions change, the method can change without every revision becoming a challenge to someone's authority.

As more people adopt these recording and evaluation practices, the practices may also change when people share ideas, creating an accelerating effect.

If people trust that sharing preserves recognition, wider adoption may produce a cycle. More people share ideas early; anyone who waits to develop an idea alone knows that someone else may independently conceive and publish something similar in the meantime. The possibility of losing the first public opportunity, together with the benefit of receiving help sooner, encourages prompt recording and appropriately scoped sharing.

Suppose a mathematics enthusiast suspects that two apparently unrelated problems can be approached with one method. Learning both fields completely and proving everything alone may take years. Another researcher may reach a similar conclusion along a different path first. Recording the original connection, distinguishing present understanding from conjecture, and seeking collaborators through the network can instead turn waiting time into shared research time.

Timely sharing makes the starting point easier to identify and gives others earlier opportunities to continue. Proofs, counterexamples, and revised conditions create further contributions. More collaborators can test a valuable connection; if it advances later work, its originator's role becomes visible through those relationships. Actual experience of this process gives people reason to bring their next insight into the network.

Recognition must support this acceleration appropriately. First publication is a recordable fact, but independent discovery, proof, and application each have value. Independent similar ideas should retain separate evidence; influence should be recorded where it exists. A message a few minutes earlier must not acquire ownership of all later work, and a content-free guess must not reserve every possible result.

The originator is seeking timely entry into shared creation. Recognition for early sharing should correspond to what they actually contributed and the role it played. People may still choose temporary privacy without losing the right to establish their contribution later from genuine records. The appeal of early sharing then comes from available cooperation and trustworthy recognition, supporting lasting trust.

If dynamic decision mechanisms combine evidence, tests, and feedback to reduce the chance of mistaken ideas being executed immediately, another obstacle can weaken. People need not hide sound ideas for fear of losing recognition, or refrain from discussing immature ideas merely because they may be wrong.

The stages of development must remain distinct. A new idea enters as a hypothesis to be tested. Relevant knowledge, evidence, and discussion guide whether to investigate it. Verification determines whether it is established. Affecting equipment, public resources, or other people's lives requires the appropriate decisions and execution conditions. Proposing an idea does not grant it authority to alter reality.

An employee suggesting a new production process need not remain silent because success is uncertain. The system can compare existing information, invite review by relevant people and AI, and test the proposal in simulation or a limited trial. Appropriate participants decide the resource budget and stopping conditions beforehand. Broader use is considered after sufficient results. The idea enters shared research early while evidence for execution accumulates progressively.

Dynamic weighting helps decide how to consider participants' judgments on a particular issue and revise that consideration through outcomes. High weight cannot turn a judgment into fact. Many agreeing judgments may share one error. Counterexamples, dissent, independent checks, and opportunities to stop and revise must therefore belong within the network. Strong evidence from a newcomer must be able to change established judgments. Only sustained practical effectiveness justifies expecting fewer errors to reach execution.

An honestly proposed conjecture later disproved does not discredit the originator's entire ability. A failed attempt or counterexample may eliminate an unproductive route. The record should show what was learned and how to improve, rather than attach a permanent label to a person for an immature expression. Honest uncertainty and openness to testing are also different from hiding a known problem or acting without authorization; they require different evaluation.

The relationship I seek encourages people to speak earlier because real contributions remain traceable and the community can help test and develop them. Individuals need not complete everything alone to deserve recognition. Communities need not immediately execute every suggestion to encourage creativity. The courage to propose, the ability to test, the capacity to continue another's work, and the willingness to correct mistakes support one another, helping more insights become useful knowledge sooner.

### 11. Begin with one collaboration whose record can be checked

The first step toward a global intelligence network can be a few researchers, students, and AI systems working on one real problem. Record the ideas and derivations they choose to share, preserving original words and sources. Connect new work to the material actually used and explain revisions. Each participant retains records, the public portion is held jointly, and purposes and dispute procedures are agreed in advance.

Then test the results. Are people more willing to share? Do collaborators connect more easily? Is duplicated work reduced? Are results easier to review? Do initial contributions remain visible? Include the costs of recording and coordination. If people instead become preoccupied with maintaining scores or more afraid to disagree, investigate and change the implementation.

Better collective outcomes are a goal pursued through continuing feedback. Mathematical correctness needs proof; effective collaboration needs practical comparison. Dynamic weights do not themselves produce a perfect equilibrium. Traceable contributions, control over research materials, and the progressive separation of basic needs from exclusive competition provide concrete, testable conditions for fuller cooperation.

I hope for a future in which everyone can record the parts of life they choose and use AI to understand knowledge and information more effectively. Every small insight someone wishes to share can be preserved, communicated, and developed. A global network connecting people and AI can maintain and enrich existing knowledge, helping insights become tested additions through shared exploration, then feeding them back into public knowledge in a continuing cycle.

A seed of insight need not wait for its owner to become an entire library before entering shared creation. When it grows into a tree of theory, the person who planted it should not disappear. Others' abilities extend the originator's reach; acknowledging predecessors does not deny successors' creativity. Everyone can be a creator, and no one need do everything alone.

## Afterword: Let Everyone Become a Creator

Beginning with existence, change, and constructibility, this book attempts a unified way of thinking about cosmic evolution, the constitution of matter, the emergence of consciousness, and humanity's future. It is an ambitious undertaking, and many of its ideas may seem radical or even implausible.

Their range is wide, sometimes seemingly fantastic. Yet intuitions arising from basic questions make me want to explore this path seriously. The evolution of human institutions is, after all, one local instance of the universe's evolution on a small planet. I hope to study it through the relationships among fixed points, minimization, and emergence, and to construct mechanisms that better support civilization's development. Repeated human interaction and the formation of relatively stable cooperation are themselves processes through which structures arise. “Tit for Tat” may sound violent, and history has indeed often adjusted economic power, political authority, and decision influence through destructive punishment and wasteful rewards. I want information feedback, dynamic weights, and revisable shared decisions to reduce that destruction and waste, forming stable structures better suited to living and creating together at lower cost.

Where does such a large ambition begin? If everyone must agree, all resources must be available, and every theoretical question must be settled first, no new cooperation can start. But expansion without putting the proposed organization into practice can also recreate the relationships it was meant to change.

The first step should therefore be small enough for one person to take, while carrying a direction clear enough for others to understand what they would be joining. Reclaim personal data and build a controllable space for knowledge and storage. Begin with one real service and learn to maintain it together. Preserve one original statement, one revision, or one failure so that contributions and decisions can be traced. Tell a story or explain a problem clearly enough for others to decide whether to participate.

Together, these activities form the seed I mean. It contains software and ways for people to cooperate; the preservation of knowledge and the generation of new knowledge; solutions for today and room for tomorrow's capabilities to grow.

So far, the idea has attracted at least one person: me. Through conversation online and in everyday life, I hope to bring others in. Many people's efforts can turn this seed into a great tree.

Participation does not require accepting every conclusion beforehand. People can arrive with questions, disagreements, and their own experience. Making a problem clear, leaving a record of an attempt, and sharing knowledge others can build on already contributes to constructing this future.

The civilization I hope for would not leave most people waiting for a few to create, or ask a superintelligence to live everyone's lives for them. Each person could learn, judge, practice, and create, without having to carry everything alone. Human differences would become sources of complementarity; accumulated common capabilities would support each person's further development.

We cannot write all of the future's answers today. We can begin changing how those answers are produced.

## Appendix: A Critical Assessment of the Theory and Its Practical Feasibility

I do not want this theoretical and practical framework to remain an imaginative world with no path into reality. Even where its scenes resemble future science fiction, I want them to be the kind of scientific anticipation that practice can progressively test and make possible.

The stronger that ambition, the more carefully I must distinguish what I hope will happen, what is logically possible, what evidence supports, and what has actually been achieved. A coherent narrative is not proof of every inference. Improvement in one experiment does not validate an entire theory of civilization.

For now, this should be understood as a layered research and construction program, built step by step and continuously open to counterexamples. It contains established mathematical tools, systems that can be built with existing technology, and interdisciplinary hypotheses still requiring formalization and testing. Not every distant proposal must succeed before the work has value. But unachieved results must not be described as settled.

### Which questions need proof, which need experiments, and which need shared choices?

First, the book's claims are not all the same kind of knowledge. They require different standards of judgment.

| Kind of claim | What would support it | What cannot substitute for that support |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematical definitions and theorems | Explicit objects, rules, assumptions, proofs, models, and counterexamples | Similar language, attractive analogies, or many successful examples |
| Empirical explanations of nature and society | Observable quantities, discriminating predictions, evidence, and repeated testing | Explaining only known outcomes or treating correlation as causation |
| Engineering architectures | Defined tasks and authority boundaries, working implementations, and verification within those bounds | An architecture diagram, the existence of code, or a small demonstration |
| Principles of distribution, rights, and shared life | Public reasons about values, participants' choices, actual consequences, and revisable procedures | Declaring one institution uniquely correct on the basis of a natural law |

Proving an error bound for a weighting algorithm under a specified loss function is a mathematical question. Whether it improves real groups' judgments is empirical. Whether it has authority to decide someone's life also involves values and authorization. These questions can inform one another without substituting for one another.

The assessment below therefore does not give the whole framework one score. It examines major claims and their connections: how far the evidence reaches, where additional conditions are needed, and what findings would make me revise a judgment.

### Infinity, self-reference, and expanding mathematical systems

My starting points in Zeno's paradoxes, limits, and self-reference seek to understand stable objects and continuing change. This remains a useful entry point only if it respects established distinctions. In the usual real-number system, 0.999… equals 1. Finite truncations and the limit they define are different objects. An observer's inability to write an infinite sequence step by step does not prove the limit nonexistent.

“Finite infinity” is intended to describe a finite space beyond a system's practical processing capacity. It can be an intuitive expression for resource limits. To become a mathematical tool, it needs an observer's time, space, available operations, and a definition of processing capacity. The power set of a finite set remains finite. Rapid combinatorial growth does not itself produce a new infinite cardinal. Computability, computation within a budget, enumeration, and description by formula must be treated separately.

Likewise, self-reference producing a cycle does not mean all self-reference is contradictory. A self-calling program may terminate, continue indefinitely, or encounter a specification its rules cannot satisfy. Syntax, semantics, and inference rules must be fixed before deciding which applies. Alternating truth values can be studied as a dynamic process, but that does not by itself solve a logical paradox.

The dynamic mathematical system I envisage would extend its tools at boundaries instead of remaining forever within one axiom set. This connects with research on extending theories. Turing's work on ordinal logics examined progressive strengthening in response to forms of incompleteness. But a system that remains effectively axiomatized, consistent, and sufficiently expressive for basic arithmetic does not escape incompleteness merely because it can be extended. [Turing's paper on ordinal logics and accompanying discussion](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~halorenz/4students/Literatur/TuringOrdinalLogic.pdf)

Research can ask how a system records currently unprovable propositions, proposes candidate axioms, distinguishes their consequences, preserves branches, and checks which assumptions a result depends on. Merkle-DAGs can preserve provenance and dependencies; formal proof tools can check particular derivations. Their record structures do not guarantee that new axioms are true or that the whole system is infallible.

A theory that continually adds explanations without identifying abandoned derivations or falsified predictions may grow in size without growing in knowledge. Progress should yield clearer theorems, counterexamples, distinguishable branches, and reviewable reasoning.

### What mathematical content must a Dualoid add?

I proposed the Dualoid as a basic construct expressing existence, change, and constructibility together, reorganizing the relationship between objects and actions. This can be a research program, but those properties do not yet provide a complete mathematical definition.

Two meanings of duality especially need separation. An opposite category preserves objects while reversing arrows and the direction of composition; it does not exchange objects and morphisms. The prevalence of dual structures in category theory therefore does not establish that objects and morphisms must be two aspects of a deeper entity. That claim needs its own definitions and derivation. Under appropriate conditions, the Yoneda perspective characterizes an object through a complete, compatible structure of relationships. It does not imply that observing a few external reactions reveals every internal feature. [Riehl, Category Theory in Context: duality and the Yoneda lemma](https://emilyriehl.github.io/files/context.pdf)

A mathematical Dualoid would need to specify what qualifies as one, when two can compose, whether composition is associative, how identities arise, and which relations express equality, isomorphism, or behavioral equivalence. If internal structure changes, it must state what remains invariant, which relationships change with versions, and how those changes remain compatible with external use.

Small examples provide a place to test this. Functions between finite sets, finite-state machines, and typed computational modules are easier to define than the entire universe. The proposal should recover the relevant known structures, then identify what it adds: a new composition theorem, a clearer account of state and action, or an incompatibility an older formulation obscures.

Equivalence to an existing structure could itself be a useful clarification. Different terminology alone does not establish new mathematical foundations. If new structure is added, explicit models should satisfy its rules, its consequences should be examined relative to existing foundations, and unnecessary assumptions should be identified.

Subobject classifiers need similar boundaries. In the category of sets, truth values can characterize membership. In more general suitable categories, the truth-value structure and internal logic depend on mathematical conditions. A classifier is not inherently an entity with perception, memory, or action. Modeling a detector also requires physical inputs, state, error, control, and energy. [Fong and Spivak's applied category theory text](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s097-applied-category-theory-january-iap-2019/a4175d61479a35340d6307ae5e48ef5a_18-s097iap19textbook.pdf)

I should therefore retain this direction while stating its present status: a proposal for construction awaiting formalization, not a proved theory replacing all existing mathematics.

### The missing bridges among fixed points, minimization, and maximization

Fixed points, minimization/maximization, and emergence form one of my central research ideas. Their relationship needs explicit construction. Putting the words together does not complete the derivation between them.

A fixed point first means only that f(x) = x: a state is unchanged by a transformation. It does not say trajectories from elsewhere reach that state. For f(x) = −x, zero is fixed but a trajectory starting at 1 alternates between 1 and −1. For f(x) = 2x, zero remains fixed while nearby nonzero states move away. For f(x) = x/2, iterations approach zero. Fixed-point existence accompanies very different dynamics.

The Banach fixed-point theorem gives existence, uniqueness, and iterative convergence under conditions including a contraction on a nonempty complete metric space. We cannot omit those conditions and treat every persistent social organization or natural structure as an instance of the same convergence theorem. [Mathematical discussion of contractions](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~bindel/nmds/00-Background/04-Calculus.html)

Minimization and maximization also require care. Reducing internal cost and expanding useful external connections are research directions that may conflict. Fault tolerance needs redundancy; privacy restricts some connections; additional functions require structure and maintenance. These are more plausibly interacting objectives than quantities that always attain their extremes together.

An investigation can fix a task, require preservation of function, and separately measure implementation cost, fault tolerance, and reuse, then compare trade-offs. A combined score must disclose its weights. Changing the metric after observing results and calling the selected outcome inevitable would not test the principle.

Emergence also needs an account of the new capability and why a list of components alone fails to explain it. A sorting program reaching a fixed point has not thereby become alive. Coordinated behavior does not necessarily minimize a known cost. Structure, dynamics, task, and environment must be specified before their relationship can be tested.

A preliminary test could use finite-state systems or small software compositions with states, transitions, functional requirements, and costs fixed in advance. Compare constructions guided by these principles with alternatives. If the framework merely redescribes known results without new derivations, predictions, or construction advantages, its value in that scope is explanatory organization, not evidence for a new law.

### A shorter description is not necessarily a cheaper construction

Connecting Occam's razor, Kolmogorov complexity, and actual construction first requires distinguishing length, time, space, and energy.

Relative to a chosen universal computational model, Kolmogorov complexity measures the shortest description generating a string. It is generally uncomputable. Finding a shorter program improves an upper bound without proving that the shortest program has been found. [Vitányi on uncomputability](https://homepages.cwi.nl/~paulv/papers/incomp.pdf)

A tiny program can produce a result through an enormous search. A longer program can answer the same question quickly using a prepared index. The index itself takes work to build, store, and update. Comparing one execution may miss preparation costs; comparing only code length may miss extensive repeated computation.

Merkle-DAGs identify identical content and support sharing, but fewer nodes do not inherently mean lower energy use. Hashing, addressing, indexing, verification, encryption, transfer, and migration all cost resources. Direct object processing may reduce text parsing and repeated reasoning, while adding overhead on small tasks. Comparisons must include construction and use under the same functional requirements, accuracy, hardware, and budget.

The thermodynamics of computation likewise cannot be reduced to a fixed energy cost for every logical step. Bennett's discussion of reversible computation and Landauer's principle distinguishes logical reversibility, information erasure, and physical dissipation. These constrain cost analysis; they do not convert character counts into electricity readings. [Bennett on Landauer's principle and reversible computation](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0210005)

I should accordingly use “minimization” with explicit meaning: what is minimized, under which constraints, and whether preparation, maintenance, and recovery are included. Report what decreased, what increased, and what was not measured. One representation may be better on some tasks while another retains advantages elsewhere. Such comparisons are more likely to identify worthwhile constructions than a prior declaration that the entire old ecosystem must be inefficient.
### A common scientific picture is not yet a unified physical theory

I compare existence and change, objects and morphisms, matter and energy, and knowledge and intelligence to look for structures connecting levels. Formal resemblance does not give these concepts one rigorous definition. Physical relationships need units, equations, and measurements; categorical objects and morphisms depend on the category chosen. Particle–wave relations cannot be derived merely by calling them a unity of two aspects.

A physical theory would need a state space, evolution rules, observables, and a correspondence with established theories and experiments. It must recover tested results within their domain and make discriminating predictions where it claims to go further. Saying it explains every level does not meet that standard.

Minimization principles require particular care. In classical mechanics, the action principle requires, under appropriate conditions, that the first variation of action vanish along a physical path. The phrase “least action” does not mean that every system chooses absolute minimum energy, the shortest program, or maximum social efficiency. Action, energy, and algorithmic cost are different quantities. [Tong's lectures on the Lagrangian formalism](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/dynamics/dynhtml/S2.html)

Established research also explains how local order can coexist with the second law of thermodynamics. Open systems can sustain structure through continuing exchanges. Prigogine investigated nonequilibrium conditions that support certain ordered dynamic structures. This supports research into structure formation and maintenance; it does not imply that the entire universe must become more complex or that life and civilization must develop in my preferred direction. [Prigogine, Time, Structure, and Fluctuations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17738519/)

If a more general construction principle is proposed to explain such processes, a first test could select a system with known equations—a simple reaction network or oscillator—and specify new variables, mappings, and predictions. With equations and parameters fixed, test whether it distinguishes stability, oscillation, bifurcation, or structural disappearance beforehand, rather than labeling every outcome afterward as some combination of stability and change.

Quantum questions require comparable discipline. Logical indeterminacy, classical randomness, periodic switching of truth values, and quantum superposition are not the same phenomenon. A new quantum interpretation needs state and measurement rules that reproduce calculable interference and correlations. Replacing terminology with “self-reference” or “observer” is insufficient. Bell experiments impose explicit constraints on a class of local-realist models, which a new account must confront. [Giustina and colleagues' Bell experiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.03190)

Deriving elementary particles from Dualoids, explaining string theory's candidate space, or reconciling general relativity with quantum theory remains a more distant research agenda. This book has not supplied the equations, derivations, and experimental comparisons needed to treat those questions as solved. Even a substantial software efficiency gain would not replace independent tests of physical predictions.

The wish for unification need not be abandoned. It must be divided into questions that can enter particular disciplines. A cross-level analogy that fails should be removable without bringing down the entire practical program.

### Life, consciousness, and social behavior require validation at each level

How lower-level structures participate in higher-level abilities is a valuable question. Participation in a process is still different from explaining it. Neurons, bodies, and environments contribute to human activity, but labeling the levels as objects, morphisms, and subcategories does not complete a theory of consciousness. A rigorous categorical account requires explicit mappings; an analogy should remain identified as one.

Distinguishing self from environment, retaining memory, sustaining internal dialogue, and using external feedback can guide actual intelligent-system designs. Yet self-description, long-running tasks, or repeated inner monologues do not by themselves establish subjective experience. Continuous self-dialogue may also repeat an initial error until the system appears increasingly confident.

Engineering experiments can compare long-term memory, active questioning, and reflection against alternatives under the same budget. Can the system find new evidence, correct mistakes, and transfer learning to unfamiliar tasks? External review and controlled comparisons help establish functional improvement instead of merely more humanlike self-description. Even demonstrated functional gains remain distinct from philosophical and scientific questions about experience.

Human physiology and psychology likewise require a distinction between broad understanding and specific causal chains. Research supports interacting influences from genetics, physiology, upbringing, and social environments. A chain from one gene to punishment preferences, then hawkish or dovish attitudes, and finally democratic or authoritarian institutions still lacks several layers of validation. A 2024 systematic review covering 87 studies of childhood aggression genetics found inconsistent conclusions for many candidate-gene main and interaction effects, alongside important measurement and sample differences. [Systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11167064/)

Examples involving MAOA can open research into biological–environmental interaction. They must not justify political or moral labels for people or groups, or become necessary premises of the institutional proposal. Even if a particular genetic explanation fails, data sovereignty, verifiable collaboration, and revisable decisions remain independently testable.

Understanding harmful behavior can help identify better interventions. It does not establish that all crime is illness or that one medical intervention can ultimately eliminate it. Explaining causes, assigning responsibility, protecting victims, and choosing interventions are connected tasks requiring distinct arguments. An uncommon preference should not be judged pathological merely because it is uncommon.

Understanding the causes of human differences may also encourage tolerance, without guaranteeing that one intellectual revelation ends fear and conflict. Virtual experiences' effects on emotional regulation need comparisons of particular methods and actual outcomes. Leaving these questions open to research makes institutional aims less dependent on overly strong assumptions about human nature.

### How far does the evidence on social interaction reach?

The conflicts between local and shared interests, reciprocal feedback, and the effects of information and enforcement on cooperation connect with established research. Repeated interaction, reputation, and network structure can change the conditions under which cooperation emerges and persists. Each mechanism has assumptions. There is no general theorem that universal Tit for Tat alone produces the best result. [Nowak on mechanisms for the evolution of cooperation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3279745/)

My proposals for a cooperative start, verification, repair, and common knowledge are institutional designs for improving cooperation. Their reasoning needs every connection exposed: Does more information improve understanding? Does that understanding enter decisions? Do better decisions give everyone reason to keep cooperating? Does the effect survive greater scale? A failure at any stage can alter the outcome.

If two people need the only available machine at the same time, complete information and perfect rationality do not remove the conflict. Rotation, queues, compensation, or buying another machine are possible responses. Choosing among them still requires judgments about needs, costs, and values. Knowledge helps handle disagreement; it does not make every legitimate need compatible.

There is also empirical support for communication and shared experience improving relationships. A meta-analysis of intergroup contact found contact generally associated with reduced prejudice and examined conditions affecting the result. This supports cooperative practice, not the conclusion that every disclosure reduces hostility. [Pettigrew and Tropp's meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16737372/)

Public knowledge must especially not mean eliminating all private boundaries. Sharing that invites humiliation, surveillance, or punishment can reduce honesty. More records may then mean less meaningful feedback. Open rules, protected private life, dissent, and exit must work together.

Equality, provision according to need, and freedom from domination are value commitments for which public reasons should be offered. They cannot be deduced directly from carbon bonding, neural connections, or an energy law. Science can help establish how goals may be pursued and at what cost. Participants still need to discuss the goals themselves.

Eliminating exploitation, corruption, and deliberate harm should therefore remain an institutional aim under continuing examination, not an attribute conferred by a new name. Define appropriation concretely, how it is detected, who may respond, how appeals work, and whether new hidden privileges arise.

### Fair conditions, complementary abilities, and preventing domination

My aim is fairer access to learning opportunities, equipment, and basic living conditions, together with enough freedom and time for people to understand their strengths and develop the abilities they value. The point is not to make abilities approach a normal distribution. It is to build mechanisms through which people with different abilities cooperate effectively—enabling specialists and generalists to communicate and undertake deep work across fields.

This institutional argument does not require normally distributed abilities or needs. Observed performance reflects education, opportunities, accumulated resources, task selection, and evaluation methods as well as individual characteristics. It cannot directly reveal the distribution of innate ability or prove that fairer conditions must produce another particular distribution. Equal clock time is not equal discretionary time, learning speed, or a suitable developmental path. Fair conditions are worth pursuing without waiting for a statistical curve to be established.

Needs likewise cannot be reduced to one total or an average. Different people at different stages may need different care, tools, environments, and forms of learning. Provision according to need must understand those differences; shared productive resources must be organized around tasks and their effects. Distinguishing these uses does not prove identical limits on all needs or immunity from resource abuse.

“No dominating node” should therefore be a design objective tested continuously, not a theorem inferred from an ability distribution. Even where someone has exceptional expertise, the system should use that knowledge while allowing others to question, review, learn, and develop alternatives. Influence on a specialist judgment must not automatically become permanent authority over basic living conditions, personal data, or unrelated fields.

Tests should allow long-tailed abilities, substantial accumulated experience, unequal information, and coalitions among nodes. Can shared resources, weighting rules, and execution permissions still be inspected and revised? A mechanism that avoids concentrated power only when everyone has similar abilities and no historical advantage has not solved the relevant problem.

Cooperation also needs concrete assessment. Do fairer conditions actually allow formerly excluded people to enter? Do deep specialization and cross-field connection support each other? Does communication reduce misunderstanding and rework? Can work continue after a coordinator leaves? Comparisons with feasible alternatives can gradually establish where the new mechanism succeeds and where it needs revision.

### How can dynamic weights avoid becoming entrenched power?

Adjusting judgments' weights from past performance has an explicit algorithmic research basis. Work such as Weighted Majority studies the combination of judgment sources under specified prediction and loss models and derives performance bounds under those conditions. Dynamic weighting can therefore be studied rigorously. The result does not establish automatic fairness when transplanted into every social activity. [Littlestone and Warmuth's paper](https://homepages.math.uic.edu/~lreyzin/papers/littlestone94.pdf)

Social decisions add difficulties. A prediction of equipment failure can be checked after a defined period; the fairness of a public budget includes differing values. An enacted policy yields outcomes, while its rejected alternative has no same-world control. Participants can adapt to scoring by choosing easy questions, concealing failures, copying judgments, or gaining control over interpretation.

Node counts are not evidence counts. A thousand AIs using the same model and sources may repeat one error. High-weight opinions may be implemented more often, generating further opportunities to observe and interpret results and accumulate advantages. Without examining that feedback, dynamic weights can become fixed power.

Predictions about facts and consequences, choices of aims and trade-offs, and authorization and execution should therefore be distinguished. Specialist influence can change with performance in a limited domain. Better prediction does not automatically authorize decisions about another person's basic life. Affected people's expression, dissent, and appeal cannot be reduced to past prediction scores.

Begin with bounded tasks whose outcomes can be independently checked: equipment-failure predictions, whether a test will pass, or estimates of service resource needs. Compare dynamic weighting with equal weights, fixed experts, and a single AI under the same information and budget, using tasks not used for tuning. Check accuracy, confidence calibration, whether a consistently correct minority is heard, and whether copying or hiding failures manipulates weights.

If dynamic weighting gains an advantage there, investigate how it can assist shared decisions. If it cannot beat a simpler method, preserve that result and revise the model or narrow its scope. Its central role in the theoretical picture does not exempt it from comparison with simpler mechanisms.
### What can be built now, and what cannot yet be promised?

Personal data repositories, encrypted backups, provenance and version tracking, bounded AI retrieval, and dynamic weighting on explicit tasks all have usable technical foundations. Content-addressed software also has precedents. Unison identifies definitions through hashes of program structure and stores names separately. This supports further exploration of AI working directly with structured objects, without guaranteeing the performance of an entire new ecosystem. [Unison documentation](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea/)

| Layer | Work reasonably possible now | Evidence still needed |
|---|---|---|
| Personal knowledge and data sovereignty | Import, encryption, queries, provenance, recovery, and migration | Coverage, usability, long-term costs, and genuine ability to leave |
| Merkle-DAG records | Immutable content, versions, references, and processing relationships | Authentic collection, valid permissions, and treatment of gaps and errors |
| Coordinated audio and video collection | Prototypes within small, clearly authorized settings | Handovers, visitors, outages, revocation, hardware state, and bypass risks |
| AI assistance and dynamic weighting | Compare judgments and advice on bounded tasks | Errors, correlated sources, manipulation, feedback bias, and transfer across tasks |
| A new software ecosystem | Content-addressed modules, verification, and reuse | Full costs of performance, energy, compatibility, debugging, and migration |
| Common resources and open organizations | Voluntary small-group trials of accounting, cooperation, discussion, and exit | Sustainable provision, distribution, responsibility, disputes, and scaling |
| Civilization-scale self-governance | Research models, local tests, and cooperation across levels | No present basis to promise global fairness, long-term stability, or the elimination of all malice |

The running personal knowledge archive supplies practical evidence that sources, content, and versions can be preserved and queried. It does not establish that the audio/video system is deployed or that dynamic social governance works. Semantic relationships can contain cycles; immutable version DAGs can represent a knowledge network with cyclic relationships. Ideas need not be forced into a tree.

Growing AI capability expands the work that can be attempted, but reliability must be checked task by task. METR's evaluations distinguish success rates and warn that clear, relatively self-contained technical tasks do not translate directly into autonomy across real-world work. A capability curve cannot set an inevitable resolution date for every governance problem. [METR's methods and limitations](https://metr.org/time-horizons/)

A robust path should make its first stage useful with current AI. Stronger AI, robotics, or brain–computer interfaces can later enter tested processes without replacing early protections. An architecture safe only after everyone becomes perfectly rational, every AI infallible, and every device trustworthy has not solved the problem of beginning in the real world.

### Working technology does not by itself establish viable economics or organization

Copyable software and shareable model files do not eliminate the costs of inference, storage, energy, hardware, maintenance, or care. Public intelligence can make access easier by sharing those costs. Free use and zero social resource cost are different claims.

Provision according to need must explain its sustained sources of supply, who performs necessary but unpopular work, how needs are expressed and reviewed, and how scarcity is handled. Physiological limits on food consumption do not establish a uniquely calculable healthy optimum for every psychological need, lifestyle, or scarce opportunity. People must be able to challenge the system's interpretation of their needs.

Contribution records are likewise only part of the basis for distribution. Originating an idea, proving it, conducting an experiment, correcting mistakes, and maintaining infrastructure may all be indispensable. Exact records do not supply a unique ratio of their values. Evaluation methods must be publicly discussed, account for independent discovery and influence, and prevent contribution scores from becoming permanent status ranks.

Shared governance and polycentric cooperation have a genuine research basis. Ostrom studied resource and public-service governance under varied conditions, emphasizing relationships between context and organization. This supports exploration without proving that one arrangement will prevail everywhere. [Ostrom's research on polycentric governance](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/items/484f4147-bdce-4d55-9b8d-fe61f3b2d37b)

A small storage community must first sustain a reliable service, account for real costs, handle failures and disputes, and let people take usable data to another provider. Downloadable code does not by itself make the necessary equipment, materials, or cooperative relationships portable. Expansion must continue to examine actual control, concentration of resources, and the circumstances of weaker participants.

Commitments to fairness and openness should withstand personnel changes, financial pressure, malicious members, and departures. If operation depends on one person's long-term uncounted labor and funding, that can be acknowledged as a personally supported experiment. It is not demonstrated economic sustainability.

### Letting reality revise a scientific anticipation

A self-realizing vision can motivate action, and action can create the conditions for institutions that did not exist before. But if every success proves the theory while every failure is blamed on insufficient understanding, there is no scientific test. The vision needs conditional predictions.

| Research question | A small, discriminating test | Findings that require revision |
|---|---|---|
| Does the Dualoid add mathematical content? | Define it precisely, supply a model and mappings, and prove a clear statement in a small example | Composition rules cannot be satisfied, or all results merely rename existing concepts |
| Do fixed points and minimization explain better construction? | Fix states, transitions, task, and costs; compare convergence, function, and expense | A fixed point exists without convergence, or savings rely on omitted costs |
| Does the cross-level framework improve physical explanation? | Make calculable predictions for a known system and compare established models | Failure to recover tested results, or no discriminating prediction |
| Do semantic records improve knowledge work? | Compare ordinary files and existing retrieval, including organization and review costs | More wrong sources or omissions, or persistently higher total cost |
| Do reliable records and AI improve cooperation? | Separately compare recording, AI assistance, and rule changes | More recording suppresses honesty, or gains arise from uncontrolled factors |
| Do dynamic weights improve judgment? | Compare simple baselines using independent scoring, held-out tasks, and manipulation tests | Unstable gains or feedback that entrenches a few nodes |
| Is data sovereignty real? | Test unauthorized access, revocation, recovery, deletion, migration, and actual exit | Administrators bypass permissions or exported data cannot be used independently |
| Can the organization grow sustainably? | Compare costs, reliability, and power distribution at successive scales | Growth depends on hidden subsidy or increases concentration and exclusion |

Before starting, specify aims, budgets, comparison conditions, acceptable outcomes, and stopping rules; preserve the results afterward. Mathematical claims ultimately require proof, not finite tests in place of general arguments. Empirical claims require comparison with reality; success in simulation does not establish that real people will behave as modeled. Repetition across populations, tasks, and scales is further work needed to widen a conclusion's scope.

Counterexamples need not destroy the entire direction. A failed weighting algorithm can be replaced with a simpler mechanism. A rejected biological explanation does not prevent building data-sovereignty services. An unsupported physical unification does not invalidate proven provenance methods. Each layer should be justified by what it actually accomplishes, not by borrowing unproved conclusions from another.

I hope the framework's strength will come from each stage improving reality independently of later stages. First give one person controllable data and tools; then support reliable voluntary cooperation among a few people. Verify judgment mechanisms on limited questions before exploring wider organizational capability. As scope grows, preserve disagreement, exit, and correction instead of turning an initial proposal into a new unquestionable authority.

The present assessment is therefore this: the framework contains research questions worth formalizing and practical entry points for engineering and institutional experiments. Its unifying mathematical and scientific claims are not completed theories, and its social and psychological arguments are not all fully established. Applying records, verification, feedback, and revision to the theory itself gives it a chance to develop from a vision into something credible, useful, and open to shared improvement.
