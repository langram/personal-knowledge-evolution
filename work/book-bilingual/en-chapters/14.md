## Merkle-DAG Languages and Software in the AI Era

Reorganizing data and capabilities scattered across devices, applications, and companies raises a deeper question: how should the resulting network represent knowledge, construct programs, and invoke capabilities internally? If disconnected files, names, and platforms remain beneath a conversational AI, every boundary still demands translation, discovery, and confirmation. A common front door can conceal unchanged fragmentation inside.

Personal data sovereignty therefore reaches into the foundations of languages and software ecosystems. I want to investigate a more comprehensive Merkle-DAG approach: object identity, composition, dependencies, and development history would become structures the system handles directly. Files, folders, applications, and interfaces could progressively become particular views and combinations of that structure. To understand why this might suit AI, we should first ask why computing acquired its present organization.

### Why Are Human Conventions Everywhere Inside Computers?

Different instruction sets can encode comparable operations differently. Programming languages choose different words, symbols, and syntax for related functions. Blocks may use indentation, braces, or `begin` and `end`. Data may be represented in JSON or XML. Names may use underscores, hyphens, or capitals: where permitted, `two_word`, `two-word`, and `twoWord` can reflect a human preference without changing the underlying data.

Command-line options vary too: a slash, a dash, or an unadorned letter. Graphical interfaces offer a dropdown here, radio buttons there; one number field expects typing, another adds increment buttons. The same function appears in different menus, pages, and keyboard shortcuts. Windows and macOS preserve different conventions for copying, pasting, and screenshots. CAD, animation, and image-editing applications have their own habits.

This diversity has understandable origins in human language, culture, history, and preference—a kind of Babel effect. For much of computing's history, people wrote, read, discussed, and operated software directly. Names made programs memorable; interfaces made actions visible; folders helped limited human attention organize material. Those priorities spread throughout the ecosystem.

The difficulty arises when arrangements intended for people become mandatory intermediaries between machines. An AI may already know the parameter to change, yet still need to find a window, recognize a menu, infer whether a button is enabled, and enter a definite number. A second AI may reconstruct the same state from a screenshot and narrative. Directly communicating the object, parameter, operation, and permission could remove some of this repeated interpretation.

Calling this an ecosystem “based on human language” requires care. Programming languages are generally formal languages with strict rules, not ordinary conversation. The target of the proposal is the internal organization inherited from human vocabulary, text layout, paths, and interface conventions. Human-readable expression can remain available without being the only material through which machines work.

### Must an AI Builder Imitate Human Operations?

As AI takes a larger role in constructing code, tests, and tools, people can spend less effort on internal mechanical steps and more on purposes, requirements, and outcomes.

Why, then, must AI always exchange programs through naming and textual conventions designed for people? Why must code be organized primarily as human-managed text files and directories? Content hashes and Merkle-DAGs offer properties worth investigating as a shared foundation: checking content against identity, detecting changes under stated cryptographic assumptions, locating and indexing distributed material, and assigning identities without negotiating a separate name for every object.

An automated production line does not need arms shaped like human arms merely to imitate workers. A vehicle operating autonomously in an environment where no human takeover is required can reconsider whether it needs a conventional cockpit. Machine-to-machine software interaction deserves the same examination.

Natural language and graphical interfaces can remain surfaces for human input, observation, and explanation. Where dependable machine interfaces exist, agents need not reproduce every human click. Browser and computer control remain useful for compatibility with older applications, interface testing, and demonstrations. Other interactions can use typed, machine-oriented interfaces.

Think of different entrances to the same building. People enter through explanations, pictures, and dialogue; AI enters through objects, operations, and results. Both encounter the same controlled state. The machine need not imitate human gestures, and the person need not inspect long hashes to supervise it.

This is not a proposal simply to replace every word with a CID and feed the result into an unchanged language model. Hashes do not directly express meaning, and long strings can add cost. A model needs relevant structure, types, conditions, evidence, and concise references; the surrounding system can preserve and verify complete content identities. Research into models that learn such structures more directly connects this proposal with Structoken and FPM.

### Why Reorganize the Same Objects at Every Boundary?

Code and data often have compositional internal structures, but their representation changes repeatedly across a computing system. Source files express composition through syntax and indentation. Executables and data files use particular encodings. File systems organize directories, volumes, and mount points. Networks add names, addresses, and URL paths.

Across these levels, recurring questions remain: what comprises this object, how do we find a referenced part, and how do we establish that we obtained the intended version?

Whether components should be separately addressable, bundled, or compressed depends on use. During game development, small independent assets simplify editing. Distribution and runtime loading may favor large serialized packages and contiguous reads. Those are different physical arrangements of related semantic objects.

Developers make packaging choices using local knowledge, current tools, and current needs. The choices may not suit later uses, yet can harden into persistent formats. Meanwhile, each layer introduces distinct conventions: language syntax, data encodings, file paths, network discovery, DNS, service protocols, and application-specific rules. Even path spelling and escaping differences can cause agents to repeat work.

A Merkle-DAG could provide one content-identity and verification mechanism across files, directories, applications, machines, local networks, and Internet access. Packing and unpacking could then respond to measured usage rather than permanently define semantic boundaries.

The broader proposal is to apply that organization consistently instead of only in isolated subsystems. A semantic structure system would select discovery, reading, writing, packaging, and distribution arrangements for the actual storage and network environment, and test whether those choices improve performance.

A common mechanism does not make disks, memory, and networks physically identical. A CID specifies which content is wanted; location indexes and discovery say where it may be found; transport delivers the bytes. Separating these roles allows an object to move or acquire replicas without changing its content identity.

Nor is every semantic relationship a tree. Shared components have multiple users; programs can recurse; runtime state changes. Merkle-DAGs directly organize immutable objects and their content dependencies. Explicit binding structures can represent recursion, and linked versions can record changing state. Shared and cyclic relationships should be represented, not deleted to make a diagram look like a tree. IPLD's separation of a data model, content links, and encodings offers relevant prior work, although it is not the complete semantic system proposed here. [The IPLD data model](https://ipld.io/docs/data-model/)

### Deriving an Object's Identity from Its Construction

A human-readable name is convenient because people can remember it. But one name can refer to different content, and the same content can have several names. Projects therefore need further conventions: directories, installed libraries, configuration, and version requirements.

Traditional tools can pin dependencies precisely when those conditions are explicit. The question is whether precision can belong directly to composable objects rather than depend on each project's naming arrangements.

With canonical encoding and content hashing, every distinction actually included in an object—including explicit dependencies—participates in its identity. Under the hash algorithm's security assumptions, a small encoded change becomes detectable. A familiar name such as “addition,” by contrast, can conceal changes in precision, implementation, or behavior, with a library version elsewhere carrying the distinction.

Arbitrary-precision integer addition, fixed-width wrapping addition, and addition that reports overflow are different operations despite their shared name. Explicit contracts and constructions can give each a separately verifiable identity. A caller can then identify which operation it depends on.

Conversely, one behavior may have several implementations. CPU and GPU algorithms can have different content identities while satisfying a common contract. Contracts, implementations, and device-specific bindings should be represented separately and connected by verification evidence. Content identity is checked through encoding; substitutability is established under relevant behavioral conditions.

A hash only covers what was encoded. Hidden reads from an environment variable, remote service, or mutable state do not magically become part of a CID. Reliable reuse requires exposing conditions that affect the result. Content addressing must be designed alongside types, effects, environments, and dependencies.

Unison offers a relevant example. It identifies definitions through hashes of syntax trees, stores human-readable names separately, and refers to dependencies by hash. This shows that people can retain useful names while structure carries exact identity. The proposed cross-platform, cross-service, and social framework goes beyond that language mechanism. [Unison's content-addressed design](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/the-big-idea/)

### Propagating Improvements Through Dependencies

A Merkle-DAG can connect content identity to construction and expose which higher-level objects depend on a component. An improvement can then generate candidate revisions along those dependencies, together with the checks those revisions require.

Suppose application A₀ depends on B₀, which depends on C₀. An improved implementation C₁ does not silently alter C₀, B₀, or A₀. The system constructs B₁ with a reference to C₁, then A₁ with a reference to B₁. New structures grow upward while the old ones remain intact.

An upgrade becomes inspectable. Why is C₁ preferable? Does B₁ still satisfy its contract? Does A₁ work on the target device? Which evidence remains applicable, and what must be tested again? After verification, an authorized person or preauthorized rule can switch the active root from A₀ to A₁.

Some internal improvements preserve a contract and may propagate automatically. Others change precision, errors, or effects and require dependent work. A dependency graph tells us where to look; it does not prove every change compatible. Unison's update workflow likewise handles affected indirect dependencies and leaves unresolved changes to be addressed in the update branch. [Unison's code-update workflow](https://www.unison-lang.org/docs/usage-topics/workflow-how-tos/update-code/)

This preserves both development and return paths. Different devices can use different verified versions, and a disappointing update can be replaced by an older root. Payments, sent messages, and physical actions require their own treatment: restoring old code does not reverse the world. The version graph makes construction and adoption visible instead of hiding authority behind “automatic updates.”

### Fine-Grained Logic Does Not Require Fragmented Storage

Traditional packaging can bring large libraries or runtimes into a small application. It can be difficult to see which parts duplicate content and which similarly named parts are actually different versions. Finer-grained composition may help, but two questions must remain separate: can an object be identified independently, and must it occupy a separate small file?

The first concerns reuse; the second concerns storage and transfer. Binding them together creates an unnecessary conflict between convenient editing and efficient execution.

In a game, a texture or logic component can acquire its own version during development. At runtime, frequently co-used objects can be packed into contiguous blocks. The package has an identity, while its contents retain verifiable identities and an index. A changed texture may be transferred independently, then arranged for the receiving device's access pattern.

Compilers can also combine, inline, or vectorize objects. Execution need not consult an object store for every instruction. Persistent records should capture useful construction boundaries and evidence, not turn every transient value into a permanent CID.

Counting DLLs or similar-looking functions does not establish how much space can be saved. Reuse must distinguish identical bytes, similar functions, and safe replacements. Dependency analysis must accommodate dynamic loading, reflection, plugins, and external resources rather than remove what static inspection cannot see. A dependency set can be both small and sufficient only when its boundaries are adequately described.

### Choosing Capabilities Instead of Committing to a Brand

Explicit semantic contracts and mappings could describe hardware differences, operating-system differences, and alternative implementations of the same function. A person would express the operation or content they want through a suitable interface; the system would select an implementation that meets the conditions.

Today, capabilities are often bundled into brands. Messaging appears as QQ, WeChat, LINE, WhatsApp, or Telegram; cloud storage as Baidu Netdisk, iCloud, OneDrive, or Google Drive. Notes and mind maps offer another set of competing packages. Applications also expand into adjacent functions—an office suite may add cloud storage—creating overlapping combinations. Incompatibility can cause duplicated development; overlapping functionality can consume storage and runtime resources. Comparing twenty implementations of one function, or removing genuinely unnecessary duplication, becomes difficult.

My request could instead be “Keep these records somewhere I control and make them available on my phone,” without first committing my entire digital life to one provider. Or it could be “Communicate with these people and preserve my own discussion history,” with adapters handling the services currently required.

But calling several services “messaging” does not make identities, groups, encryption, platform rules, or histories interoperable. Contracts must expose those differences. A service without an adequate interface remains a real limitation. Shared semantics makes differences inspectable and replaceable parts composable; it does not conjure unavailable access.

Brands could remain useful curated configurations without defining indivisible boundaries around data and capabilities. A person might keep a preferred interface while replacing its storage component. Choosing a function would no longer have to mean permanently surrendering all past and future work to a package that cannot be left.

### A Shared Foundation for Different Languages, Platforms, and Devices

Alternative implementations should remain diverse options within a common framework rather than disconnected islands. DNA offers an analogy: shared rules need not suppress diversity and may help generate it.

The architecture should accommodate devices of very different scale, from watches and phones to tablets, laptops, desktops, vehicles, robots, and data centers. Each would use combinations appropriate to its resources and capabilities.

Operating systems and foundational libraries are themselves combinations of functions. Their existing bundles can restrict how higher-level software uses improvements. An advance in one platform may benefit its applications while equivalent applications elsewhere wait for a separate implementation. A shared structure could make verified capabilities easier to incorporate wherever their contracts fit.

Emulation, porting, migration, and interoperability already map relationships across hardware, languages, databases, and applications. A common semantic structure might provide a more general foundation for some of that work.

An initial research program could describe representative platform capabilities—from file and network operations to interface elements—in Merkle-DAG form. Mappings would identify which relationships survive translation into a common structure. Categorical ideas such as functors and natural transformations can inspire the inquiry, but each mapping must state what it preserves. Reverse implementation could also be studied: if an existing platform supplies every capability an application requires, with compatible behavior, state, and effects, can the application be realized there?

A common structure does not require a phone, server, and GPU to execute identical machine code. The same high-level task may select different implementations. Memory models, concurrency, precision, and device capabilities remain real differences to represent. Uniformity supplies a common way to compare them; it does not abolish them.

Existing compiler technology already separates levels of representation. MLIR supports progressive lowering from higher-level computations toward target-specific code. It illustrates how one framework can accommodate several levels and implementations. Content identity, development history, and cross-service cooperation require further work beyond that precedent. [MLIR design rationale](https://mlir.llvm.org/docs/Rationale/Rationale/)

“Global” also need not mean that one machine stores all knowledge or one institution approves every object. People and organizations can preserve, retrieve, and execute relevant subsets, exchanging structures through common protocols. Disagreements can form branches, compared through explicit mappings. This is the diversity-supporting role intended by the DNA analogy.

### Growing Beyond Compatibility Wrappers

The migration path can begin with compatibility: a common core and platform adapters wrap existing software and cloud services. The next questions are which capabilities can be reused, which need separate implementations, and how underlying services can gradually change while preserving familiar ways of working.

An AI-assisted software seed could describe authorized portions of an existing system—interfaces, formats, invocation methods, dependencies—in a Merkle-DAG database. It would develop a corresponding body of objects for the new framework, adding content identities and verifiable mappings. Where an applicable replacement has passed the required checks, an authorized call could use it, with the selection and result recorded for further review.

The smallest seed need only identify one class of objects, describe one real function, preserve one mapping, and perform one checkable replacement. It might begin with reading and querying personal documents. Existing applications could continue using the files while the new system accesses the same versions through object interfaces. For mutable state, the system must identify the authoritative current record and how changes synchronize, avoiding two diverging copies with no rule for reconciliation.

As mappings accumulate, more work can occur directly in the new structure and old interfaces can move toward its edges. Growth should be judged by independently working capabilities and reduced dependence on old formats and services, not merely the number of wrapped applications. Verification and the ability to exit must remain available.

This route starts with accessible APIs, plugins, and permitted platform capabilities. Missing access should remain a recorded gap, with work continuing where possible. Increasing privileges or bypassing restrictions is not a general migration method.

The seed's AI machinery must itself be replaceable. Models, agent systems, and their surrounding execution environments should have explicit interfaces rather than become an inseparable dependency on one vendor or framework. Tasks, records, tool contracts, and evidence should remain usable after a model changes. One model may propose implementations while another checks them. Personal history, authority, and capabilities should survive the substitution.

### Where Might Efficiency Improve—and How Would We Know?

Practical feasibility depends on implementation difficulty across current platforms, added memory and storage requirements, and runtime overhead. A claim of greater efficiency must explain which work disappears.

First, direct transmission of established objects and operations may reduce interpretation. Less time would be spent moving between command strings, screenshots, summaries, and paths, leaving AI to address unresolved questions.

Second, shared content and verified components may reduce repeated construction. Explicit dependencies can identify the scope affected by a change. A cache can be trusted more precisely when all relevant conditions are recorded. Unrecorded external state, however, still requires renewed evidence; a repeated CID is no excuse to ignore the environment.

Third, storage layout and execution can adapt to use. Composable objects can be read in batches. Implementations can be selected for devices, task sizes, and permissions. Bundled dependencies can be examined to determine which are necessary and which can be separated.

All of this has costs. Canonical encoding, hashing, indexing, dependency records, verification, and cache maintenance consume resources. Adapters add steps. Excessively small objects can increase metadata and random access. Changes can invalidate large caches. A hash-table lookup does not make reading content, hashing it, or transmitting it constant-cost. A simple task burdened by an elaborate structure may become slower.

We can compare three implementations of one actual task: the existing direct approach, a wrapper around it, and an implementation organized around objects and dependencies. Hold the work, output requirements, and permissions constant. Measure initial construction, repeated use, local modification, and failure recovery. Record latency, storage, memory, transfer, model calls, and verification. Fewer nodes do not establish an energy saving that has not been measured.

If a bounded task demonstrates reduced wasted work, extend the approach to adjacent capabilities. If a case becomes slower, preserve the result and investigate whether the boundary, implementation, or task choice explains it. The architecture's value should accumulate through reproducible improvements. Its promise lies in a systematic route to reducing duplication and fragmentation; the phrase “Merkle-DAG” is not a performance guarantee.

### An Information Foundation for a Different Civilization

A compositional semantic framework could connect functions across devices and platforms, and eventually provide common descriptions of human activities, creations, and products. A digital game already depends on layers such as engines, graphics techniques and APIs, and hardware. Explicit mappings among Unreal or Unity, DLSS or FSR, Vulkan, DirectX or Metal, and different processors would illustrate part of this effort where the relevant contracts can be expressed.

If we extend the metaphor to civilization's activities, the framework becomes something like a semantic engine for the real world: shared tools through which people can act, interact, and create. The point of the analogy is constructive capacity. Real people have purposes, dignity, and lives that cannot be reduced to one game score. A common technical foundation does not authorize a center to prescribe everyone's objective.

Verifiable links among knowledge, code, experiments, and their evidence could let a mathematician's idea, a user's observation, or an improved function enter wider cooperation. Others could ask what a result used, what changed, what has been checked, and what remains speculative. The earlier discussion of contribution and recognition would gain a practical technical basis.

If personal data and capabilities no longer depend entirely on an application's private names and packaging, leaving a service, changing devices, or choosing a different AI could preserve personal continuity. That is a technical condition of data sovereignty. Community rules, resource allocations, and decision procedures could likewise have explicit versions and grounds for change.

CIDs do not decide who may read or execute something, or how resources should be allocated. Private information, public knowledge, keys, permissions, and constraints on physical execution still require distinct arrangements. Preserving a distribution rule does not make it fair. Affected people must be able to understand, participate, question, and revise. The technical foundation supplies objects through which those commitments can become inspectable practices.

Personal archives, languages, tools, software ecosystems, distributed resources, common knowledge, and collective decisions therefore address related questions. Can the grounds of construction become visible? Can improvements inherit earlier work? Can differences be compared? Can people retain choice and the ability to leave?

On that foundation, AI could turn completed work into capabilities for the next task, while people connect their creations to wider cooperation. People continue to articulate needs, purposes, and direction; intelligent systems use internal structures suited to computation and make their actions and results understandable. That is the path I envisage from a Merkle-DAG software ecosystem toward a new civilizational architecture.
