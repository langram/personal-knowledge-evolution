## Common Knowledge, Reciprocity, and the Growth of Cooperation

Where information systems lack a foundation of shared knowledge, incomplete information and deliberate concealment make it harder to find outcomes that benefit the whole. Participants may become trapped in mutual defense and wasteful competition. Opaque and unequal access to information can also turn suspicion into distrust, emotional hostility, and eventually active obstruction, keeping the psychological and material costs of conflict high throughout the system.

We must distinguish a stable outcome from a better collective outcome. A Nash equilibrium means that, with everyone else's strategies held fixed, no participant can improve their payoff by changing strategy alone. Such an equilibrium may leave everyone worse off than cooperation would. The prisoner's dilemma is a familiar example. My aim is therefore to change the conditions of information, protection, and reward so that cooperation beneficial to our shared lives can endure—not merely to stabilize some existing state. [Equilibrium and efficiency](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~eva/agtchap17.pdf)

### Why concealment costs more than the concealed fact

The recent controversy over a broken brake-pedal bracket in a Maextro vehicle, Boeing's disclosures about the 737 MAX's MCAS and subsequent disputes over safety culture and whistleblowers, and Volkswagen's Dieselgate all raise a question: What does cooperation cost when designers, manufacturers, regulators, and users hold different information and lack reliable ways to check it together?

The evidence differs among these cases. In the Maextro dispute, test conditions, the state of the sample, and the cause of failure require comparable original records and independent examination. The existence of a dispute does not establish deliberate concealment by either party. It does pose an immediate question: How can the public move from conflicting claims to evidence that can be examined jointly? [Reporting on the dispute](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34216478)

In Boeing's case, materials released by the US Department of Justice in 2021 described concealment of important MCAS changes through relevant flight technical personnel, leaving key evaluation documents and associated manuals and training materials without that information. Safety-culture hearings and FAA testimony in 2024 also addressed whether employees could raise safety concerns without fear of retaliation. The information problem thus reaches inside the organization: Can people who know about a problem actually speak about it? [Department of Justice materials](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/boeing-charged-737-max-fraud-conspiracy-and-agrees-pay-over-25-billion), [Senate hearing](https://www.hsgac.senate.gov/subcommittees/investigations/hearings/faa-oversight-of-boeings-broken-safety-culture/), [FAA testimony](https://www.transportation.gov/faa-oversight-boeings-broken-safety-culture-0)

Volkswagen's case more directly exposed a separation between what a test displayed and what happened in use. The EPA's 2015 announcement described software that recognized official emissions tests and activated full emissions controls during them, while behaving differently in ordinary driving. Independent external analysis raised questions, and further investigation revealed the problem. [EPA announcement](https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/newsreleases/epa-california-notify-volkswagen-clean-air-act-violations-carmaker-allegedly-used.html)

Imagine if evidence relevant to safety and responsibility—from design and testing through production, sale, and user feedback—could be traced and checked within appropriate access boundaries, much as versioned open-source code can be examined in Git. Many suspicions now left to speculation could be clarified by inspection. Existing defects would also be more likely to be found and corrected promptly.

Publishing a drawing, however, still leaves the question of whether the physical product was built to it. Publishing a software version does not establish that a device is running that version. Original tests, failure records, reasons for changes, and delivered batches need to be cross-checked. A Merkle-DAG can help verify the content and version relationships among records; physical objects, measurements, and on-site processes still require independent examination. Trust grows because questions can be answered, not because people are told to stop asking them.

### From suspicion to hostility: how mutual defense becomes a shared loss

These are commercial examples, but related problems of information and trust occur throughout political, economic, and social life. Some conflicts threaten civilization itself. The two world wars, the US–Soviet Cold War, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and contemporary tensions between China and the United States all merit examination in terms of information, expectations, and reciprocal responses. Each also has complex historical conditions; opacity alone cannot explain its origins.

The progression from suspicion to distrust, then emotional hostility and active resistance, recurs in many conflicts. One important reason is the widespread human tendency toward reciprocity: I respond to you as you treat me. Under reciprocal responses, trust and cooperation can form a positive chain of reinforcement, while distrust, betrayal, and conflict can form a negative one.

When parties primarily act in this way, inadequate communication or concealment can generate suspicion. Suspicion obstructs communication and encourages further concealment, which deepens suspicion until it becomes distrust. Distrust brings further refusal to communicate and more concealment, then intense hostility. Fearing an attack, one party strengthens its offensive and defensive capabilities; the other responds in kind. Resistance becomes confrontation. In severe cases, localized, low-intensity violence escalates beyond control, drawing ordinary people who once wanted friendly cooperation into fear, hatred, impulsive retaliation, and even bloodshed.

Each side may believe it is merely responding to a threat, while its own defenses appear to the other as preparations for attack. Both act in the name of security, yet the system spends ever more on confrontation. Changing this process requires ways to check together whether a threat is real, what an action means, and how both sides can stop the escalation.

### Why a cooperative start matters—and why it needs error correction

Reciprocity can also move in the other direction. Every increase in truthful, useful, protected communication, and every reduction in concealment that obstructs cooperation, gives trust a chance to grow. Greater trust can encourage more sharing, less concealment, and more willingness to cooperate. The benefits of cooperation then strengthen trust and encourage further cooperation. Through this process, more relevant information suitable for sharing can become common knowledge, helping people identify better collective arrangements and sustain them.

Because reciprocal feedback can reinforce either cooperation or conflict, I draw the following conclusion: if social interaction is to improve our shared lives, its initial direction matters greatly. It is one of the key conditions influencing which way the chain reaction develops.

In game theory, Tit for Tat has a precise meaning: cooperate in the first round, then repeat the other player's previous choice. It performed well in particular studies of repeated games. That does not establish that everyone actually follows it or that it is optimal under all conditions. [Axelrod and Hamilton's study](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf)

Indeed, if both sides mechanically copy the other's previous action, one mistake can trigger alternating retaliation. A intends to cooperate but makes an error; B responds next round; A interprets B's response as a fresh betrayal. Both began willing to cooperate and still become trapped. A cooperative start therefore needs channels for checking facts, explaining behavior, and repairing misunderstandings, so an interaction that has gone astray can return to cooperation.

### Making cooperation, protection, and repair common knowledge

I would therefore like to develop the following rules and make them common knowledge among all participants:

1. Participants share a principle of reciprocity: respond to cooperation with cooperation; respond to evidenced harm with bounded protection; and preserve a path back to cooperation once misunderstandings are clarified and problems repaired. This retains reciprocal response while adding verification and correction to avoid mechanical retaliation.

2. Everyone begins with a cooperative strategy. Where real protections exist, they willingly share information that is necessary and useful for cooperation without harming their own or others' legitimate interests. Sharing can start within a limited scope and expand as experience and protective capabilities grow.

3. Information suitable for publication is recorded and shared as public knowledge through a Merkle-DAG. Private information is handled according to purpose and authorization. Dynamic decision weights and constraints on execution work together to prevent greater influence from becoming permission to use information against the person who shared it.

4. If complexity and uncertainty cause a decision's execution to harm an individual's interests, the system promptly limits further losses, provides necessary assistance, pays compensation under arrangements specified in advance, and revises its strategy and mechanisms in light of an investigation. An adverse outcome alone need not be taken as proof of malicious intent; the person harmed also retains the right to have its cause established. Evidence must distinguish limited capability, forces beyond control, negligence, and deliberate wrongdoing.

5. If a better long-term outcome requires temporary losses, the decision system sets out the proposed plan, expected results, uncertainties, allocation of costs, and stopping conditions in advance. Affected people can participate with adequate information, and the applicable authorization must be obtained. Execution is continuously monitored and adjusted; it stops when continuing no longer meets the agreed conditions. Success brings fulfillment of the agreed commitments. Failure still brings the agreed treatment of compensation and responsibility. Affected people cannot simply be made to bear every loss, nor can failure be declared unrelated to malice or negligence in advance.

For example, a community may plan to migrate records to reduce long-term storage costs. Before migration, it should explain who may temporarily lose service, how old copies will be retained, how new copies will be checked, when the process will stop, and who bears losses. If errors appear, the first priorities are restoring service and protecting data. Investigation then establishes whether the plan was inadequate, execution failed, or someone concealed a known problem. Compensation needs actual resources behind it. Where a loss cannot be repaired, a promise of compensation cannot substitute for protection beforehand.

These arrangements give affected people a practical reason to continue cooperating: they will not be abandoned when something goes wrong. Investigation and accountability need not delay immediate assistance. Protecting individuals and improving the shared system need not be competing choices.

### Making public records and shared decisions dependable

Two features of this framework are especially important.

First, the distributed public-knowledge system built on Merkle-DAGs and the social neural network of dynamic decision weights must serve a common aim: progressively removing the conditions under which individuals can use power to harm others arbitrarily.

Records allow the evidence to be checked; the decision network compares proposals; constraints on execution limit actual effects. All three must work together. Unaltered content is not necessarily true content. Agreement among many nodes does not strip affected people of basic protections. Changes to rules, the conduct of supervisors, and adjustments to weights must also remain reviewable.

Making something common knowledge also requires more than placing it in a public directory. Participants need access to the rules, a way to confirm which version applies to everyone, and an understanding of how to object and check compliance. A personal AI can help explain changes relevant to its user. A notification marked “sent” cannot replace actual understanding or necessary consent.

Second, people should develop a habit of giving reason priority and avoiding impulsive responses before facts are checked. A cooperative initial strategy needs support both in judgment and in feeling. Reason here includes taking fear and anger seriously as responses to people's circumstances, and providing ways to express, examine, and address them.

### Understanding how people develop can weaken the urge to make them permanent enemies

Differences and conflicts among individuals, between individuals and groups, and among groups—in appearance, physiology, personality, understanding, language, behavior, and needs—have histories. These higher-level differences relate to physiological structures, upbringing, social environments, and interactions at different levels. They continue changing through social evolution and repeated interaction. Scientific theory and evidence can help us construct testable causal explanations and identify conditions that can be changed. This gives us a chance to be less governed by fear of what we do not understand and by anger continually reinforced through conflict.

We can also recognize how judgments that fix people or groups as “angels” or “demons,” or turn every disagreement into an absolute struggle between good and evil, may be amplified by these positive and negative feedback processes. Such moralized or religious condemnation can obscure another person's actual circumstances and the possibility of changing the relationship.

Understanding causes does not abolish the need to judge and stop harmful conduct. Where an action causes loss, victims still need protection and responsibility still needs to be established. Understanding the conditions of harm should reduce its recurrence, not silence those already injured. We can oppose an action while leaving room for a person to change. We can respect the importance of morality and faith while rejecting labels that put inquiry and dialogue beyond question.

As this understanding becomes more widely shared, people may experience it as a profound renewal of perspective. Their way of seeing the world, events, and other people may change: a calmer disposition, greater hope for the future, and more tolerance toward those who think differently. A cooperative initial strategy then gains support from both reason and emotion.

This understanding can begin in a small act of cooperation: provide truthful, useful information; let another person see your needs; make protection work in practice; and explain, correct, and repair mistakes. Shared institutions help people accumulate experiences worthy of trust. Those experiences make the next act of voluntary cooperation easier. Knowledge, institutions, and human attitudes can then develop together through the same positive feedback loop.
