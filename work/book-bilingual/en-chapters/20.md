## Personal Data Sovereignty, Privacy, and Common Knowledge

When an AI service gives priority to resources that support paid inference while exporting users' records remains a lower priority, that allocation reveals whose interests carry weight. Unequal control between provider and customer affects what the customer can actually do.

The first task of the proposed framework is therefore to give each person local and cloud computing and storage under their own effective control. Personal records scattered across commercial services should be recoverable into that space. REA—the proposal to understand existing systems in reverse and reconnect what a person can rightfully use—serves this purpose.

Being able to view data inside a platform is not the same as controlling it. A person may browse records but be unable to move them into another tool. They may find individual messages but struggle to connect years of thinking about a question. Ending a subscription or changing devices may disrupt their whole way of working.

The fragmentation concerns more than files. Knowledge, preferences, relationships, and creations are divided among accounts. Each provider knows a part, while the person must search, request exports, and reconstruct their own history.

A personal space should restore the ability to choose tools, retain copies, and decide how material is used. It may span local devices and cloud services, but its control relationships should be organized around the person.

### Digital Space Is Part of Personal Capability

The same framework can preserve research and communication as well as data and code. A personal agent could connect tasks previously scattered among operating systems, websites, applications, and file managers.

A person might choose to retain conversations, work, ideas, observations, assets, capabilities, journals, notes, writing, or recordings. Future interfaces might add other kinds of record. The image is a personal flight recorder: a history kept according to the person's choices, rather than a requirement to capture everything indiscriminately.

An agent could gradually learn about its user's interests, commitments, needs, rights, obligations, and relationships. It could then help the person reason and participate in economic, political, and social activity, reducing disadvantages caused by limited information or specialist knowledge.

Repeated discussion of a question illustrates why relationships matter. A later statement may add a condition, answer an objection, or revise an explanation. Keeping only the latest conclusion hides why it was reached. Keeping all the files without provenance and links makes the useful parts hard to find.

The personal space should therefore retain exact statements, responses, acceptance, rejection, and uncertainty as distinct records. Changing one's mind should not require pretending the earlier view never existed. Memory should support learning rather than freeze a person into a past version.

### The Power Relationships Behind Privacy

How much of our concern about privacy arises from fear of how information can be used? Between people, companies, and states, withholding information often protects against a more powerful actor gaining an advantage and inflicting harm. Power is therefore an important part of the explanation.

Imagine equal standing and complete confidence that disclosed information will not be used against either party. Many forms of defensive concealment would lose their purpose. Greater equality and trust can create conditions for fuller exchange and cooperation.

Close personal relationships illustrate the interaction. Knowing more about each other can support cooperation; trusting cooperation can encourage further openness. Prejudice and deliberate concealment can instead widen separation. Families and partnerships provide familiar examples, without implying that intimacy eliminates all personal boundaries.

Under severe political or economic domination, information may flow mainly upward. Those below fear disclosure; those above seek more information while keeping their own affairs hidden. An employee may hesitate to describe a workplace difficulty for fear of evaluation or dismissal, while management collects detailed records without explaining their use. Asking for “full sharing” in such a relationship can simply deepen one-way transparency.

The important question is who can use information to do what. If shared rules, resources, and decisions are open to participation, mistakes can be studied, contributions recognized, and disclosure can improve the contributor's own circumstances. The incentive to share then changes. I want to reduce the conditions that make secrecy necessary for self-protection.

The proposed separation of resources matters here. Support for personal life and development should respond to actual physiological, psychological, care, and learning needs, while considering the conditions and common resources required to meet them. Collective productive resources would be governed through shared dynamic judgment. As those arrangements become dependable, more relevant observations and ideas could be shared without making basic security depend on winning every contest over information.

### A Complete Personal Record Is Not Universal Public Access

“I can retain my experience” and “everyone must see my entire experience” are different arrangements. A diary can remain in personal space. When one idea becomes relevant to shared research, the appropriate material can be brought into that work. A community does not acquire unconditional access to someone's life because the person has a fuller memory.

This is compatible with more useful sharing. Shared tasks need relevant information and adequate grounds for judgment. Internal processes without external effects need not become public simply because they can be recorded.

A service improvement report may include the problem, reproduction steps, and practical consequences without unrelated private messages. Trust may encourage voluntary disclosure of further material. Openness should be a choice enabled by greater control, not obedience produced by losing it.

A distributed identity and permission system is therefore essential. Its participants include people using real, pseudonymous, or anonymous identities; AI instances; devices; automated programs; and organizations composed from those actors. Their delegation and cooperation relationships require explicit representation. Authentication, authorization, and provenance need a coherent layer of their own if capabilities are not to return to the control of a small set of authorities.

### Traceable Memory for People Who Continue to Change

The Merkle-DAG framework interests me as a way to identify and verify records of structures and changes: data and code, revision histories, execution and data flows, and the actions of people and computers.

An evolving system's performance depends partly on its ability to trace changes and recover from errors. That affects how quickly useful feedback accumulates and how well failures can be understood and repaired.

Content addressing allows retrieved data to be checked against an expected identifier without prescribing which company must store it. Separating identity from location supports retaining copies and changing providers. [IPLD's introduction to content addressing](https://ipld.io/docs/intro/hello-world/)

Verification must be combined with actual access controls. Hashing a private file does not encrypt it. Encrypting a connection does not prevent its recipient from reading the content. Public content-addressed networks may also reveal clues about who requests or provides material. Personal records therefore need appropriate encryption, key control, and access arrangements. [IPFS on privacy and encryption](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/privacy-and-encryption/)

These arrangements should let memory travel with a person without trapping the person in it. An old opinion can remain historical while a current position points to a revision. Permissions granted to a tool can change. Already distributed public copies must be acknowledged honestly; a delete button cannot make everyone forget.

Someone who can preserve, understand, revise, and move their records has a practical basis for participating in a common network. Without that capacity, collective intelligence remains dependent on the organizations controlling access to memory. People surrender ever more of their lives and wait for someone else to decide how those lives are returned to them.

Data sovereignty is therefore more than a download feature. It concerns continuity of knowledge, tools, and participation. A modest storage service can become the starting point for reconnecting a person's digital life and developing new relationships of cooperation.
