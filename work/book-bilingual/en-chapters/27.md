## Self-Realizing Science Fiction and Cultural Creation

Whether people understand an institution depends on more than complete definitions. Readers also ask: What would a day in this society feel like? What happens when we disagree? How would I find help to create something? If a decision fails, how do we go on living?

Theory explains relationships; stories put those relationships back into human experience. I want practical construction and cultural creation to advance together, giving people ways both to understand a mechanism and to imagine or experience it.

### Connecting imagination and reality through self-realizing science fiction

Science fiction does not ordinarily undertake to bring its entire story into being. I want to explore a form that connects imagination and reality and contributes to realizing this proposed civilization. Its world could begin with the premise that Earth is neither the universe's only intelligent planetary civilization nor its most advanced. Many worlds reached the new form of civilization I envision billions of years ago. An individual from one of them arrived on Earth thousands of years ago, blended into its ecology, quietly observed its development, and lived through it as a kind of life-spanning role-playing game. At a critical threshold in Earth's development, this visitor emerges as a science-fiction writer and creates a self-realizing work—a form combining science and art that advanced civilizations use to connect imagination with reality. Through it, the visitor helps Earth cross its threshold peacefully and gradually.

The story could present this as a mechanism that emerged and repeatedly recurred in cosmic civilization. A gifted individual in the first civilization to cross the threshold—Alpha—conceived it when conflicts among nations, peoples, beliefs, and institutions threatened that civilization's transition. It helped Alpha pass through peacefully. The civilization visiting Earth, which we can call Beta, was itself helped in this way by Alpha. Earth would be a third-generation civilization and the 5,768th approaching the threshold.

Alpha, Beta, and Earth's number are fictional worldbuilding. Their narrative purpose is to place humanity's immediate transformation within a longer history. One civilization's difficulties become material through which another understands itself. More advanced beings can help without taking decisions away from those they help.

The richest conflict is therefore not simply whether an alien has stronger weapons. The visitor may know many possible futures, yet must still enable people on Earth to understand and participate. Declaring answers through superior force would recreate the very relationship the visitor hopes to change.

### Long life, diverse forms of life, and knowledge across time

In this fictional civilization, biological humans and AI together form intelligent nodes—the neurons of a distributed social intelligence. A Merkle-based public-knowledge system serves as civilizational DNA, supporting collective decisions among participants of equal standing. Biological individuals have also achieved indefinite life, overcoming aging, death, and the decline of physical and intellectual capacities. Knowledge, memory, and skills can therefore be shared across individuals and space, and also chosen and inherited across time.

Under this far-future premise, reproduction through descendants is no longer the only way genetic inheritance operates. Marriage and family organized around raising the next generation are no longer the dominant social units. Most people choose a new form of horizontal inheritance developed for indefinitely living beings. Through intimate biological exchange, they can safely replace and recombine genes, allowing each person to experience genetic diversity. This mechanism also supports extensive combinations across species, overcoming conventional reproductive isolation and enabling forms familiar from mythology and fantasy: life adapted to prolonged underwater existence, centaur-like bodies, or extraordinary regenerative capacities.

This explains how the visitor from Beta could remain undiscovered for thousands of years. The character does not transform without conditions. Through the fictional horizontal-inheritance mechanism, the visitor combines genetic material with terrestrial humans and alters their own biology to acquire human appearance. These are far-future premises of the work.

They allow deeper questions. If memory need not vanish with a short life, how do people change their interests and relationships? If appearance and capability become more plastic, how is diversity understood? How can a very long life preserve a desire to learn rather than endlessly repeat its past?

Science fiction gives such questions concrete situations. It may boldly stipulate future conditions, provided they remain internally consistent. Real services and institutional experiments, meanwhile, proceed from capabilities that actually exist. The two connect through questions and the desire to create.

### The creators and the builders can be the same people

The story itself follows the hidden visitor as they find like-minded people online and in everyday life. Through a self-realizing work, they communicate a possible future civilization and organize people to create novels, animation, film, and television while also building open hardware, software, and eventually a wider range of projects. These projects operate through equal, distributed cooperation, a Merkle-based public-knowledge system, and collective intelligence organized as a social neural network. Their gradual aim is to remove inequality, information silos, violence, and entrenched power within the pyramidal structures of conventional states and companies.

I want literature, comics, film, television, and interactive games to let people experience this possibility psychologically before committing their lives to it. The game can also be a laboratory for the new system. Participation can move gradually from imaginary situations to real problems. Someone may keep a job in the old system while taking part in their spare time, first exploring fictional scenarios, then helping solve actual problems and earning supplementary income. If the work brings better returns and a more enjoyable, less burdensome experience, they may gradually devote more time to it. More effective production, lower costs, better treatment, and a better working experience can make the new arrangements attractive through voluntary experience and practical benefits, reducing the conditions for violent transition.

Readers need not change their entire lives after one story. They can discuss a problem in it or propose another ending. They can help with subtitles, illustrations, or code. They can simply try a real service and report where it is awkward.

Each form of participation provides feedback. If the fictional institution is appealing but the real tool is hard to use, that gap is itself a problem to solve. A successful real collaboration can, in turn, become material for a story. Culture and production nourish one another, rather than one promising a future the other never makes available for experience.

### A virtual social laboratory for experiencing and testing institutions

Alongside diverse real experiments, an AI-driven virtual society and game could greatly accelerate exploration. This is somewhat like using biological laboratories to study traits and genes deliberately, speeding up observations and comparisons that would take much longer under natural conditions.

In a digital environment, the same initial state can be copied into branches with different rules. The state before a failure can be saved, one condition changed, and the consequences compared. Sudden scarcity, mistaken information, or very unequal abilities can test whether rules still sustain cooperation. Their value need not be illustrated only by an idealized promotional image.

Three activities need distinction. A story helps people understand a possible life. A game lets participants experience choices and feedback. Social simulation seeks testable predictions about defined questions. Stories may assume a distant future; research models must state which conditions come from reality and which are assumptions. Studies have built generative agents from interviews or surveys involving more than a thousand people and predicted some responses and behaviors within a defined scope. Such progress supports further research; it does not establish an ability to simulate society's entire long-term evolution. [Research on generative simulations of individuals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109v3)

If all virtual participants come from similar models, they may share the same blind spots. A million simulated people agreeing does not mean a million real people would consent. Experiments should be compared with consensual observation and small-scale practice, checked across different populations and initial conditions, and retain failed predictions. Methodological work on language-model social simulation likewise emphasizes human baselines, scope, and calibration. [Methodological discussion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26080v2)

The acceleration I seek is therefore a cycle: virtual worlds generate plans, expose problems, and identify directions worth testing; real practice then tests and corrects the models. Digital states can be rolled back, but human losses cannot always be undone. Moving into real activities requires renewed checks of authorization, cost, and stopping conditions. Only with this two-way connection can the game become a laboratory for institutional evolution rather than a repeated performance of a future we already want to believe in.

### Begin with a story a reader can enter

Self-realizing fiction can begin in its easiest form as one important part of public communication. A short story can introduce key elements of the world, followed by linked stories and eventually novels that expand it. AI-assisted short films can provide an initial multimedia form, later growing into series and longer productions.

The first story need not explain the whole history of civilization. It might follow an ordinary person trying to retrieve years of creative work scattered across services. They discover a tool built by a community, initially seeking only a solution to an immediate problem. A genuine disagreement then reveals something different: the rules are not imposed unilaterally by an invisible administrator, and their own experience can contribute to improvement.

The story can include failure. An import goes badly, an attribution error causes anger, and a shared decision needs revision. Readers see that the new system's appeal is not sudden human perfection, but the way problems become visible, discussable, and correctable. Hints of the visiting civilization can emerge in the background, connecting the small immediate event with the larger world.

Later stories can explore production, care, research, education, art, and shared decisions. Each addresses a concrete problem while leaving characters and relationships that connect to others. By the time a novel grows from them, its world is no longer an empty catalogue of premises. It is a world people have already lived in.

### From text to video to a world people can participate in

Communication can begin in several forms together: persuasive writing that combines theoretical argument with accessible public expression; multimedia treatments of material suited to images; and sharing through online communities and independent media.

Video requires a different order of presentation. An abstract argument can unfold through a task, a person's choice, or visible cooperation. Where readers of an essay would pause to think, an image can show a relationship. A video should do more than narrate every sentence of a long article.

Once stories, short films, and real services provide enough material, an interactive game has a firmer basis. Players can try different cooperative arrangements and see how resources, information, and feedback alter outcomes. If participation later connects to real tasks, people must know when they are entering actual work, how its results will be used, and what they have chosen.

Self-realization does not mean disguising fiction as fact. Imagination poses questions; the work attracts collaborators; practice answers those questions; and new experience returns to culture. A short story, a short film, and one useful small service can begin the process, expanding into a fuller world as participants and capabilities grow.
