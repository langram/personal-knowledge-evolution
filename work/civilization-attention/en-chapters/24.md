## Civilization's Technological Power and Its Capacity to Govern Itself

### Positive and negative chains of response are idealized directions

The positive and negative chains associated with reciprocal behavior should first be understood as simplified, idealized models. Phenomena near the two extremes help illustrate them: the love or friendship of lifelong partners who cooperate closely, or a criminal case in which a trivial dispute escalates into lethal violence. Much of ordinary life falls between these extremes. Cooperation and betrayal intermingle, misunderstandings and repairs recur, and outcomes are difficult to predict or control.

Someone may cooperate on one matter and remain defensive on another. A long friendship can include mistaken judgments; a conflict can stop because someone explains or makes amends. The useful question is therefore not how to divide people into permanent cooperators and permanent defectors. It is which conditions move interactions toward cooperation, which push them toward conflict, and whether an interaction can return after going astray. Strategies in repeated games produce different results under different conditions of error, payoff, and selection. That is why mechanisms need comparison. [Research on reciprocity and error correction](https://www.nature.com/articles/364056a0)

### From limited personal experience to cumulative civilizational knowledge

In early civilization, people lacked both sufficiently reliable theoretical guidance and effective means of gathering the information needed for judgment. Through repeated social interaction, human minds—with limited capacity, imperfect long-term memory, and reasoning and calculation slower and more error-prone than machines—collected information about relatively simple or small-scale matters. They generalized, developed experience and theories, then tested, corrected, refined, and extended those theories in practice.

Over this long history came language and writing, printing and drawing, photography and audio and video recording, and eventually computers and AI. Carbon-based biological minds created an entire family of silicon-based computing and intelligence technologies. These greatly expanded the capacity to gather, store, and process information, form abstractions, and create new knowledge, technologies, and tools. Humanity's ability to investigate complex systems and, within limits, intervene in them has grown. We now influence Earth's natural environment and biological systems within our own bodies.

In studying these systems, we can make precise measurements and records, use computers and AI to gather richer data, and combine computational power with AI-assisted reasoning and abstraction to understand mechanisms and improve prediction and intervention. Computers have deepened our systematic understanding of geology, oceans, and weather, improving forecasts for some phenomena. Computers and AI have also advanced our understanding of heredity, protein structures, and genetic systems, enabling prediction, modification, and intervention in particular tasks and conditions.

Prediction and control remain distinct. Forecasting the weather does not mean controlling it at will. Predicting a protein's structure does not mean understanding an entire living system. Weather models and AlphaFold demonstrate progress on defined tasks; the World Meteorological Organization also distinguishes limited weather modification from large-scale weather control lacking scientific support. Respecting these boundaries lets us use real scientific achievements as our reference. [Weather forecasting research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06079), [AlphaFold paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34265844/), [WMO statement](https://public.wmo.int/content/wmo-statement-weather-modification)

### In social research, the sources of information are willing—or unwilling—participants

For the complex system of human civilization, the information to be gathered is information about us. Unlike many physical measurements, its providers understand and assess the research, then decide what to disclose in light of their own circumstances. Even research on bodies and diseases must take human wishes seriously. Social research involves interests, trust, and power more directly still. As the technical ability to collect and process information improves, people's willingness to provide truthful information may become a decisive bottleneck.

In some animal experiments, humans collect information and test theories by overriding animals' wishes, restricting their freedom, and sometimes injuring or ending their lives. These practices have generated moral controversy.

If information about human society is forcibly collected through a traditional hierarchy, relying on unequal power and threats of violence, even an abundance of records may yield incomplete and misleading data, distorted by coercion, concealment, and deception. Such methods damage both trust and information quality when the aim is lasting, voluntary cooperation.

Suppose someone knows that reporting an equipment defect could cost them their job, or that expressing their actual needs could bring punishment. More questionnaires and more cameras may not improve understanding. People learn which answers are safe; an organization gradually hears only what it wants to hear. Records multiply without a corresponding increase in self-understanding. To change that, we must first change what happens after someone speaks truthfully.

In my view, this is one important reason why, in many fields, humanity's knowledge of nature and its ability to predict and alter natural processes have advanced further than its understanding, coordination, and restraint of its own social systems. Whether people trust the arrangements governing the use of their information is a key to understanding this mismatch.

Willing disclosure does not automatically solve every problem of knowledge. Data can omit important factors; correlation need not imply causation; participants may change their behavior in response to a published prediction. Protecting truthful expression is an important precondition. Testing theories, identifying causes, comparing plans, and revising conclusions must follow. The new system must improve all these conditions together.

### A civilization wielding enormous power must learn to restrain itself

If civilization's power over the external world increasingly exceeds its ability to understand and restrain its own behavior, that mismatch can lead to self-injury or even self-destruction. It resembles an immensely strong but fragile child swinging a massive chain hammer: ever more force, without knowing how to control its direction, may eventually strike the child itself.

In little more than a century, humanity has experienced two world wars, the US–Soviet Cold War, and nuclear crises. In great-power confrontation and competition over AI, if each side fears that the other's mastery of AI will threaten or destroy it, each may struggle desperately to get ahead. The fear of falling behind in the nuclear arms race can recur.

When both parties fear an outcome and respond in ways that intensify the other's fear, they may make that outcome more likely. The more they fear injury, the more they arm themselves; the more they see the other arming, the more afraid they become, and the more they arm again.

Even if this negative chain temporarily stabilizes, it has not necessarily produced a better collective result. Comparable powers may reach mutual deterrence, as in the Cold War nuclear competition between the United States and the Soviet Union. Alternatively, one side may gain a decisive technological lead and use it to inflict devastating harm, as the United States did in dropping atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Neither temporary stability nor military superiority establishes an outcome as better for humanity. Both can involve enormous costs of confrontation, mistaken judgment, loss of control, and the risk of self-destruction. This is one important source of fear about nuclear technology and AI.

### Humanity has more than the trolley problem's two tracks

In the science-fiction work *Watchmen*, Adrian Veidt, described as the world's smartest man, manufactures a greater common threat to pull the United States and the Soviet Union back from the brink of nuclear war. In the original graphic novel, an artificially created giant squid-like creature is used to stage an alien attack. The 2009 film instead has him cause a catastrophe and frame Doctor Manhattan. The plots differ, but both use mass death and deception to redirect rival powers toward a common enemy. [DC's account of the graphic novel](https://www.dc.com/blog/2019/10/27/after-watchmen-its-summer-and-were-running-out-of-ice), [the 2009 film's plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watchmen_%282009_film%29)

To me, this is a fragile peace sustained by an external threat. The tensions have not been resolved at their roots; a greater fear has pressed the opposing sides together. Adrian believes he is substituting a smaller loss for a larger one, choosing something like a solution to the trolley problem. But who is entitled to tell those sacrificed that their lives are a price worth paying? His conviction cannot turn an untested prediction of the future into an established fact.

I do not regard this as the wisest course, or as what we should hope for from the world's most intelligent person. The path of human social evolution is not a railway laid in advance, with only two tracks available: run over one person or run over a crowd.

An immediate crisis certainly calls for protecting life and limiting the damage already unfolding. But when designing institutions for the long term, we can also change the transport system, add brakes, establish warnings, and repair communication so that people are not pushed back to the same switch again and again. Treating a choice within a temporary predicament as civilization's inescapable destiny obscures our capacity to create other paths.

History contains many divisions rooted in earlier institutions, understandings, and technologies that once seemed intractable. As knowledge and technology developed, some were reinterpreted, some displaced, and others given distinct roles within a broader framework. AI offers an instructive example. Symbolic and connectionist approaches opened different research paths; convolutional and recurrent networks organized computation differently; Transformers subsequently changed how many tasks connect information and computation. Yet they did not settle every disagreement or remove all uses for symbolic reasoning, convolution, or recurrence. The lesson is that a new construction can change the available options. Competition need not always end with one side eliminating the other.

Such differences are not inherently bad. In my understanding of evolution, diverse attempts, comparisons, and mutual inspiration create conditions for improvement. But wasteful energy expenditure should be reduced, and avoidable injury to people should be prevented. A failed experiment that leaves an honest record and helps others rule out a mistaken path still creates knowledge. Repeated costs incurred to hide failure, defend status, or obstruct information may do little for common progress.

Unfortunately, change is not equally inexpensive in every domain. Competing technical approaches consume research time, computing capacity, energy, and equipment. Commercial secrecy and duplicated infrastructure can waste capital and resources. These activities also affect livelihoods and public safety, so their losses cannot be dismissed as merely financial. When political divisions become entangled with military force, the price can be human life, turning questions that could have been discussed into irreversible injuries.

Alongside particular interests and historical circumstances, the rules by which civilization organizes its own evolution need scrutiny. Lack of trust, antagonism, path dependence, and responses that treat one loss as proof of permanent hostility can draw people into structures resembling a prisoner's dilemma. Individual intelligence may then be devoted to more effective defense and suppression, without improving the shared situation.

I want better rules of interaction to create different conditions for exploration. An initial willingness to cooperate can coexist with explicit boundaries. Facts can be checked, objections heard, mistakes repaired, and alternative arrangements tested within bounded settings. What works can be developed further by others. Shared risks can receive joint monitoring and response; disputed approaches can retain room for exploration; actual harm requires prompt protection and remedy. Cooperation need not wait for every disagreement to disappear.

Let us build a greater capacity to find new paths when these predicaments arise: paths that reduce antagonism and allow people to develop together, more flexibly, sooner, and at lower cost. In institutional change especially, protecting life, sustaining basic living conditions, and preserving voluntary choice should be requirements of the transition. Institutions can grow by demonstrating that they are more useful, fairer, and better able to correct themselves. People can join because they experience an improvement, without a manufactured enemy or the sacrifice of innocents being required to hold them together.

### The reliability of technology and the reliability of the civilization using it

Technology's capacity to help and to harm depends on two connected factors: the reliability of the technology itself, and the reliability of the civilization that possesses and uses it. In many domains, our ability to understand, predict, and modify nature exceeds our ability to understand, coordinate, and restrain our social systems. That makes the second factor a serious weakness requiring urgent attention. As technologies grow more powerful, mistaken decisions and mutual confrontation can cause greater damage.


Look at the world around us. Under particular experimental conditions, we can position individual atoms into desired arrangements. We can put hundreds of millions of transistors on tiny chips, edit genes in certain human cells to treat some diseases, and build spacecraft. Yet society remains filled with ideological conflict, political upheaval, and brutal war: road rage, domestic violence, neighborhood disputes, labor conflicts; the events at the US Capitol and the social tensions visible in France's historical student movements; the war between Russia and Ukraine, conflict between the United States and Iran, and the long wars and conflicts involving Israel, Arab states, and Palestinians.

These events differ in character, responsibility, and historical context. Peaceful expressions of disagreement must not be equated with violence. But together they prompt a question: with such formidable technical powers, why can humanity not handle its own conflicts more reliably? If resources and attention flow disproportionately into making technology more capable and reliable while institutional reform and society's capacity for coordination and restraint are neglected, the mismatch can widen. We may pursue safety in ways that increasingly magnify harm, until recovery becomes difficult.

There are documented experiments in arranging individual atoms, and gene editing has entered clinical treatment for particular diseases. These achievements show that certain capabilities have arrived, not that we can reshape matter and life at will. They make the shared governance of powerful tools a concrete, present question. [Single-atom positioning experiment](https://research.ibm.com/publications/positioning-single-atoms-with-a-scanning-tunnelling-microscope), [FDA announcement on the first gene therapies for sickle cell disease](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapies-treat-patients-sickle-cell-disease)

### Keeping civilization's capacity for correction abreast of technological acceleration

As AI develops rapidly, systems have begun, within particular tasks, to contribute to the improvement of their own tools, programs, and research processes. As these capabilities develop, AI-assisted technological progress may in turn make AI research more efficient. The technological singularity that people have long discussed therefore deserves serious consideration as a possible scenario for which to prepare.

There are already bounded examples of this feedback. AlphaEvolve combines model-generated programs with automated evaluation to optimize algorithms, including parts of computing infrastructure and AI training. The Darwin Godel Machine modifies an agent's own code in a defined programming setting and compares new versions through evaluation. These systems demonstrate paths to improvement on verifiable tasks. They do not establish that a general intelligence capable of unlimited, comprehensive, autonomous improvement has arrived, or provide a reliable date for a technological singularity. [The AlphaEvolve research account](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/), [the Darwin Godel Machine research account](https://sakana.ai/dgm/)

My concern is that when major participants, including the United States and China, distrust one another and interpret the other's lead as a threat, neither wants to be the first to slow down. AI competition can then create pressure resembling a prisoner's dilemma. Each side regards its acceleration as defensive, while its actions become the other's reason to accelerate. The United States' 2025 AI Action Plan explicitly organized policy around winning the AI race. The Global AI Governance Action Plan published that year called for open cooperation and shared governance. Policy documents establish publicly stated aims; whether credible cooperation follows depends on actual constraints, verification, and performance. [The US AI Action Plan announcement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2025/07/white-house-unveils-americas-ai-action-plan/), [the Global AI Governance Action Plan](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/xw/zyxw/202507/t20250729_11679232.html)

Competition can make unilateral restraint harder. That does not establish that acceleration is absolutely irreversible, still less a date on which a singularity must arrive. Shared verification, credible commitments, and enforceable risk constraints can still change the choices available. We should be particularly wary of allowing "there is no alternative but to accelerate" to become the only answer everyone accepts.

If technological development accelerates sharply while the mismatch between our institutions and our technical powers remains unresolved, the gap may widen quickly. A decision whose consequences once remained local may, through more powerful automation, have effects that are larger, faster, and harder to reverse. Fear of AI, or fear of the few who control it, may provoke responses that deepen confrontation, allowing technological power and distrust to amplify risks together.

How far the risks grow depends on technical capabilities, how they are used, international cooperation, and arrangements for correction. We lack the grounds to compress civilization's complex future into a precise number. The concern is more concrete: if the scale and speed of harmful consequences exceed society's ability to recognize, stop, and repair errors, institutional weaknesses that were once bearable may become intolerable dangers.

I therefore believe institutional development cannot be postponed until after some confidently predicted singularity date. We may have less time for unhurried trial and error than we habitually assume. While choices and opportunities for correction remain, we should design, test, and build institutions capable of iterative improvement themselves: knowledge can be updated, rules compared, errors corrected, and participants can decide together how increasingly powerful capabilities are used.

By a "civilizational singularity," I mean a hoped-for change in capacity. Civilization would no longer rely primarily on long accumulations of conflict, the occasional authority of an exceptional individual, or destructive crises to achieve major institutional corrections. Continuing learning, public examination, and peaceful improvement would become part of ordinary operation. This is not a scientific date with an agreed measurement procedure, nor does it imply that everyone would thereafter think alike.

I want the pace of civilizational development to keep up with technological development, so that the two transformations can reinforce one another and arrive in a coordinated way. A community of humans and AI could then move beyond the limitations of earlier civilization. Its reliability would have to take concrete form in mechanisms that continually reduce loss of control, abuses of power, misjudgment, and mutual destruction. Eliminating these risks is an aspiration to approach through work, not a guarantee a proposed architecture can claim in advance.

Urgency should lead us to begin bounded practical work sooner and preserve the lessons of failure and repair more carefully. The more we fear the consequences of concentrated future power, the more important it is to develop verification and shared restraint now. Any proposal made in the name of protecting civilization must itself remain open to scrutiny by its participants.

### Protection and reward for truthful sharing can change how civilization understands itself

We already possess, or are gradually acquiring, the means to collect many important forms of information about society. Storage capacity, computing power, and rapidly developing AI continue to advance. Brain–computer interfaces, still under exploration, suggest further possibilities. Enabling people to provide relevant personal information voluntarily is therefore becoming an important bottleneck in this development.

The new system seeks to reform existing institutions, remove entrenched differences in power that allow domination, increase cooperation, and reduce conflict. It should constrain abuse and harm, establish practical safeguards against information being used against its providers, and strengthen people's willingness to share. Benefits from sharing can then encourage them to expand its useful scope and depth, provided that this positive feedback remains reliable and sustainable. This is the bottleneck I want to address at its root.

The return need not consist only of money or prestige. Having one's problem solved, finding collaborators for an idea, receiving help after an injury, and retaining basic protections when declining unnecessary disclosure are all reasons to keep participating. Nor is exposing ever more private detail the only measure of progress. Producing equally useful public knowledge with less disclosure is progress too.

These changes could improve the study of civilization as a complex system, support more accurate theories and more effective institutions, and allow its components to cooperate through intricate, dependable mechanisms—much as the parts of a complex organism sustain the whole and strengthen its ability to perceive and interact with its environment.

With computers and AI, people could obtain fuller, more reliable information, reason and decide through testable causal relationships, and continually improve their understanding and adjustment of social interactions. Together they could build a powerful, distributed neural network of dynamic decision weights: a brain for civilization through which they jointly participate in its evolution in the universe.

The biological analogy concerns feedback and functional cooperation. It does not turn people into cells without choices. A shared brain must not become another master standing above individuals. People must remain able to express different needs, challenge judgments, and choose the scope of their participation. Civilization's control over itself should first mean that participants are better able to constrain arbitrary harm together, not that a few are better able to manipulate everyone else.

### Cooperation, information quality, and institutions developing together

No particular mathematical model is necessarily the best eventual basis for the new system. What matters is not a particular model's name, but how the algorithms we construct through repeated research, iteration, and experiment can understand and improve real cooperation. As with a Markov game, computing the variables requires sufficiently rich and accurate information about the objects within the system. What counts as sufficient and accurate must be judged against the actual task. This brings us back to an important condition for making the system possible: active cooperation and meaningful participation by everyone taking part.

Information about human civilization differs from many measurements of natural phenomena because the people supplying it interpret the institution and choose how to respond to their circumstances. Willingness to cooperate cannot be treated as an arbitrary constant outside the algorithm. Institutions influence what people are willing to say and do; those choices affect information quality; information quality affects the judgments algorithms can make. The mechanism through which information is obtained must itself be studied as part of the institution.

The practical direction I envisage is to reduce antagonism and strengthen the willingness to cooperate through shared rules, then establish a workable process of iteration to research cooperation algorithms and institutional arrangements, gradually building new mechanisms and improving or replacing old ones. Participants need to see what follows honest expression, whether the benefits of cooperation materialize, whether their information can be used beyond permission, and whether losses receive explanation, remedy, and repair.

Active cooperation is both a condition needed to begin and an outcome the system should continually create and sustain. A small group with some existing trust and a willingness to try can cooperate within a limited scope. Participation and the depth of information sharing can expand as the mechanism proves itself. Reduced antagonism should grow from experiences of cooperation that people can verify repeatedly. The starting arrangements can accommodate doubt, reservations, and limited participation; humanity need not first arrive at complete agreement.

Every link in this cycle needs examination. Do verifiable protections and actual benefits encourage voluntary participation? Does participation produce more truthful and relevant information? Does that information influence decisions? Do the resulting improvements reach the participants? Are mistakes repaired? If a link repeatedly fails, the mechanism should change rather than simply demand greater faith.

"Sufficient" must also be understood in relation to a task. Determining whether a device can perform a backup does not necessarily require its owner's entire life history. Some processing can stay within a personal space, with only the necessary result shared. Achieving the same cooperative benefit with less exposure and effort is an improvement in system capability and may make participation easier for more people.

Each experiment should therefore examine both technical performance and the conditions of participation. Do people understand the rules? Can they decline unnecessary disclosure? Do their objections actually change decisions? Are benefits and promises of repair fulfilled? Cooperation and computing capacity can develop together through feedback; a perfect society need not exist before the algorithms can begin to operate.

### Choosing at a critical fork

We stand at a critical point, facing two directions that deserve clear recognition. One intensifies confrontation through negative feedback and increases the danger of civilization injuring or destroying itself. The other strengthens cooperation and self-correction through positive feedback, seeking a threshold in civilization's evolution and a new human form of social life. I hope this book helps people see these choices clearly and choose in favor of shared survival and creation.

These are not two predetermined endings. Real societies move repeatedly between cooperation and conflict. Institutions matter because they can improve our ability to recognize those changes, correct mistakes while correction remains possible, and protect cooperation as it begins. Technological progress and institutional development should reinforce one another, so that growing power is accompanied by a greater capacity to understand ourselves, protect one another, and choose a future together.
