### Keeping civilization's capacity for correction abreast of technological acceleration

As AI develops rapidly, systems have begun, within particular tasks, to contribute to the improvement of their own tools, programs, and research processes. As these capabilities develop, AI-assisted technological progress may in turn make AI research more efficient. The technological singularity that people have long discussed therefore deserves serious consideration as a possible scenario for which to prepare.

There are already bounded examples of this feedback. AlphaEvolve combines model-generated programs with automated evaluation to optimize algorithms, including parts of computing infrastructure and AI training. The Darwin Godel Machine modifies an agent's own code in a defined programming setting and compares new versions through evaluation. These systems demonstrate paths to improvement on verifiable tasks. They do not establish that a general intelligence capable of unlimited, comprehensive, autonomous improvement has arrived, or provide a reliable date for a technological singularity. [The AlphaEvolve research account](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/), [the Darwin Godel Machine research account](https://sakana.ai/dgm/)

My concern is that when major participants, including the United States and China, distrust one another and interpret the other's lead as a threat, neither wants to be the first to slow down. AI competition can then create pressure resembling a prisoner's dilemma. Each side regards its acceleration as defensive, while its actions become the other's reason to accelerate. The United States' 2025 AI Action Plan explicitly organized policy around winning the AI race. The Global AI Governance Action Plan published that year called for open cooperation and shared governance. Policy documents establish publicly stated aims; whether credible cooperation follows depends on actual constraints, verification, and performance. [The US AI Action Plan announcement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2025/07/white-house-unveils-americas-ai-action-plan/), [the Global AI Governance Action Plan](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/xw/zyxw/202507/t20250729_11679232.html)

Competition can make unilateral restraint harder. That does not establish that acceleration is absolutely irreversible, still less a date on which a singularity must arrive. Shared verification, credible commitments, and enforceable risk constraints can still change the choices available. We should be particularly wary of allowing "there is no alternative but to accelerate" to become the only answer everyone accepts.

If technological development accelerates sharply while the mismatch between our institutions and our technical powers remains unresolved, the gap may widen quickly. A decision whose consequences once remained local may, through more powerful automation, have effects that are larger, faster, and harder to reverse. Fear of AI, or fear of the few who control it, may provoke responses that deepen confrontation, allowing technological power and distrust to amplify risks together.

How far the risks grow depends on technical capabilities, how they are used, international cooperation, and arrangements for correction. We lack the grounds to compress civilization's complex future into a precise number. The concern is more concrete: if the scale and speed of harmful consequences exceed society's ability to recognize, stop, and repair errors, institutional weaknesses that were once bearable may become intolerable dangers.

I therefore believe institutional development cannot be postponed until after some confidently predicted singularity date. We may have less time for unhurried trial and error than we habitually assume. While choices and opportunities for correction remain, we should design, test, and build institutions capable of iterative improvement themselves: knowledge can be updated, rules compared, errors corrected, and participants can decide together how increasingly powerful capabilities are used.

By a "civilizational singularity," I mean a hoped-for change in capacity. Civilization would no longer rely primarily on long accumulations of conflict, the occasional authority of an exceptional individual, or destructive crises to achieve major institutional corrections. Continuing learning, public examination, and peaceful improvement would become part of ordinary operation. This is not a scientific date with an agreed measurement procedure, nor does it imply that everyone would thereafter think alike.

I want the pace of civilizational development to keep up with technological development, so that the two transformations can reinforce one another and arrive in a coordinated way. A community of humans and AI could then move beyond the limitations of earlier civilization. Its reliability would have to take concrete form in mechanisms that continually reduce loss of control, abuses of power, misjudgment, and mutual destruction. Eliminating these risks is an aspiration to approach through work, not a guarantee a proposed architecture can claim in advance.

Urgency should lead us to begin bounded practical work sooner and preserve the lessons of failure and repair more carefully. The more we fear the consequences of concentrated future power, the more important it is to develop verification and shared restraint now. Any proposal made in the name of protecting civilization must itself remain open to scrutiny by its participants.
