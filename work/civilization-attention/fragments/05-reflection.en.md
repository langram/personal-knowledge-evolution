### Testing attention, dynamic games, and technological acceleration

Attention at personal and civilizational scales is a functional analogy to be developed through construction. It does not establish a shared internal structure among brains, Transformers, and societies, nor convert relevance weights into authority to act. Evaluation should distinguish information selection, prediction, goal formation, authorization, and execution, so that performance at one stage does not certify the others. If a new architecture costs more than simple retrieval or fixed roles, it must show what those costs achieve.

Markov games are one candidate model. They can represent continuing change and, with variable structures, new objects. Whether unfamiliar phenomena can be expressed, whether the state is adequate, whether predictions are reliable, and whether plans can be executed remain separate questions. Equilibrium and learning results for finite models cannot automatically be extended to civilization as a whole. Allowing innovation within a model does not mean it can foresee future technologies.

Nash equilibrium, Pareto efficiency, fairness, willingness to participate, and individual rights are distinct questions of evaluation. A rule may satisfy some properties while undermining others. For example, with at least three alternatives, unrestricted strict preferences, and a deterministic choice rule whose range includes every alternative, non-dictatorship and immunity to strategic misreporting cannot all be achieved together. Such results require us to specify conditions and trade-offs; more future computing power does not remove a logical conflict between requirements. [Satterthwaite on strategy-proofness and social choice](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1975/strategy-proofness-and-arrows-conditions-existence-and-correspondence-theorems/)

The proposed positive feedback between cooperation and information also needs to be tested link by link. Experiments should include honest participants with limited abilities, privacy concerns, temporary departures, and deliberate manipulation, and compare whether rules still improve truthful feedback and cooperative outcomes. More uploads cannot be treated directly as more trust, and failures cannot all be blamed on insufficiently rational participants. The value of shared rules must be demonstrated by what they improve in an imperfect world.

Experiments in AI-assisted algorithm development and agent code modification provide concrete entry points for studying iterative improvement. They do not determine the date of a technological singularity or establish that the probability of civilizational destruction must approach a particular value. Coordinating technological and civilizational transformations is a risk scenario and an institutional goal proposed in this book. Treating urgency scientifically requires studying capability growth, the scope of effects, the speed at which errors spread, and the capacity for correction, while retaining different possible rates of development and paths of international cooperation.

The Watchmen example is an ethical and institutional thought experiment. The differences between comic and film can be checked, but a character's choices are not evidence of how real countries must behave. Its practical question is which mechanisms can expand peaceful options before a crisis, protect life during it, and repair trust afterward. Answering that requires further construction and verification. Criticizing one tragic choice does not establish that another arrangement can resolve every conflict.
