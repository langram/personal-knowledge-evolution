# Nova Principia: Mathematical Principles of Social Science

## Relativity, From Physical to Social Theory

**新原理：社会科学的数学原理**

**从物理相对论到社会相对论**

Author: Tangram Liu (langram)

## Preface: Why Nova Principia, Why Relativity, and Why Now

Let me be frank: the resemblance between this book's title and the title of Newton's classic, and between its subtitle and the name of Einstein's theory, are both deliberate.

In an age of information overload, attention is a scarce resource, and without an eye-catching title a book has almost no chance of being noticed. Clickbait is a necessity.

But the real justification for the title lies in the content.

Newton's *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* was the first monument in the history of science. On top of a few basic laws, using rigorous logical reasoning and mathematical calculation, it built a complete and self-consistent physical system, described the workings of the world at a higher level of abstraction, and became the cornerstone of the entire edifice of science. Later generations shortened its name to the *Principia*, as if it had described the principles of everything. In fact it could not: general relativity tells us that the Newtonian system is only an approximation of the universe at the macroscopic, low-speed level. But this does not diminish its value in the slightest. Its value lay not in how many phenomena it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided a unified architecture within which work could be accumulated. The next two hundred years of physics were all carried out inside that architecture.

Social science today is in exactly the state natural philosophy was in before Newton. The various schools of economics, sociology and political science contradict one another, are incompatible with one another, and lack the kind of unified axiomatic foundation that physics has. Most of them are less sciences than philosophical structures built on psychology, morality and experience, borrowing at most a few mathematical tools for statistical work, rather than being systems built on mathematical principles the way Newtonian mechanics is. You might ask: is there any mathematical principle in existing political science? The different social theories are more like observers riding on multiple trains, each moving at a different speed and in a different direction, each creating a theory from its own observations, with no framework for converting between them or referring one to another.

So what this book sets out to do can be said in one sentence: to place social science on the same foundation of mathematical principles as natural science, and to use that principle to build every level, from elementary particles to human civilization, organically into one harmonious, unified, self-consistent architecture. The system of social science should be a natural extension of the system of natural science, not some additional set of rules outside the laws of cosmic evolution, governed by supernatural factors such as a creator god, consciousness or the soul.

The subtitle likewise has two reasons.

The first reason is also in the content. What relativity did was abolish absolute time, absolute space and the absolute reference frame, and reduce them to relations between reference frames that can be converted exactly. Part I of this book argues that relativity is not a special discovery of physics but the necessary expression, at the level of "properties", of the most basic principles of mathematics and science, duality and symmetry: any property is defined only within a reference frame, between frames there are only transformations, and none of them is absolute. This relativity of the properties of all things at the physical level, extended into the social domain, is the relativity of cognitive reference frames: there is no absolute right and wrong, good and bad, justice and evil, only coordinates formed under different conditions, and rules for converting between them. What the observers on those trains lack is exactly a Lorentz transformation. That transformation is what this book offers social science.

The second reason is practical, and urgent. Humanity is facing its second civilizational crisis after nuclear weapons. The crisis on the surface is AI, the revolutionary tool that frightens and excites the whole of humanity at once, double-edged like the bomb, with its destructive edge being sharpened faster every month. But the deeper crisis is in humanity itself. Human history is an evolutionary record full of violent conflict, driven over and over by the tit-for-tat strategy, and that history has deposited in traditional religion and culture a mass of emotional genes of extreme binary opposition: angels and demons, justice and evil, us and them. They are the old release of civilization's DNA, and every conflict that replays itself between individuals, between groups and between nations is these genes acting as catalyst and accelerator: first define the other side as evil, and then every means becomes justice. In the nuclear age humanity was lucky enough not to press the button. In the age of AI, the speed of the decisions these genes drive and the scale of the harm they do will be amplified a hundredfold or a thousandfold. How likely is it that we get lucky twice? The framing of relativity is precisely the dissolution and remaking of this civilizational gene: it reduces "justice and evil" to "differences between reference frames that have conditions of formation", and reduces moral judgment to facts that can be checked and rules that can be changed. Once that remaking is done, the same tit-for-tat strategy turns from the catalyst of violent conflict into the catalyst and accelerator of positive cooperation under a new civilizational order. So the subtitle is not rhetoric. It is the entrance to the whole argument of this book, and its exit.

Why now? Because two things have become real for the first time in the last few years, and a third is becoming real, and it will not wait.

First, large AI language models have given the human space of consciousness a mathematical model for the first time. The whole of human knowledge has been integrated into a high-dimensional semantic space of billions of parameters that can be measured, recorded and processed, and on which higher-order social models can be designed, constructed and evolved. A Cartesian coordinate system and a Newtonian reference frame for social science have become technically possible for the first time.

Second, silicon-based computing devices can now possess human intelligence, and this fact provides the basis for a consensus: problems in the domain of human society can also be solved by mathematical and scientific models, just like problems in the natural domain. Every cognition and behavior of a person at the social level is an objective phenomenon that, like a natural phenomenon, can be observed, studied, quantified, recorded, analyzed and modeled.

Third, and this is why the book cannot wait: the evolution of technology is accelerating, the evolution of institutions has stalled, and the structural contradiction between the two is approaching a critical point. Since the Second World War, the rules of the game between nations and the political and economic systems within them have seen almost no fundamental change; over the same period information technology has passed through five waves: the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet and AI. We can already manipulate single atoms, edit the human genome and build ships bound for space, yet we still settle our conflicts with pyramid institutions designed centuries ago, and decide policies that touch billions of people by emotion and position. The gap between humanity's power to understand and remake nature and its power to understand and govern its own society has never been this wide. A civilization whose power over the outside world outruns its control of itself is a child of enormous strength and fragile mind, swinging a chain flail that weighs a ton: sooner or later it hits itself. Nuclear weapons were the first flail, and humanity was lucky; AI is the second, and it doubles in weight every few months. The AI race between China and the United States is one creature whose two hemispheres are not aligned, swinging a flail in each hand. When a gap in capability can open to a hundredfold or a thousandfold within months, the redesign of the rules has to happen before the gap locks in; after it locks, whether the outcome is winner-take-all or a standoff among several, no one will need a new set of rules any more. So this is not a book that can wait for the theory to mature and the conditions to be ready. The window is closing, and what this book sets out to do is exactly to turn this civilization from a system that corrects its errors by emotion and violence into one that corrects them by records, verification and dynamic weights. Time itself is a variable.

The book has five parts. Part I is the principles: starting from limits and paradoxes, it proposes the dualoid, a concept more basic than objects and morphisms; it proposes the relationship between fixed points, minimization, maximization and emergence; it explains the mathematical roots of consciousness; and finally it gives the form the principle of relativity takes in the domain of cognition. Part II is the construction of society: it treats people as atoms, cooperation as chemical bonds, and the tit-for-tat strategy as the iterative operator that builds social structure, and from these it derives common knowledge, the allocation of two kinds of resources, and a decision mechanism in the form of a social neural network with dynamic weights. Part III is the foundation in information science: a new language that uses hashes as names and addresses, a software ecosystem that moves from files to semantics, and every person's digital space and data sovereignty. Part IV is civilization: attention, the Large Civilization Model in which training and inference are one, the gap between technology and institutions, alignment, and the fork in the road at which humanity now stands. Part V is the road: starting from one person's cloud storage, to the growth of free zones, to self-fulfilling science fiction and culture, to continuous creation.

On method, I want to make three things clear first.

First, I have had no professional training in mathematics, and rigorous formal proofs are beyond my ability. The path this book takes is mainly a more generalized abstraction of existing scientific and mathematical knowledge, a process of reverse reduction toward a deeper essence, and it relies more on intuitive insight and philosophical reflection than on strict logical derivation. Kuhn said that paradigm shifts usually do not come from logical deduction within the old paradigm, but from an entirely new worldview and conceptual framework. Nearly every category theory entry on nLab opens with an "Idea" section, which describes the intuition from which the concept arose, and you rarely find rigorous reasoning there. But this book also contains a deductive part: the process of reconstructing each field starting from the new principles. In that process I have tried to make the premises correct enough and the reasoning simple and intuitive enough that every step can be followed by a reader with a university education. This book sacrifices mathematical rigor in pursuit of universality of thought.

Second, a word of warning in advance, with an example from chess. The evolution of a complex system often gets stuck in a locally optimal state of play, like a chess position in which both sides' pieces are tangled together, neither willing to give anything up, as if whoever wins this skirmish wins the whole game. A true master has a higher intuition, and the courage to sacrifice a piece and enter a temporarily worse position in order to reach the global optimum. Ptolemy's geocentric model was an extremely effective local optimum: it predicted planetary motion with considerable precision, and precisely because it was so effective, the trap of the local optimum was so deep. Today's mathematics, science and social theory have entered this state in almost every foundational discipline. The proposal in this book is counterintuitive, and as you read on you will be unable to resist denouncing it as the reckless audacity of an outsider, like a child casually sending the queen into the enemy's line of fire. Please have a little more patience. Once the first move of the sacrifice is played, the position often suddenly opens up.

Third, in the theory presented in this book, the part concerning mathematics, category theory and physics is overwhelmingly hypothesis, and I am very clear about that. The part concerning human physiology, psychology and social games has a relatively reliable theoretical basis and a complete chain of reasoning. The part concerning the Merkle-DAG information system, the dynamic-weight decision network and the rules of a new civilization is an engineering proposal premised on current and foreseeable computing and AI capabilities. I do not want this body of work to exist only in fantasy; I want it to be the kind of scientific prophecy that can bring about its own fulfillment. Which parts are hypotheses, which are inferences, and which could be started on today, the appendix at the end of the book will spell out item by item.

Last, on what this book is and what it is not.

Let me stress this: I have not discovered and defined the specific rules and structures of a new human civilizational order. What this book proposes is a foundation of mathematical principles for social science, extended naturally from the mathematical foundation of natural science, together with some meta-game strategies and a plan for getting started on building a new civilizational order. They are like the mathematical principles of the early days of large language models: those theories and designs were a compass, a telescope, a chart, but not the destination; the day they were written down did not mean that a powerful AI model at once appeared in perfect form. In the same way, these theoretical ideas of mine are only a torch held up to scout the road toward the singularity of civilization. All I hope is that it lets this road be traveled a little more smoothly and a little faster, so that the gap in speed between institutions and technology does not grow so wide that it brings disaster on human civilization.

The direction this torch lights up can be said in one sentence. The field of AI has a famous "bitter lesson": over seventy years, every approach that relied on experts hand-writing their own knowledge and experience into the system worked in the short run and lost in the long run to approaches that relied on general mathematical models, enormous data and immense computing power. The study and evolution of human civilization's institutions now faces the same bitter lesson. To this day humanity solves the problems of constructing and organizing society by relying on political, economic and academic authorities, on their personal experience and learning, working together in simple cooperation; this is like the traditional edifice of science, raised brick by brick from the personal learning of geniuses and authorities. I do not deny what that road has achieved: it is how humanity built today's very advanced system of science and its highly developed political and economic institutions. But to reach the singularity of technology and civilization, to make a leap to a higher level, the best road is the other one: as artificial intelligence does today, use mathematics and computers to model mathematically the complex problems of social construction and organization that all of humanity faces, gather data on an enormous scale, and, in a loop of learning, training and inference in practice, build together a large model at the level of civilization. I am almost certain that this is a far better choice. Chapter 15 and the postscript develop it.

So when trying to reform a civilizational order that has fallen far behind technological evolution, one must have the courage to break every stale habit of thought and step outside the existing framework. Do not let the concepts of nation, regime and corporation, those traditional pyramid structures, confine your imagination of the future. In the evolving system that is the universe, every entity, structure and evolving object has a beginning, and will eventually meet its moment of extinction or transformation.

Good. Now let us begin.
