## Chapter 3: Fixed Points, Minimization, Maximization and Emergence

### The static face and the dynamic face of the same object

Two words kept recurring in the previous two chapters: fixed point and emergence. Now I will put them into a single framework.

Fixed point and emergence, like the wave-particle duality of elementary particles, are the static and dynamic manifestations of the same object. The fixed point describes the object's static existence; emergence describes the object's dynamic changeability. And what connects these two forms, letting them sustain each other, convert into each other and iterate forward endlessly, is a pair of laws that sound like exact opposites: the principle of minimization, and the principle of maximization.

Take minimization first. When discussing the evolution of categories with an AI, I once wrote it as three sub-principles: among different objects with the same structural properties, the lower the internal total energy, the higher the probability of existence; when the same object has different ways of dissipating energy while maintaining its structure, the way that consumes the least energy has the higher probability of existence; when the same object has different paths for evolving its structure, the path that consumes the least energy has the higher probability of existence. Now maximization: for a collection of basic objects of the same size, an ordered structure consumes more energy than a disordered one, a complex structure consumes more energy than a simple one, and as structure evolves, energy always develops along the path of the greater rate of consumption.

These two look contradictory, but they are in fact dual. So I merged them into one:

**The fixed point, minimization, maximization and emergence theorem**: In the evolution of a category of emergent fixed points, the internal structural energy by which a fixed point maintains its own stable existence tends toward minimization, while the paths by which it establishes connections outward with other objects tend toward maximization. The tendency toward path maximization is the fastest path by which objects in that category produce emergence; so, from the viewpoint of the higher-order category, it appears as a tendency toward internal energy minimization in the new object that emerges into that category. Conversely, the minimization of the internal energy by which a fixed point maintains its existence is in fact the maximization of the connecting paths among the more basic objects in the lower-order category. The two are complementary, mutually dual processes.

Scientific research itself is a typical example of this paradigm: the minimization of basic theory, Newtonian mechanics relative to natural philosophy, gave people a far stronger ability to transform the external world and construct things that did not previously exist; and new scientific and technological tools and products in turn often promote progress in basic science, and so it evolves back and forth.

### Minimization is everywhere in natural science

From the microscopic to the macroscopic, from physics to chemistry to biology, the principle of minimization is visible everywhere.

At the level of elementary particles, the motion of all particles and fields obeys the principle of least action; the paths they choose make the action stationary, usually a minimum, though it can also be a saddle point. The Higgs field, in order to reach the lowest-energy stable vacuum state, chose a nonzero vacuum expectation value, and this spontaneous symmetry breaking gave the other elementary particles their mass. In quantum tunneling, a particle finds the path through the barrier that minimizes the product of the barrier's width and height.

At the chemical level, electrons outside the nucleus preferentially occupy the lowest-energy orbitals; at constant temperature and pressure, reactions always proceed spontaneously in the direction of decreasing Gibbs free energy until a minimum is reached; molecules always adjust their spatial structure to minimize total potential energy, which is why the water molecule takes its V shape with an angle of 104.5 degrees.

At the biological level, the folding of an amino acid chain into its native structure is a search for the lowest point of free energy, and misfolded proteins are toxic because they sit in high-energy metastable states; phospholipid molecules spontaneously form bilayer membranes in water in order to minimize the free energy of the system under the hydrophobic effect; metabolic pathways, genome size and neuronal connections are all optimized under evolutionary pressure to the most efficient state of minimal consumption of matter and energy.

At the level of gravitation, a celestial body made of free-flowing fluid spontaneously gathers toward its center under gravity, so that its total gravitational potential energy reaches a minimum, which is why large bodies collapse into spheres; the elliptical orbit of a planet is the path that makes the action stationary.

General relativity interprets gravity as geometry: an object acted on by no external force always moves along a geodesic, the path between two points in four-dimensional spacetime along which proper time is stationary; a photon bending in a gravitational field is taking the quickest shortcut through curved space; and the curvature of spacetime itself obeys the same principle, since making the Einstein-Hilbert action stationary yields the field equations.

### Every minimization is a maximization

Yet nearly all of these minimization principles can, mathematically, be seen equivalently as the maximization of some corresponding physical quantity.

In relativity, minimizing the action is equivalent to maximizing proper time: a freely falling object's own clock runs fastest. In thermodynamics, minimizing Gibbs free energy at constant temperature and pressure is essentially a way of maximizing the total entropy of the universe: phospholipids packing tightly into a membrane is a local decrease in entropy, done so that the surrounding water molecules can be completely liberated to thrash about wildly. In chemistry, minimizing the total potential energy of a system is equivalent to maximizing bond energy, that is, binding energy: the more energy released, the more firmly the atoms are bound. In optics, Fermat's principle says light takes the path of least time, and from the path-integral viewpoint this means that only along this path is the superposition of phases maximal, while all other paths cancel to zero by destructive interference.

This is the duality I am talking about: minimization and maximization are two faces of the variational principle. This part is not my discovery: the variational principle and each of these equivalent forms are textbook material, and strictly speaking these principles require the action to be stationary, a minimum being only the most common case. What I have done is set them side by side to bring out the common structure, and then take it out of physics and make it a meta-principle that runs through every level.

### From the theory of evolution to social science

Now let us focus on the theory of evolution and on social science.

Darwin's survival of the fittest, described with this theorem, says: living things all have an instinct to maintain their own existence and an instinct to establish connections and interact with the other objects in their surrounding environment, and their evolutionary tendency is, in the functions required to maintain their own existence, always to evolve toward forms that consume less energy; and in connecting with other external objects, always to evolve in the direction of being able to relate to more objects. Examples of the former: the divergence of herbivores and carnivores, and the increase in the efficiency of protein absorption. Examples of the latter: from plants to animals, from single-celled to multicellular organisms, from solitary animals to social, cooperating animals.

The games between individuals and groups in human society, and the various economic and political objects, are the same. The individual's instinct to maintain its own survival, the instinct to develop social relationships and economic interests, Nash equilibrium, Pareto optimality, the maximization of productivity, the cost-cutting, efficiency-raising and profit-maximizing of firms: these phenomena of minimization and maximization are everywhere. Even the demands and slogans at the level of politics and human rights can be interpreted by this principle: the right to life corresponds to the fixed point's instinct to maintain itself; freedom corresponds to the maximization of relations with other objects; democracy corresponds to the maximization of the emergent higher-order group organization; and equality is the precondition that truly allows individual interest and group interest to be maximized at the same time. Eliminating the inequality of power is the best way to eliminate the waste of resources in the whole system: it lets the whole system reach minimal energy consumption while maximizing the capacity of individuals within the system to connect freely with one another. Marx's sentence, "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all", is, I believe, the best mathematical-principle formulation of this, and also the mathematical-principle expression of Darwin's "natural selection, survival of the fittest".

### The shortest description and the least effortful construction

There is another angle from which to see this principle: Occam's razor.

Occam's razor involves three parts: an intelligent agent capable of reasoning and computation, a phenomenon to be explained, and a theory used to explain the phenomenon. Apply Occam's razor once more and simplify these three parts into the simplest objects: the intelligent agent is a Turing machine T, the theory is a Turing machine program P, and the phenomenon is an output string D. Then the most parsimonious theory is the shortest program P that, on Turing machine T, outputs the string D and nothing else. The length of this shortest program is the Kolmogorov complexity. A string of a thousand zeros and a random string of the same length obviously differ in complexity: the former can be generated by "loop a thousand times and print 0", the latter can only be printed character by character; defining complexity by the length of the generating program is far more objective than defining it by the length of the string itself.

Kolmogorov complexity defines static complexity; it depends only on content. But the world is always evolving, and the birth of any object goes through a construction process; an object's dynamic complexity is related to its construction process, and that corresponds to computational complexity theory: how many steps of computation are needed, how much memory, whether it can be parallelized. And in reality, for any Turing-complete computer, whether computing or storing, every instruction and every bit read or written consumes energy; seen from the level of quantum physics, these operations are in fact one kind of operation: consuming energy to change the topological structure among existing quanta and form a new topological structure. The evolution of everything in the universe can be abstracted as this kind of change in quantum topological structure.

In this way, Occam's razor, Newton's four rules of reasoning in philosophy and Kolmogorov complexity can be seen as the names of the same thing in different historical periods: an evaluation function, evaluating "the construction of the greatest external connectivity at the least internal cost". Its ultimate answer is essentially uncomputable, but the function itself can be iteratively optimized, approaching the optimum step by step along the direction of evolution. Later in the book, when discussing how AI learns to create theories, I will bring it out and use it as the reward function.

### Whatever exists is justified

This principle has a corollary that I must state clearly, otherwise much of what follows will be misread.

Of course one can take "a civilization's capacity to evolve" as an evaluation criterion, but that does not mean this is the value of the civilization's existence. Just as in a software project you can treat some benchmark as the evaluation criterion for a hot path, but that is not the same as the value of the hot path's existence. In fact, everything that exists is justified; everything is a fixed-point object that emerged from some converging minimization process. We can judge that the hunting ability of felines far exceeds that of rodents, but you cannot say the existence of felines has higher value; there are many dormant, ancient fragments in the human genome, and you should not say the active fragments are more valuable. Once something exists, it does not vanish; it only increases the diversity of the whole system and advances the further emergence of this infinitely high-order category system with the capacity to evolve. Every direction of evolution has value, and its value lies in enriching the space of evolution and preparing for the next emergence.

So the evaluation function is a descriptive tool, not a judgment on the legitimacy of existence. Later in this book I will compare institutions and strategies at great length, better and worse, higher and lower, but please remember: what is being compared is the energy-cost ratio and the capacity to evolve, not the right to exist. Having a better option does not mean the old option must be eliminated. Maintaining diversity is the safer and faster way to achieve evolutionary emergence. This point will come up again in Chapter 6 when discussing gentle and severe game strategies.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: In physics, "action" and "free energy" have precise mathematical definitions and measurable values. In social science, "least cost" and "system energy consumption" do not. Without a precise definition, "minimizing system energy consumption" is not a falsifiable proposition but a rhetorical frame: any outcome can be explained after the fact as "having minimized some unspecified cost along some unspecified dimension". Is this not exactly the kind of theory Popper warned about, so powerful in explanation that it cannot be falsified?**

A: This objection is not a rebuttal based on principle; it is only an appearance based on the current level of knowledge. If, in the seventeenth century, when Newton had just proposed his theory, I had asked: can you give a mathematical description of the mechanism by which steam acts after water boils? He could not, could he? Does that mean one could say Newton's theory was wrong, was metaphysics, had no practical value? The value of Newton's framework in 1687 lay not in how much it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided an architecture within which work could accumulate. What I am doing is precisely to establish the whole of social science, like natural science, on the foundation of a mathematical principle. You cannot, at the moment this architecture is just sprouting, question the principle itself because it cannot yet give a precise mathematical model of some particular phenomenon.

But I also accept the reasonable part of the objection: Newton in 1687 did give operational definitions of mass, force and acceleration, and that is the minimum bar for a theory in its infancy. So the demand this book places on itself is this: whereas Newton's theory targeted only the specific domain of physics, my theory is a meta-theoretical framework covering both natural science and social science, and you cannot expect it to directly contain formulas for specific indicators, but it should be expressible precisely in the language of category theory; and when this meta-principle is applied to any specific discipline, it should turn into specific formulas, just like the relationship between a generic interface and its concrete implementations in a programming language. The minimization and maximization principles listed above in the various disciplines are the explicit versions of the basic properties that emerge when this meta-principle is applied in disciplines at different levels. As for its operational definition in the social domain, Chapter 6 of this book gives the first one: the severity of the tit-for-tat strategy, as the control parameter for the convergence speed and stability of social structure.

**Q: This merely reorganizes what is already known in more elegant language. Does that count as new physics?**

A: I believe that reorganizing language to express the same thing is not a meaningless act. When Eilenberg and MacLane introduced categories and functors in 1945, they discovered no new mathematical fact; they merely provided a precise language for the "natural transformations" already in use in algebraic topology. If someone had asked at the time, "what can your category theory predict that group theory cannot?", strictly speaking the answer was nothing. But that work was one of the central advances of twentieth-century mathematics, because it made previously invisible relationships visible, let bridges be built between previously independent fields, and let later generations do work in the new language that could not be done in the old one. The entire wager of this book lies here: whether it lets people, for the first time, ask questions that could not be asked in the old language of social science. When you reach Part II, you can judge for yourself.
