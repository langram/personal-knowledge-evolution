# Part V: The Path

## Chapter 19: Starting with One Person

### The first thing: taking back personal data and services

What I have in mind is not merely the ability to pull my data back at any time. It is the complete replacement of commercial companies' products with software and services supplied by the entirely new organizational form of the new system. This goes a step further than merely using open-source software and open-source products: not only is the product open source, but the organizational entity that produces, designs and operates these products and services also runs under the new mechanism. Everything is open source: the organizational form, the method of distribution, the decision algorithms. Anyone can audit, anyone can trace, anyone can take part in decisions, anyone can help build.

This is the first concrete step by which the entire theoretical vision of a new organizational architecture for human society lands in practice. Build a free zone inside the old system that conforms to the new vision, then gain advantage through competition with the old system, and expand step by step, peacefully, to replace it. Beginning with the recovery of personal data and services is an excellent starting point. Starting with me, one person, and then, through advocacy, bringing in more people.

For software and services, begin with open-source projects as substitutes for traditional services, then provide compatible interfaces and take over the software and services those commercial companies provide. For hardware, rent at first; when enough people have joined and the funds are sufficient, buy outright to cut costs further; as it grows stronger, keep expanding: from renting, to buying from retailers, to wholesale, to designing and manufacturing hardware directly. In the early phase, focus only on cloud storage; when the numbers and finances are strong enough, expand into cloud computing, using ready-made open-source models at first, and once the finances are solid, buy hardware outright, write the software, and train large models ourselves. In short, starting from seizing personal data sovereignty, gradually, in an orderly and planned way, take over the full territory of every commercial productive organization of humanity.

### What the first service actually delivers

The first service is cloud storage. What it delivers is not a block of disk space but something more concrete: one person's ability to independently recover and keep using all of their own material. First, preserve the material from which one can start over: original files, original conversations, original records, each with a content identifier and a source. Then, on top of that, build an ever-better understanding: semantic relations, timelines, versions. Local and cloud together support personal control: the user holds their own identity and keys, chooses the execution environment, limits the permissions of agents, keeps recoverable copies, and can switch the party hosting the cloud side. The physical devices in the cloud may still be under the operator's control, which is why a locally usable path, copies in several places, and a migratable running state are all parts of turning control on paper into real capability.

When the service grows from one person to a few, what has to be ready first? How new participants obtain rights, express needs, contribute labor, influence decisions, and how cooperation continues when opinions diverge. Going from one person to two is the first real organizational test of this whole vision. Letting one person's improvement become a capability others can use is the hot-path sharing of Chapter 12: as soon as one person on a given platform has a capability, every user running that platform can share it. When do we expand into computing and more content services? When storage has proven itself, and when the participants' needs and resources have genuinely grown to that point. Every expansion should be backed by real conditions.

### Internal organization changes from day one

Throughout this whole process, fully embrace equality among all. Separate the allocation for individual needs from the allocation of collective resources. The allocation of collective resources, and every decision concerning the collective, is made by the dynamic-weight intelligent network of all participants. Every piece of information that ought to be public is common knowledge, recorded, tracked and made tamper-proof through the Merkle-DAG, which records each participant's contribution. That is to say, from the very beginning, its internal organizational form exists as this brand-new civilizational ecology, while outwardly it takes the form of a traditional commercial company and competes fairly in the market with other organizations of the old system, expanding step by step, attracting, absorbing and transforming the traditional companies of the old system, just as the winners in traditional commercial competition acquire and merge the losers.

We hold to this direction from the start. At the beginning there is only me, so the initial allocation and decisions are made by me alone; later, when there are several people, those people make fair allocations and decisions according to the new system. Funds and resources come in through a mechanism resembling crowdfunding: by describing this mechanism, this organization's ideas, functions, benefits and prospects, we gradually attract people to join. At least as of now, this vision has attracted one person, and that person is me. Through advocacy on the internet and in the real world, we work to attract more people to take part. Many hands make the fire burn high; the vision grows from a seed into a towering tree.

There are, of course, some differences from crowdfunding. First, it is not a matter of consumers paying money without contributing effort: consumers are also producers and creators. Second, there is no need to reach some crowdfunding headcount or funding target before starting. I start on my own, with the cheapest and simplest cloud storage, with my own small pocket money. Third, the ways of taking part are more flexible: if you have confidence and conviction, you can put in more money and effort, and be a participant as well as a consumer; if you lack confidence, you can be a consumer only and provide the most basic user feedback. But in every case, this new mechanism must guarantee the data sovereignty of consuming users. Data sovereignty is the baseline guarantee, not conditional on how much money a user puts in, whether they contribute labor, or whether they agree with the ideas.

### Why we need not wait for the ideal state to be fully realized

The highly efficient organizational mechanisms and capabilities of the future described earlier, along with complete and fast collection, publication and sharing of information, cannot be achieved in one stroke. A game with sufficient and complete information, and peer equality between nodes, are descriptions of an ideal state. The new mechanism does not need to reach this goal immediately, and the goal is most likely unsolvable in the same way Occam's razor and Kolmogorov complexity are, but it is a goal that can be approached by step-by-step iteration. As long as the new mechanism beats the old one by even a little, that is enough to achieve the replacement.

When comparing a traditional collaboration system with a Merkle-plus-AI system, there is no need to prove that the latter has already formed global common knowledge. It is enough to observe whether, on the same kind of task, it reduces the cost of finding knowledge, duplicated labor, collaboration delay, omitted information, or the cost of correcting errors. That is already a valuable result. The goal of the new mechanism is not complete information and absolute rationality; it is to keep reducing, under resource constraints, the losses caused by information asymmetry, cognitive error, collaboration friction and bad decisions.

### The smallest unit of conversion is a task

Traditionally, when people discuss replacing one system with another, they take the nation, the regime, the enterprise or even the individual as the unit of conversion, as if a person had to first decide whether to join the new system completely before benefiting from it. But the new mechanism opens an important possibility: the smallest unit of system conversion can be a single task, a single collaboration, a single contribution of knowledge, or even a single concrete act of resource allocation. A person can keep a full-time job at a traditional company while using spare time to complete an open-source task through the public collaboration network. That task uses the Merkle system to record requirements, task decomposition, knowledge sources, contributions, verification results and payment. The same person can go back to doing other tasks in the traditional way next week. They do not need to change their social identity or employment relationship first in order to genuinely experience the new collaboration mechanism.

The path from spare-time participation to a real occupation is therefore testable: learn and experience, by reading the works and trying the simulations, with no employment or financial risk; low-risk task collaboration, sharing knowledge, proposing solutions, taking part in code and data projects, and observing how the new mechanism actually performs; paid real tasks, taking part in real projects under clear contracts, payment, responsibility and dispute rules, and earning verifiable compensation; stable hybrid collaboration, keeping the existing job while voluntarily raising the share of tasks done in the new network; and finally, only when income stability, risk protection and quality of life meet one's own requirements, considering a change to one's main work arrangement. Along this route, social migration itself has costs, and the payoff depends on whether others migrate too. So the new mechanism must lower not only the cost of executing tasks but also the cost and risk of migrating from the old mechanism to the new. This is the same thing as the engineering route of compatibility, wrapping and gradual migration in Chapter 12.

Gains in collaboration efficiency do not automatically bring higher personal income. If a new system halves the total cost of a task, and most of that saving comes from AI automation, then how that value is divided among users, developers, data contributors and compute providers still requires independent institutional design. Otherwise the new system can perfectly well recreate the concentration of wealth while raising efficiency. So the economic model must develop in step with the technical prototype, not wait until the system has grown large before deciding how to distribute. This is exactly why the internal organization must change from day one.

### From a service for oneself to a shared intelligence

What truly deserves verification is whether the self-fulfilling positive feedback actually appears: when the new mechanism begins to deliver real benefits, more people join voluntarily; more participants bring more knowledge, capability and opportunities to collaborate; these increments keep improving the system's performance, which attracts still more participants. There is also a reverse mechanism: growing numbers can bring spam, fake contributions, dispute costs and governance complexity. So what needs to be watched is not raw user growth but whether, as the scale grows, the net benefit available to each participant rises or falls. This should be the most important early research metric for the whole project.

One person's knowledge base is this organization's first prototype of a service for its own use. It first has to prove that I really can take my scattered records back, keep using them, and control their storage and authorization; that the cost of building and running it can be calculated clearly; and that when a second person joins later, they too can obtain clear guarantees and take part under public rules. This I have already done. All the material of this book, every original utterance, every revision, every version, is in this base, with content identifiers, and can be verified. From this base, to storage shared by two people, to a service maintained jointly by a few, to a small organization running by the new rules, each step is the natural extension of the one before.
