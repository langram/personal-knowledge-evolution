## Chapter 17: Alignment: Aligned with Whom

### The definition of alignment

Alignment, for artificial intelligence and large models, means the process of ensuring that the outputs and behavior of an AI system stay consistent with human values, intentions and social norms: turning the model from a base that can only continue a piece of text into an assistant that is helpful, honest and harmless.

Start from this definition and combine it with an objective fact: within human civilization, the values, intentions, political rules and social norms of different peoples, different regimes and different classes differ from one another and cannot be made consistent. The conclusion follows naturally. In the game of human society, every player with the ability to create and control AI, whether a regime, a class, a company or an individual, develops and holds an assistant that is "helpful, honest and harmless" to itself, and in that same game this assistant may become, in the eyes of the other players, the accomplice of a "fearsome and harmful" adversary. Because AI's powerful capacity for self-iteration and self-upgrading amplifies and accelerates the capability of the player who controls it, it not only increases the chance that one's adversary acquires a far greater ability to harm or even destroy oneself, but, because different players advance AI at different rates, it also widens the gap in capability between players: a difference of a few months becomes a difference of hundreds or thousands of times in capability.

### Balanced or unbalanced, the result is terrifying

If development is unbalanced and one player dominates, the gap in strength between the parties is so great that for the dominant player the highest-return, lowest-cost way to play is to use AI to empower violence and crush all the others, ending in winner-take-all, with the rest enslaved or destroyed.

If development is balanced and several parties stand off against one another, then because of mutual distrust and insufficient exchange of information, any of them might, without any strong deliberate malice, trigger the destruction of civilization through a passing idea or a tiny misunderstanding. It is like the Cold War, when the whole world worried that the political powers holding nuclear weapons might destroy the entire world over a small misunderstanding.

Even at the global macro level, humanity as a whole treats AI as another opponent in this game, afraid that once AI gains self-awareness it will turn from humanity's assistant into a tyrant that enslaves or destroys humanity.

The example of the child swinging the chain flail now has a concrete setting. The AI race between China and the United States is like an intelligent creature with two brains, a left and a right, each hand swinging its own chain flail, but with no effective alignment mechanism between the two brains, so that the swinging of the flails cannot be properly coordinated, and sooner or later the two hammers may collide and tangle, the weapons go out of control, and the creature wounds itself.

### The alignment that really needs solving

In truth, what most needs to be solved right now is the alignment between the values, intentions and social norms of every organization and every individual in all of humanity, and their outputs and behavior. Once that is solved, the alignment between humanity's carbon-based biological intelligence and the computer's silicon-based AI intelligence, which is the aggregated product of human knowledge, follows as a matter of course. Going further, what we must do is the ultimate alignment between the new civilizational entity of humans plus AI and the whole ecology of the Earth and the universe. That is the final goal of alignment.

Humanity has already faced the risk of nuclear weapons destroying the Earth's ecology and of modern civilization driving global warming. If this global alignment at the most macro level is not done well, human civilization empowered by AI will keep facing risks of this kind.

Alignment therefore has three layers: the alignment of humans with humans, the alignment of humans with AI, and the alignment of civilization with the ecology. All the attention today is on the middle layer, and the middle layer looks unsolvable because the first layer has not been done. A civilization that is internally split in its values and whose parts threaten one another will train "aligned" AIs each of which is a piece of that split.

### Alignment as a fixed point: a direction still to be worked out

Chapter 5 said that my intuition is that alignment can also be studied from the angle of fixed points: place it on the mechanism by which the parties' strategies and institutions are updated in the evolutionary game, and look at where that updating process converges. This section is a blank left open, not a conclusion. My original discussion was very vague, and I write down here only the few things that can be settled now, leaving the rest for later.

First, a fixed point does not tell good from bad. Cooperation against cooperation pays three each; defection against cooperation pays five and zero; mutual defection pays one each. Under mutual defection, either party switching alone to cooperation falls from one to zero, so it is a stable state that neither wants to change unilaterally, yet mutual cooperation is plainly better. If "any unilateral change would only make that party worse off" is called alignment, then the prisoner's dilemma this book wants to escape becomes alignment too. So what alignment has to characterize is the cooperative kind among the fixed points of the updating mechanism, not the mutually harmful kind; how to separate the two mathematically is something I have not done.

Second, whatever the final characterization is, alignment will not be a fixed endpoint but a state within the runtime. When new things appear, new players enter, or resources change, the old fixed point breaks and the system has to find a new one. So what alignment requires is not a one-time injection of values but a continuous capacity for correction, which is exactly what a social neural network of dynamic weights does.

Third, alignment is not one party imposing its values on the others. What that produces is not a fixed point but a tension held down by external force, ready to spring back at any moment. The false peace in *Watchmen* is a fake fixed point of exactly this kind. A true fixed point is the position the parties walk to on their own, under common knowledge, verifiable records and symmetric rules of the game.

Apply this direction to humans and AI: an intelligence trained on the knowledge of all humanity takes its training material from all humanity, but its payoff function is not decided by the training material; it is decided by whoever trains it. So alignment between humans and AI comes down to who sets the payoff function and by what standard. Only when the payoff function is set as the maximization of the interests of all human civilization can its misalignment with humanity happen solely when humanity's own payoff functions contradict one another. Apply it to civilization and the ecology: a civilization that regards itself as part of the Earth's dissipative structure and pursues maximal connection with external objects will not choose to destroy the environment it depends on, unless the game inside it forces it to. So the order of the three layers of alignment runs from the inside out, and the root is in the first layer.

### The reliability of two kinds of civilization

Chapter 15 said that technology is a double-edged sword for two root reasons: the reliability of the technology itself, and the reliability of the civilizational entity that uses it. The alignment problem is another name for the problem of civilization's reliability. The more aligned a civilization is internally, the safer the technology in its hands; the more divided a civilization is internally, the more every technology in its hands is a potential weapon of self-injury. So with nuclear weapons, so with AI.

This is why this book does not treat AI safety as an independent technical problem, but as one indicator of a civilization's capacity for self-governance. To make AI trustworthy, first make civilization trustworthy; to make civilization trustworthy, first make it possible for every node inside it, under common knowledge and symmetric play, to verify one another and correct one another instead of threatening one another.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Disagreements over values are not disagreements over facts. Science can dissolve disagreements over facts; it cannot dissolve disagreements over values. How could the first layer of the three-layer alignment possibly be achieved by a single mechanism?**

A: The principle of relativity in Chapter 5 already answers half of this: there is no absolute coordinate system of values, only transformations between coordinate systems. What the first layer of alignment demands is not that everyone hold the same set of values, but that everyone accept the same set of transformation rules: verify facts under common knowledge, negotiate values under symmetric play, and bear the consequences under the rule that failure means compensation and correction. This set of rules contains no specific value content of its own, so it applies equally to people holding every kind of value. Disagreements over values do not vanish under these rules; they become the diversity described in Chapter 2, part of the space of evolution rather than a reason to annihilate one another. The other half of the answer is in Chapter 5: that piece of meta-cognition, which reduces the opposition of values from "good against evil" to "differences with conditions of formation," lets the disagreement cool down psychologically first, and then enter the rules.
