## Chapter 16: The Gap Between Technology and Institutions

### A startling fact

Since the Second World War, the rules of the political, economic and financial game between the nations of the world, and the political and economic systems within each nation, have seen almost no fundamental change. Technology over the same period has developed at breakneck speed. Information technology alone has gone through the waves of the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet and AI. Especially in the information age from the late seventies to today, the world's total wealth has grown rapidly, but the gap between rich and poor has grown wider and wider: the share of total wealth held by the richest one-thousandth of the population in the United States tripled over thirty-odd years, and the share held by the richest one percent in China doubled in twenty. Every country shows fractured public opinion and rising nationalism, people everywhere are dissatisfied with their governments and their economic conditions, and conflict between nations continues without pause.

At the individual level, thanks to the technology dividend, people have been strengthened on the objective plane of survival and well-being. But the many negative effects of a governance system that has fallen out of step with technological development, compounded by the information age's amplifying effect, in which bad news travels a thousand miles, have pushed ordinary people, and young people above all, into a collective slide in subjective well-being. If the enormous speed gap between technological development and institutional change is allowed to keep widening, destabilizing factors will accumulate step by step, quantity will turn into quality, and sooner or later a serious conflict or crisis will break out.

### The root of the old institutions' disease

This gap is not accidental. Chapters 6 and 7 already explained its mechanism: the inequality of power in the pyramid structure produces unfair distribution, unfair distribution produces distrust, distrust produces a still larger power gap, and so on around the loop. Technological progress merely speeds up the creation of wealth, and in this loop the creation of wealth only speeds up the divergence. So the technology dividend has not automatically turned into shared happiness. That is not a problem of technology; it is a problem of institutions.

The old institutions are hard to change, and that too has its mechanism. Historically, replacing an institution has usually meant violent revolution. A person of the old system possesses a life experience that was tested through long iteration under the old system, lived through and adapted to; they have never really experienced the new mechanism, do not understand it, and still less know what long-term work and life under it would really feel like. People instinctively resist, doubt and reject what is unknown and unexperienced; that is the nature of seeking gain and avoiding harm. In the past, transitions from old systems to new, apart from violent revolution, were also accomplished by a spiritual pre-experience delivered through religion and propaganda: heaven and the afterlife in religion, the description of communist society in the Communist Manifesto, America's rendering of the American Dream. Chapter 21 will explain how the new system provides this pre-experience, and provides it as something people can take part in and verify.

### We do not have much time left

Because of the rapid evolution of AI technology, AI has already begun to acquire a preliminary capacity to iterate and evolve on its own. As that capacity matures, technology will be driven forward by AI at tremendous speed, and the arrival of the technological singularity that people have long discussed lies in the not-distant future.

Because China and the United States are now caught in something like a prisoner's dilemma, confronting and distrusting each other, neither willing to stop accelerating AI research, the current frantic AI race makes the acceleration of AI irreversible, and the day of the technological singularity draws closer and closer. Once the technological singularity arrives while the mismatch between civilization's institutions and technological development remains unsolved, the gap between the two will be stretched without limit, and the probability that human civilization, out of fear of AI or out of fear of the few who control AI, walks into self-destruction will be stretched without limit along with it.

So we do not have much time left. Within this brief window, we must design, as fast as we can, an entirely new system of human civilization that has the same self-iterating, self-upgrading mechanism as AI systems, so that the speed of civilization's evolution matches the speed of technology's evolution, so that the civilizational singularity and the technological singularity arrive together, and so that we build a community of humans and AI that transcends the old civilization and eliminates once and for all the risk of humans and AI systems collapsing out of control and heading for destruction.

### Let civilization's self-correction capacity keep pace with technology's acceleration

Human history has seen countless disagreements over old systems, old understandings and old technologies that looked impossible to resolve, and that were then unified or replaced by entirely new institutions, knowledge and technologies as understanding and science advanced. The latest example happened in AI itself: from the early dispute between symbolism and connectionism, to the later dispute between CNNs and RNNs, until the appearance of the Transformer architecture unified mainstream understanding in the field. These disagreements are not bad in themselves; they are the very logic at the bottom of the universe: evolution through diversity and mutual competition, upgrading step by step. Still, in this process of diversified exploration, the less energy wasted the better.

Unfortunately, not every field can complete its iterations as peacefully as pure technology does. Political disagreements in human civilization cost vast numbers of human lives for nothing; disagreements over product and technology routes in the economy waste vast amounts of capital and resources for nothing. The reason lies in the meta-rules of human civilization's evolution: the traditional tit-for-tat strategy model, with its persistently high risk of confrontation and betrayal, is still far too primitive. As a result, although the development of society's institutions, knowledge and technology does not run on a preset track, problems of trust, adversarial tension, path dependence and many other causes produce problems like the prisoner's dilemma and the trolley problem, which burn enormous amounts of cost. Technology and business are the lucky cases: the former loses only intellectual and computing resources, the latter capital and resources. In political disagreements, once the state's machinery of military violence joins in, what is lost is precious human life.

Let us use better meta-strategies, so that on the road of human civilization's evolutionary exploration we stop running into the problems above as far as possible, and so that every time such a problem does arise we can find, flexibly, quickly and cheaply, a new path that dissolves the disagreement and lets us evolve together. Above all, let the transformation of human social institutions never again cost human lives, as changes of institution have throughout history.

### Humanity's road is not the two tracks of the trolley problem

In the classic science fiction work *Watchmen*, in order to pull the United States and the Soviet Union back from the brink of nuclear war and make hostile regimes cooperate, the smartest man in the world creates a more powerful common enemy, an external threat that forces all sides into a false and fragile peace. The tension between them does not disappear; it is only pressed together by a larger outside force. His method replaces a greater loss with a smaller one, a solution of the same kind as the trolley problem. In my view this is obviously not the wisest course, nor the means the smartest man should adopt. Because the trajectory of human society's evolution is not a track laid down in advance, and the choice is not between the two tracks of crushing one person or crushing a crowd.

The trolley problem is a dilemma because it presupposes tracks. A civilization that can correct itself, explore in parallel, and move its conflicts out of reality into virtual space to make trial and error there faces not two tracks but an open field that can be laid out anew. Chapter 18 will finish describing this choice.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: If a more advanced regulatory mechanism can only be built by first destroying the old one, then the losses it causes during the transition should be counted in its own total cost.**

A: Agreed, and this is exactly why I insist on a non-violent transition. When comparing two civilizational mechanisms, we cannot compare only their efficiency once they are running stably; we must also compare the costs of learning, migration, reorganization, coordination and risk that moving from one mechanism to the other necessarily entails. As long as the new mechanism is even slightly stronger than the old one, people will switch of their own accord, just as objects always move toward lower potential energy. What we need is not the energy to destroy the old system, but a reduction in the energy the switch requires. So the new mechanism must not only lower the cost of carrying out tasks, it must also lower the cost and risk of migrating from the old mechanism to the new. That is what the engineering route of Chapter 12, compatibility first, then replacement, means at the level of institutions.
