## Chapter 20: The Growth of the Free Zone and the Peaceful Evolution of Institutions

### Unify the basic rules, expand the diversity of institutional exploration

Under the premise of unified basic rules, the new mechanism can maximize the capacity for diverse experiments with public social rules. How does an evolvable system, in the maze of evolutionary paths through the near-infinite possibilities of human social rules, fork efficiently in parallel, explore all the possibilities in diverse ways, traverse these paths fully, and, with higher energy efficiency and lower evolutionary cost, find the faster, shorter, better path and construct a better human social order? This is the core question of institutional evolution.

The DNA system of living things is unified in its rules and architecture, and this unity did not restrict diversity; it released it. Institutions are the same. What is unified is the five rules of Chapter 7, the two kinds of allocation of Chapter 8, the dynamic weights of Chapter 9, and the data sovereignty of Chapter 14: these are the base. On top of the base, the concrete organizational forms, the detailed rules of distribution, the decision procedures can all fork, run in parallel, and be compared. China's special economic zones, which experiment locally first and spread what works, are in essence diversified experiment plus feedback selection, and they ran in the field of economic policy for thirty years. The new mechanism turns this from a practice inside one country into a basic capability that every participant can use: any small organization can fork its own rules on top of the base, run them, record them, compare them with other forks; the good ones are adopted, the poor ones are recorded as negative results.

Beyond diversified experiments in reality, the AI-driven virtual human-society laboratory in digital space that I envision can also act as an enormous accelerator, just as humans use biological laboratories to run accelerated experiments on genes, completing evolution that would have taken hundreds of millions of years in the real world. Chapter 21 will cover it.

### From renting resources to building productive capacity

The previous chapter laid out the path from cloud storage to cloud computing to training our own models. Each step on this path corresponds to a growth in organizational capability: when renting resources, the organization only needs to manage allocation; when buying resources, the organization needs to manage assets; when designing and manufacturing hardware, the organization needs to manage research, development and production chains. Each step is the natural result of the previous step running successfully under the new mechanism, not a pre-planned timetable.

### Free entry and free exit

The replacement process is free. Anyone can join or leave at any time, and the system provides compatibility of data and applications on joining and leaving, together with free import and export. Replacement relies not on force but on higher efficiency and better returns, raising attractiveness by raising returns, a win-win expansion.

The right to exit is part of this mechanism, not a threat to it. In a closed agrarian environment exit is expensive, and cooperation can be sustained by patience and local hierarchy, so a stable centralized order grows up. In an open maritime environment exit is cheap, and cooperation has to invent identity costs or portable reputation. The free zone is open, so it must have portable history: a person leaves carrying their entire record from here, takes that record to another fork, or brings it back. Portable history makes honest exit cheap and treacherous exit expensive. This is why the records of Chapter 14 and the data sovereignty of Chapter 19 are not only protections for the individual; they are the precondition for this organization being able to be open.

### Why growing in scale should not turn back into a closed giant

If you truly understand the new system, this expansion, merging and transformation will not produce the kind of monopoly seen under the old system. On the contrary: through more equal distribution, fuller collection and circulation of information, and better decision algorithms, participants, being more equal and less antagonistic, lean more toward cooperation and sharing, so the new institution produces the exact opposite of monopoly, and powerfully drives the advance of science, technology and productivity.

A traditional giant cannot defeat this system by building a competing product, because it cannot monopolize this ecology by locking down accounts, data, permissions or toolchains. It can only transform its own organizational structure, merge into the system, and become a powerful contributor; and the whole company thereby changes from a pyramid organization into a social-neural-network style of equal division of labor and dynamic-weight decision making, with fairer distribution of economic returns and higher organizational efficiency. The big-company disease, in which a pyramid-structured company gets less efficient and less creative as it grows, is cured at the root. Joining this system means a project's development efficiency does not face the same problem as it scales. The mark of this system's maturity is that the project's development process is ultimately completed entirely by the project itself, through self-iteration, on its own capability.

Internal equality and the absence of outward domination must each be implemented separately. Even if an organization practices joint governance internally, those who control key infrastructure, interfaces or resource scheduling can acquire new power. So open standards, interoperation between different nodes, replaceable administrators, appealable decisions, and members' real ability to exit or form a separate organization should all be capabilities continuously maintained throughout the expansion. If the right to exit, portable history and appeal work only internally, the free zone will grow hawks facing outward, as Chapter 6 said. So toward other people still in the old mechanism, the rule is: no worse off, once externalities are counted. That is the hard constraint on expansion.

### From productive cooperation to wider public affairs

On one side, advance the commercial territory in the real world; on the other, advance the cultural territory through literature, film, television and games, so that the whole new system grows ever stronger in both the economic and the cultural domains. When a certain moment of maturity is reached, the new system can formally be carried into the political domain, gradually replacing the old political system. In this process, traditional commercial companies and national political institutions are gradually eliminated, or more precisely, their functions are replaced by the brand-new architecture of the new system.

Two things must be kept apart here: eliminating permanent, unaccountable privileges of power, and eliminating all coordinating functions. They are not the same thing. Any large-scale complex system needs to handle assignment of responsibility, emergency response, specialized coordination and disputes. Even without a boss in the traditional sense, someone may need to serve as project coordinator for a certain period; even without a fixed management layer, someone needs to be responsible for resource security and system failures. The new system's approach is to turn these responsibilities into functional roles with clear scope and limited terms, that can be delegated and revoked, and that are subject to audit, rather than permanent positions bound to particular people.

Chapter 9 said that the legitimacy of existing institutions comes from equal voting: the losing side accepts the outcome because it knows that next time its vote will still count. The new system's legitimacy has a different source. What the losing side accepts is a rule that it is itself inside, in which its own judgment is counted, which can be checked after the fact, and which will be corrected when wrong. This cannot be made believable by argument; it can only be made visible by running. So the free zone does not sell this institution; it builds its foundation. git never marketed open-source governance. First build what a single person needs on day one: verifiable records, portable personal data and agent memory, a contribution ledger for open-source collaboration. The first polity to choose is a newly founded organization, a cooperative, a foundation, not a nation. If the foundation proves itself, people will come asking for the social layer; if not, no argument for the social layer, however good, means anything.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The definition of a state is a monopoly on violence. A state that does not take part in your system can simply shut it down. A ledger cannot replace that.**

A: Correct, which is why this book never advocates confronting the state. It advocates running, within the state and in a lawful organizational form, an entity that operates by the new rules, and letting its efficiency and the real gains of its members speak. Its relationship to the state is like git's relationship to software companies: git never overthrew a single company, but today not one company can do without it. A state can shut down a node; it cannot shut down a content-addressed, distributed way of keeping records that is already used by millions. When this way of working becomes part of people's daily work, when the history it records is more complete and more trustworthy than any institution's archives, the institutions themselves will begin to use it, and then depend on it. That is how institutions are replaced: layered on top of the old, one layer at a time, until the center of gravity shifts.
