# Part II: The Construction of Society

## Chapter 6: People and Atoms: Tit-for-Tat and the Fixed Points of Social Structure

### What social science lacks is a basic building block

The reason social science lags so far behind natural science in precision and reliability is that it has never found the properties and laws of its basic constituent element, and so it has never been able to build an axiomatic system. What this chapter sets out to do is find that element, and show how it constructs the various structures of society.

Take the analogy between human society and a chemical system. The basic object of a chemical system is the atom, and atoms connect through chemical bonds to form all kinds of molecules. The rules for forming chemical bonds are determined by the properties of the atom's outer electrons, the whole process follows the principle of energy minimization, and it finally reaches a stable fixed-point state under the constraints of quantum mechanics. In human society, the basic object is the person, and the simplest connection between one person and another is cooperation. Cooperation is reached through repeated games driven by each side's own interests, and that process likewise follows the fixed-point principle and the principle of minimization: the Nash equilibrium of game theory is a fixed point in strategy, where no one can gain by unilaterally changing strategy; Pareto optimality is the energy-minimized state of resource allocation, where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

So what inner property of the human individual corresponds to the outer electron structure of the atom? The answer is a piece of common knowledge rooted in our physiology and psychology and universally observed in games: tit-for-tat. The strategy was proposed by Rapoport, and it proved its power in Axelrod's computer tournaments: cooperate on the first move; after that, if the other side cooperates, I cooperate; if the other side defects, I punish; if the other side comes back, I forgive. It fits perfectly the properties a dualoid needs in order to construct complex structures: stability, variability, composability, duality.

### The reward-punishment mechanism is the evaluation function, tit-for-tat is the iteration operator

Apply the principle of Chapter 3 here: the game between people is the dynamic process that constructs the fixed-point structures of human society. The mechanism of reward and punishment is an evaluation function; the tit-for-tat strategy is the iteration process of that function. It makes the process tend toward convergence or divergence rather than random oscillation, so that structures which satisfy the evaluation function tend to converge, and structures which do not tend to separate, instead of combining and separating at random without ever forming a stable structure. Through tit-for-tat, people reward cooperation and punish defection, which sets off chain reactions that keep strengthening or weakening their ties, continually constructing every kind of relational structure between people, just as atoms form chemical bonds by losing, gaining and sharing their outer electrons. This chain effect of positive and negative feedback is the basic reaction of human social construction.

The operation of tit-for-tat is controlled jointly by three layers of the human being, physiological, psychological and rational, which together form a multi-layered feedback loop. At the bottom is the physiological hardware: when an individual gains from cooperation, the brain's reward centers produce dopamine; when his interests are damaged by betrayal, they produce norepinephrine. In the middle is the operating system of the psychological layer: under the influence of these two neurotransmitters arise the basic emotions of pleasure on one side and fear and anger on the other, which are fast heuristic algorithms for decision-making and supply the motivation for rational thought. At the top is the application software of reason: driven by emotion, the prefrontal cortex runs a cost-benefit analysis and finally returns reward for goodwill and punishment for malice.

### Strictness: the first operational definition

Tit-for-tat strategies of different intensity affect the speed of convergence, the strength and the stability of the fixed-point structure. This is the first operational definition this book gives to social science: strictness, meaning the intensity of punishment for defection and the time interval required before forgiveness.

Strictness is not a discrete type in the strict mathematical sense but a complete spectrum from lenient to strict. It is like naming the colors of light red, orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue and violet: we are only drawing conceptual divisions on a continuous space of wavelengths. The universality of this spectrum shows itself in the fact that human structures, at whatever level and in whatever concrete organization, always differentiate from lenient to strict. The members of a family, a criminal gang, a corporate board, the parliament of a democracy, the politburo of an authoritarian state, the several parties of one country, any association at all: among the individuals taking part there is always a spectrum from doves to hawks. It is just as in physics and category theory, where symmetry, being everywhere, becomes one of the basic principles of the discipline and takes part in the construction of every higher property; and just as the outer-electron properties of atoms are a major factor in the production of countless higher structures at the chemical and biological levels.

### Genes, emotions and who stands on which side

What determines which side of the spectrum a person falls on? The first factor is physiology.

I split a person's decision into two parts: the part that is rational thought, and an emotional amplification factor. The former is a higher-order function whose variables are higher-order conclusions of knowledge at the social, economic, political and moral levels; the latter is a physiological parameter that sets the intensity and duration of emotional response. Differences at the genetic level, such as the different variants of the monoamine oxidase A gene, affect how fast neurotransmitters are broken down, and thereby affect the development of the emotion-regulating circuits. People carrying the low-activity variant show a stronger amygdala response when provoked and impose heavier punishment after intense provocation; this has been observed both in brain imaging and in economic experiments. The chain is simple: people of different genotypes respond to betrayal with emotions of different intensity and duration; in the execution of tit-for-tat, that difference shows up as different punishment intensity and different forgiveness intervals; in an actual game, one kind behaves more like a hawk and the other more like a dove. What genes influence is how much the emotional factor interferes with the rational game, and thereby the probability that a person leans hard or soft in the game.

In an early draft I wrote outright that "the low-activity variant corresponds to hawks and centralism, the high-activity variant to doves and democracy." That was far too absolute a claim. The effect size of a single gene is tiny, and it depends heavily on childhood environment; as an explanation of political systems it does not stand. The correct position is this: genes determine the ordering, the game determines the cut-off point. A thought experiment makes it clear. Suppose you cut out every hawk in a group. The remaining people get re-ordered by the relative intensity of their emotional responses, and as the game iterates in chain reaction, the relatively more reactive portion differentiates into hawks again. Cutting out half only cuts the full range of emotional response in half; the remaining half differentiates just the same. Genes decide who crosses the line first; the payoff structure of the game decides at which person the cut is made, and the proportion drifts back to the neighborhood set by the ratio of the gains that can be seized to the cost of conflict. Genetically identical mice in identical environments still form stable dominance and subordination hierarchies, which shows that differentiation itself happens even without genetic differences: iterated games amplify tiny random differences into stable roles, which is symmetry breaking; when genetic differences exist, they set the starting point of the amplification.

### Environment: the cheapest way to resolve conflict

The second factor is the acquired environment of the game, and at the level of the group it matters far more than genes.

By the principle of minimization, people always tend to resolve disagreements in the way that costs least. In a country, regime or civilization that is already under authoritarian rule, the power gap is enormous, violent suppression is the cheapest method, and so people tend to solve problems by violence. This happens again and again. On one side, those inclined to such methods become more successful within the system and are rewarded, and the reward encourages more people to adopt such methods; on the other side, violent suppression builds up enormous rebound tension in the suppressed, and that tension pushes those in power to keep widening the power gap in order to increase structural stability; the widened power gap takes us back to the iteration we started from. The whole structure converges to an authoritarian system of enormous size, enormous power gap and extremely high structural strength. This is a strict positive feedback loop, with a clear mechanism at every step, relying on no mysticism and no narrative of "evil rulers," derived purely from the principle of minimization, selection pressure and feedback loops. It also explains why authoritarian systems are so stable: they are stable attractors, and every iteration reinforces them.

So even a person whose emotions are genetically milder, if born into such an environment, will tend to use hard methods to solve problems. Conversely, in the environment of a democratic system, violent suppression is the more expensive method, and people tend more toward peaceful communication, playing against each other with lenient tit-for-tat, because that way costs less and succeeds more often; even a person whose emotions are genetically more intense will tend toward lenient methods in that environment. Of course, in the same democratic environment, at some critical state, people who all leaned lenient will still split along genetic lines into hawks and doves; and under an authoritarian system, though everyone leans hard, a critical state will still produce a hawk-dove split over some particular issue. The payoff structure of the environment sets the baseline of the group's strategy and the proportion between the two camps; genes decide who crosses the line first at the critical state. Join the two factors and the model is complete.

When hard strategies are cheap in an authoritarian environment, cheap for whom? Cheap for those above, extremely expensive for those below: hardness directed upward gets crushed. So that environment does not just push everyone's baseline toward hardness; it binds strategy to position: hawk downward, dove upward. A strategy that decides who yields by position rather than by strength saves the cost of fighting, which is why it is stable, and that is exactly where the pyramid is cheap. The same logic explains the hawks in the so-called democratic camp: violence is expensive at home and cheap abroad, because outsiders have no channel for appeal or retaliation, and so the same people are lenient inside and hard outside. The history of colonialism is the product of this payoff structure.

### Two higher fixed points: authoritarianism and democracy

In a vector space shaped by the overlapping influence of physical, geographical, physiological, psychological, economic and political factors, human civilization has evolved two important higher fixed-point structures: the authoritarian system and the democratic system. They correspond to two subtypes of the tit-for-tat strategy: the strict version centered on East Asian Chinese culture, and the lenient version practiced in the European regions around the Mediterranean.

Strict tit-for-tat has been validated countless times in history; the strength and stability of its structure are enough to support a mighty empire, Rome and the Chinese dynasties. But an overly strong single structure usually slows the pace of a civilization's evolution. In China, after the Qin and Han, the contention of a hundred schools of thought never returned, and the following two thousand years were basically the difference between Confucianism 1.0 and Confucianism n.0; China's writing system also stagnated after the Qin and Han. Western civilization likewise went through a long stagnation of natural science under the authoritarian control of the Catholic Church. And the collapse and reconstruction of such a strong structure is enormously costly: China, as the most famous and largest authoritarian system, has seen every dynastic change accompanied by massive loss of life; and even so, it was only a change of dynasty, not progress of civilization. The European civilization around the Mediterranean evolved far faster and with far more diversity than the East Asian cultural sphere, and the intensity of internal conflict during political iteration differed enormously as well.

What I am using here is the theorem stated earlier: high strictness means fast convergence and strong structural stability, but it also means being easily trapped in a locally optimal fixed point and unable to climb out, weakening or even losing the capacity to evolve; a structure whose stability is too fragile will not do either. How to construct a sensible mechanism of reward and punishment and a sensible strategy of convergence and divergence is the fundamental factor in how strong a human civilization's capacity to evolve will be.

Geographical parameters have to be counted too. Both were authoritarian empires, but China sat in the relatively isolated geography of East Asia, ringed by high mountains, plateaus and deserts, with large continuous farming regions whose population moved little, whose economic life was tied to the land, and where exit was extremely expensive; only these factors combined with the mechanisms above produced the single dominant and continuously developing authoritarian system. In the fragmented, open environment of the Mediterranean, with its diverse and mobile mix of farming, herding and sea trade, exit was cheap, the Roman Empire could not sustain its rule, cooperation had to invent identity costs or portable reputation, and so horizontal institutions grew up: merchant law, credit, guilds. To test how well the fixed-point and minimization principle generalizes, one must also examine it against the concrete parameters of geography, climate and agriculture, and of physiology, psychology, society and ethnicity; only then can one build a social science genuinely based on first principles, verifiable by statistics over the data of human history, and falsifiable.

### Why preserving difference is more powerful than eliminating it

Through the energy-efficiency ratio we can compare the merits of two strategies or two systems, but that does not mean that once a better option exists the old one should be destroyed. Leading in energy-efficiency ratio only means leading overall in most situations; it does not mean leading in every local performance. Because Chinese has many homophones and every character is a single syllable, it can produce classical poetry of perfectly regular line length and exceptional rhyme, and its lack of complex grammar in turn yields blank space and a hazy beauty. Nor is strict tit-for-tat inferior to the lenient strategy in every situation: when forces must be concentrated to complete quickly a large task that is structurally complex but highly deterministic, the former is more efficient; for tasks that demand rapid response, the former also has the advantage. Even the military systems of democratic countries are based on the strict strategy; in wartime, governments tighten centralization; in nationwide large-scale infrastructure, China is more efficient than the West. Science fiction films often run the plot where the enemy destroys our side's advanced computer network and it is the old, backward radio set that turns out to work in the crisis. So while tilting overall toward the strategy with the higher energy-efficiency ratio, diversity must be preserved, and the democratic system of the lenient strategy preserves diversity better.

The virtual version of tit-for-tat that I envision, which Chapter 9 will describe, moves reward and punishment from material goods and violence in reality to points in a digital space, turning real damage in the real world into virtual damage in a game. Under that mechanism, human civilization will be more diverse, will develop faster, and will be more peaceful.

### Crime is a disease, not evil

From the mechanism of this chapter a conclusion about crime follows. If you regard crime as evil and the criminal as a demon, then at the very bottom of your cognition you are already bound by the fanatical emotion of religious morality; you will be strongly inclined to wipe it out directly by violence and to refuse to understand it and correct it through scientific research. If you do not understand its principle, simple violent elimination can only eliminate the individuals already produced; it cannot eliminate the mechanism that produces them, and it can only keep restaging the cycle of violence answered by violence. This is the other face of the same theorem as "cutting out half the hawks": what changes is the individuals, not the proportion.

To regard crime as a disease is to put this object and this phenomenon into the framework of science and medicine for study, and to treat and prevent it by scientific means. Treatment and prevention aim at the mechanism: reduce the gains that can be seized, raise the cost of harmful acts, insert a delay between punishment and emotion, leave room for appeal against misjudgment. "Disease" here means a phenomenon that can be studied, prevented and corrected, not a person who may be forcibly corrected; what gets recorded is the harmful act, not a category pinned on the person. Treating every harmful person or thing whose internal mechanism we do not yet understand and cannot yet correct with a scientific attitude of study and correction: that is the direct application, in the criminal field, of the meta-cognition from Chapter 5.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: "There is a hawk-dove spectrum at every level" is universality at the level of phenomena, not at the level of mechanism. A stern father in a family and a hawk in a parliament may look similar while being driven by different mechanisms. The conditions under which Axelrod proved that tit-for-tat wins, a two-player game, a fixed payoff matrix, long repetition, no communication, are not strictly satisfied in most real organizations.**

A: That is exactly why I do not treat "tit-for-tat" as a concrete strategy table but as the iteration operator of the evaluation function. In any sufficiently repeated multi-party game, the participants evolve some mechanism of reward and punishment to stabilize cooperation; conditional cooperation, indirect reciprocity, reputation mechanisms, third-party punishment are all different instances of this operator under more complex game structures. They all present a strict-lenient differentiation in appearance precisely because any reward-punishment operator can be parameterized along a strictness dimension. Strictness is the control parameter of the operator; convergence speed and stability are its outputs; and on this point it is the same mechanism at every level. As for the differences between a family and a parliament in kinship, symmetry of power, number of repetitions and cost of exit, those are exactly what I called the "concrete parameters": they change the operator's values in that situation, not the operator itself.

**Q: The hawk-dove game already has ready-made mathematics: in the mixed equilibrium, the proportion of hawks equals the ratio of the contested value to the cost of conflict, and that proportion is set by the payoff structure regardless of who the individuals are. Genes are unnecessary.**

A: That claim goes too far. The hawk-dove game is not a theorem that excludes genes; on the contrary, in its original biological setting it assumes strategies are heritable, and the very concept of an evolutionarily stable strategy is defined for heritable strategies. It merely does not ask who carries which strategy. The moment you allow individuals to differ, the model itself yields a conditional strategy: play hawk when you are the stronger one. At that point individual traits, including the genetically determined part, directly decide who stands on the hawk side. Genes and environment decide who the hawks are; the payoff structure decides how many hawks there are. In the side-blotched lizard, the three throat-color morphs are set by genes, while the proportions of the three morphs in the population rise and fall in turn according to frequency-dependent payoffs. The type is genetic; the proportion is game-theoretic. The two things complement each other; they do not contradict.
