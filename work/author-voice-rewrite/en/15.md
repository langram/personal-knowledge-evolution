# Part IV: Civilization

## Chapter 15: The Attention of Civilization and an Evolvable Runtime

### Resources are limited, so attention is necessary

The attention mechanism of the Transformer architecture resembles the human brain in three ways. First, resources are limited, efficiency drives the design, and weights are used to focus selectively on the factors that matter more. Our eyes and brain take in a flood of visual and auditory signals every second. Reading a book in a noisy cafe, the brain cannot process every detail, so it automatically filters out the background noise and locks its energy onto the text. An AI model processing several thousand words of text has equally limited computing resources. It does not treat every word alike; it computes weights and concentrates them on the few key terms most relevant to the current task. Second, out of a vast, complex and changing body of information, it builds accurate contextual associations through dynamic weights. On reading "that apple is really sweet," a human and an AI both connect it with fruit and taste, not with a phone. Third, it links an enormous number of information objects, with their contents and functions, through query, key and value.

I have been wondering whether the architecture of human civilization I envision, built on decision-making by dynamic weights, could be set up in the same way. On one side, let the root intelligent agent belonging to each human individual map out an individual space covering that individual and their private resources. Through the cooperation of their own brain and their own agents, each person constructs a private attention mechanism, and in their own private space carries out self-management and self-evolution in the three ways above. On the other side, these individual spaces and individual intelligences together make up the whole civilizational space, and at that level a similar mechanism constructs the attention mechanism of civilization, which completes the self-management and evolution of civilization, again in those three ways. What I mean is this: I want to take that architecture of human civilization based on dynamic-weight decisions out of vague imagination, borrow the concrete architecture of attention at the level of human physiology and at the level of AI models, and turn it into a plan that can actually operate through real social organization and real science and computer technology.

### Objects change, so we must enter the runtime

But obviously we cannot copy the algorithms of AI models the way the man in the old story carved a notch in his boat to mark where his sword fell overboard. The basic objects the Transformer handles are semantic nodes: static objects with the dual nature of data and code, and the algorithms rest essentially on probability theory and statistics. The basic objects the new architecture of the human civilization system must handle also possess the persistent state of a runtime. The former is like a collection of programs containing data and code; the latter is a far more complex system that contains the entire runtime. When a model runs inference, the trained parameters stay fixed and what changes with the input is the attention weights; but whichever weights we mean, the objects they attach to, the semantic nodes in the vocabulary, do not increase, decrease or change. The real world is not like that. All the people and external objects attached to human society change as the decision weights and behavior of humans change; these objects can diminish, multiply, change their properties, even be destroyed. Non-renewable resources shrink by exactly what is consumed; renewable resources do not. One person's reward or punishment changes another person's quantity of resources, and even that person's future decision tendencies.

So in a Transformer-like architecture at the individual and civilizational levels, what query, key and value correspond to is clearly not as simple as in an AI model. What I can give at present is only a preliminary correspondence. Like the fixed point of Chapter 17, it is still at the stage of needing to be worked out; I write it down here so that the idea does not stay an empty analogy.

| | In the personal space | In common affairs |
|---|---|---|
| Query (Q): the current problem and demand | What I want to accomplish, what needs solving, what constraints of time and resources apply | What problem the participants face together, what needs and limits each of them raises |
| Key (K): descriptions relevant to the problem | The source, time, version, capability and availability of files, knowledge, devices and tools | How collaborators, evidence and resources relate to the current task, with their scope of validity and conditions of access |
| Value (V): the content or help that can be provided | Material, explanations, methods and tool capabilities one is permitted to read | Evidence, expert opinion, resource commitments and candidate actions permitted to be shared |

Value here means content that can be obtained and used, not a social worth assigned to a person. The same person who asks the question in this computation can be the provider of evidence or the executor of a plan in the next: Q, K and V are roles within one concrete computation, not a permanent sorting of people into those who command, those who are rated and those who are called up as resources. On this correspondence, the minimal process of a common affair has five steps: the query discovers the relevant objects; the relevant capability nodes each propose candidate plans, carrying their weights; a plan executes once it has the authorization of the parties it touches; the result of execution is recorded; and the result feeds back into the weight of every node that took part. Discovery, proposal, authorization, execution, feedback: this is the concrete shape of Chapter 9's dynamic-weight mechanism inside an attention architecture. What it still lacks is a precise way of writing how state updates with action, and how authorization, the right to take part and attention weight are computed separately; that is left for later work.

Whatever the final correspondence is, besides the algorithms of probability theory and statistics, we need the principles of game theory: tit-for-tat, Nash equilibrium, Pareto optimality, the prisoner's dilemma. A mathematical model like Markov games can handle both zero-sum games and cooperative games; the question is whether it can be applied to a system that, like the real universe and human society, evolves dynamically and gives rise to new things and new technologies. My answer: this mathematical model is not necessarily the best choice for the algorithm of the future new system. What matters is not which specific model, but the new-system algorithm constructed through repeated iteration, research and experiment. And whichever model it is, computing the variables in its formulas requires sufficiently abundant and accurate data on the objects in the system.

This brings us back to an important precondition for realizing the new system: the active cooperation and full participation of all participants. The data on these objects in the human civilization system cannot be collected directly the way the data of natural systems can. But as long as we do what Chapter 7 describes, use those meta-rules as the basic rules of cooperation, dissolve the adversarial mindset, strengthen the will to cooperate, and then push forward the research of cooperative algorithms and mechanisms through a sound iterative process, we can build the new mechanism step by step and replace the old one.

### How sequential dependency and parallel exploration combine

Chapter 9 already said that the operation of society is a mixture of two kinds of logic: there are tasks that require causal relations and a fixed order, and there are tasks that can run in parallel. Take the collection, processing, decision and action of information: the overall sequence must be serial, but within each stage there are vast numbers of operations that can run in parallel. This matches the history of computing: the early era of computer applications was dominated by the CPU, the early AI era by the GPU, and in the latest trend the CPU has become important again. What the attention mechanism of civilization must do is decide, according to the nature of the task, which parts follow the causal construction order of whole-system cooperation and which parts run independently in parallel and are then integrated by the market or by the rules of the game, and hand that decision itself over to dynamic weights.

How do multiple perspectives form an executable plan? Multi-head attention lets the same problem receive attention from different angles, then merges the results of the heads. The social counterpart is this: the same problem is judged by capability nodes in multiple fields, each giving its own judgment and plan, each carrying its own weight; these are merged into multiple candidate plans with their weights, and then go into pilot trials. Which angles we assign to the social system is our design choice; the Transformer's attention heads do not naturally correspond to these social functions. But from humans to large models, this mechanism is an effective way to face complex objects and raise the probability that a decision succeeds.

### From the outcome of the game to the rules of cooperation

Under the old system, the outcome of the game determines the rules: whoever wins makes the rules. Under the new system, the rules come before the game: the five rules of Chapter 7 are common knowledge, the positive initial strategy of tit-for-tat is common knowledge, how weights arise and expire is common knowledge. What the outcome of the game changes is the weights and the plans, not the power to make the rules. This is the difference between the runtime of civilization and an ordinary game: a game has an ending; a runtime has none. It has only persistent state and a constant arrival of new things.

Persistent state and the arrival of new things are the hardest part of this runtime. Before a technology appears, no node has any record of judgment about it; after a kind of resource is exhausted, all the weights built around it lose their object. So weights must attach to problems, not to people, and they must expire: when a problem disappears, its weights go to zero; when a new problem appears, all nodes start over from an equal initial weight. This is the fundamental difference between dynamic weights and every kind of static authority.

### The answers lie in the future: training and inference cannot be separated

Set this civilizational brain, with humans and AI agents as its neuron nodes and dynamic decision weights as its algorithm, beside an AI large language model, and you find that the two processes of learning, training and inference in practice are fractals of one structure at different levels: the civilizational body is the same kind of thing as a large language model, at a higher order and a higher level. The difference is that at the level of the civilizational body the input is not text but every problem, large and small, that the whole civilization meets and needs to solve.

These problems are of two kinds. For one kind, the knowledge system of human civilization already has the correct answer, and it is enough to call directly on the intelligence already integrated in the large language models and solve it by inference. The other kind matters more: the problems human civilization has met and is meeting in its development that still have no correct or ideal answer. For this kind, training and inference cannot be separated the way they are for a large language model, with pre-training done off the job. In conventional training the answers to all the training material are already known; the model's inference can be compared with the answer again and again, the error back-propagated, and the model corrected over and over until training is complete.

The training material of a civilizational body is not like that. The correct answers to all its problems lie in the future. The result of one inference is not the final answer but one branch of a trial strategy on the way to the final answer, one state node on one branch of the whole search tree of evolutionary paths.

In this higher-level social intelligent neural system the nodes are not the semantic nodes of a large language model either; they are every human and every AI agent. Each person has a dedicated AI, used to build a personal knowledge base and to produce a personal cognitive model: it takes the civilizational body's consensus knowledge as its base semantic model, adds this person's own semantic and cognitive preferences, and represents the structure of this person's cognitive space. It is the personal model of Chapter 13, post-trained on the common knowledge foundation. These dedicated personal models act as individual agents, either with the person taking part actively or by automatic proxy in a default mode; they join the game, give their opinions, and become neurons in the brain of the whole civilization. The civilizational system assigns different weights to the decisions of these different agent nodes, then gathers the decisions of all the neurons to make collective inferences and decisions.

The reward function used for back-propagation therefore cannot be set by "the distance from the final answer". It is set by reality: how the results these decisions produce when practiced in the real world compare with the earlier plan, how far they fall from what people foresaw and expected, how large the gap is between the results of different decision plans, how satisfied all the participating human agents are, and so on.

So this is not an off-the-job pre-training process. It is a loop in which learning, training and inference iterate again and again against the feedback of productive practice. Civilization lives inside this loop, practicing, constructing new knowledge and evolving all at once. Back-propagation in this process updates three things: the decision weights of the agent nodes across the whole social intelligent neural network; the civilizational body's consensus knowledge model, updated with the new knowledge; and the weights of the semantic nodes in every person's dedicated AI model, and, once brain-computer interface technology matures, in every human brain itself.

### The Large Civilization Model: every person has a vector in civilization space

By this point you will have noticed that this civilizational system is itself a model, a Large Civilization Model. In the high-dimensional space of civilization, each person's dedicated cognitive-semantic large model is an object like a token in a large language model, and the relations among these objects are like the relations among meanings in a large language model. Every meaning has an embedding in a language model; every person has an embedding in civilization. So we can construct a large model of a higher order than the language model, at the level of civilization.

When this civilization-level social intelligent neural network runs, it has two outputs. First, it can train a better large language model that belongs to all of humanity. Second, and more important, in the very act of running it collects all the training material the Large Civilization Model needs: every problem, every node's judgment, every collective decision, every real-world result is a training record. As the new civilizational order continues to iterate and evolve, the Large Civilization Model is constructed within it, and it in turn helps us iterate toward more complex, more advanced civilizational structures and institutions.

### More is different: from computing power to social mechanics

Looked at more generally, humanity already possesses the most powerful mathematical theories and computational tools for studying the evolution of the universe. Philip Anderson's essay on emergence, "More Is Different", revealed the essence of how basic objects in a lower-order structure, through combination, give rise to higher-order complex structures. The question is how to model and study such complex phenomena, then propose a theory, and finally put it into practice and create new emergence. The rapid development of computer technology and the growth of computing power in recent decades have given humanity a way: large numbers of high-performance computers doing linear computation on enormous tensors in high-dimensional spaces, carrying the whole process from mathematical modeling and analysis through theory-building to practice. Quantum mechanics, fluid mechanics, condensed matter physics, the construction of humanity's complex engineering works, and the development of AI itself have all benefited from this.

So far, the training of large AI models is the most computationally demanding task humanity has ever accomplished. In the Large Civilization Model we face next, every unit is itself a high-dimensional semantic model, and the complexity will certainly be several orders of magnitude higher. But I believe that humanity, with the help of AI, will be able to accomplish it. In the not-too-distant future, in the course of this social neural network's evolution, we will gradually work out the principles and models of the "mechanics" by which human nodes of different cognition, in the high-dimensional space of civilization, act on one another in real society, and then use these principles of "social mechanics" to construct more advanced and more complex social structures in human civilization, just as people use the principles of modern mechanics to build ever more complex high-rise buildings. Newton's principles gave humanity the mechanics to raise great buildings; the "mathematical principles" in this book's title are meant to arrive, in the end, at this social mechanics.

### Start testing from the smallest cooperative scenario

This runtime does not need to be tested starting from a nation. The smallest cooperative scenario is two people, one task, and one verifiable record. Start there and observe: can the relevant people find each other, can misunderstandings be detected in time, can expert judgment be tested, can the results of cooperation be developed further by others. Then three people, one project, one organization. Chapters 19 and 20 describe exactly this road.

### Why humanity's knowledge of nature far exceeds its knowledge of itself

Now let us turn from the mechanism to civilization itself.

Look at the world today. On one side, we already have the ability to manipulate single atoms into the shapes we want, to pack billions of transistors onto a chip the size of a fingernail, to edit human genes to treat disease, to build spacecraft and head for space. On the other side, our societies are full of ideological conflict, political turmoil and brutal violent war, from road rage, domestic violence, neighborhood disputes and labor conflicts at the small end, to the riot on Capitol Hill and the movements in the streets at the large end, not to mention the war in Ukraine, the endless wars in the Middle East, and humanity's fear of nuclear weapons and of AI.

Chapter 7 gave the reason. For every other complex system, the data to be collected is not ourselves; for the system of human civilization, the data to be collected is the information of every human intelligent agent about itself, and whether people are willing to provide it is the bottleneck. This is why humanity's ability to understand, predict and technically transform nature runs so far ahead of its ability to understand, predict and control its own social system.

If human civilization's ability to manipulate the external world does not match its ability to control itself, sooner or later it will do something self-harming or even self-destroying. It is like a child of immense strength but a fragile body, wildly swinging a thousand-pound chain flail: sooner or later it hits itself. In little more than a century humanity has already been through two world wars, the Cold War between America and Russia, the nuclear crisis, and now stands in the middle of the confrontation between China and America and the AI race, each side afraid that whoever masters AI first will destroy the other, each straining to get ahead, exactly like the nuclear arms race of old.

Technology can help and can harm, and there are two root causes: one is the reliability of the technology itself, the other is the reliability of the human civilizational entity that holds and uses it. The fact that humanity's knowledge of nature far exceeds its knowledge of itself makes the second factor the greatest risk and the weakest link. If we follow the current trend, pouring energy and resources into the capability and reliability of technology instead of reforming the system of human society and raising the capability and reliability of civilization itself, the gap between the two will keep widening. The final result is climbing a tree to catch fish, driving south to reach the north: going further and further down the road of technology out of control and self-destruction, until there is no way back.

In fact we already have, or are about to have, every objective capability needed to collect the information of the human social system: storage and compute, AI that changes by the day, brain-computer interfaces in their infancy, all evolving at tremendous speed. So the question of how to solve humanity's subjective willingness to provide information about itself has become the bottleneck of the whole process of development. The new mechanism is exactly this: eliminate the power gap, strengthen cooperation, reduce confrontation, limit the abuse of power and the occurrence of harm at the level of mechanism, guarantee that the information people provide will not be used to hurt them, let people get a positive return from sharing, and ensure that this positive feedback runs reliably and continuously, thereby solving the bottleneck at its root. Then we can do a far better job of studying the complex system of human civilization itself, build more accurate theories and more effective social systems, and make the interior of the human civilizational entity like the interior of a higher organism, where all the cells cooperate through complex and reliable mechanisms, giving the organism a stronger ability to survive and a stronger ability to perceive and interact with the outside world. All human individuals, with the help of computers and AI, obtain sufficient information, reason and decide by reliable causal relations, and together build a powerful distributed dynamic-weight intelligent neural network system that serves as the brain of the human civilizational entity and steers this entity's evolution and development in the universe.

### A single central nervous center and internal diversity

Some say that "planetary intelligence" is only a metaphor, and better read as multi-scale cooperation than as a single central nervous center. I do not treat it as a metaphor. I treat it as a wholly new intelligent dissipative structure that emerges in the course of the universe's evolution, more complex, higher-level and larger in scale than any single intelligent organism. And a single central nervous center is the inevitable result of evolution. A stable dissipative structure, above all an intelligent agent, must, along the direction of time, keep its interactions with the external environment stable and consistent in rational logic. Especially when its internal structure has a sufficiently stable perception of the external environment, can obtain enough information, and is capable of making the best choice, it must have a stable, reliable and consistent capacity for outward decision and action. Only a single central nervous center can accomplish this kind of stable, complex perception and response. Animals with distributed nervous systems, like jellyfish, can only manage simple interactions with the outside; the more complex the intelligent organism, the more complex its central nervous system and brain. Of course, simple centralization alone is not enough. It must be matched with a central structure complex enough, with enough neurons, with input and output systems complex enough, so that energy and information travel through this system quickly and accurately, and computation and reasoning come out right.

What happens without logical consistency in stable long-term behavior? Look at how China's authoritarian system and the Western democratic systems have developed over these decades of the information age. Once the ability to acquire, transmit, compute and analyze the basic information needed for decisions had risen enough to yield relatively accurate national economic policy, an economic system with a single central nervous center, long-term planning and strong regulation of the market pulled far ahead, in science, in industry and in the information industry, of countries run by rotating parties chosen by democratic vote, with fractured public opinion, parties undercutting one another, every incoming party chasing short-term results, policies reversed overnight, and national decisions hijacked by economic oligarchs each fighting for themselves. From the energy industry to electric vehicles to robots, in the unified and powerful capacity for policy execution and market building that it takes to land a technology fast and form a complete industrial chain, China leads by a wide margin. Now imagine that Earth's civilization has evolved into an interstellar civilization, yet lacks the unified, coordinated, logical and planned capacity for information collection and decision-making, both outward and inward, and keeps thrashing back and forth like a schizophrenic patient or Mr. Hyde, circling and zigzagging on the road of evolution. Such a planetary intelligence would clearly be a sick, inefficient, low-grade intelligence.

I am certainly not saying that China's static, single-authority decision system is the optimal solution. The optimal solution I envision is an intelligent decision system of dynamic feedback and computed weights, whose intelligent nodes are like the neurons in a brain; the system is essentially the weight-computing system of a large AI model, organized by the same principle at a more complex level. Large models are intelligent because their architecture is built on a central system of probability maximization. "Central" does not mean a structure in which one node decides everything and a single point of failure paralyzes the whole system. It means a system in which many nodes integrate dynamically, obtain accurate external information, and produce unified, stable decisions and behavior. And unified, stable behavior toward the outside does not mean uniformity inside. The stable unity of a human organism's outward behavior does nothing to diminish the diversity and complexity within it.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The brain's dynamic weights work because there is a massive, fast stream of prediction-error signals. Most social decisions lack exactly this fast feedback. Without feedback on outcomes, dynamic weights only add noise.**

A: This is exactly why this book puts the record before the decision. The collection and permission system of Chapter 14, together with the rule from Chapter 7 that failure means compensation, correction and a record, exists precisely to manufacture prediction-error signals for social decisions. Under the old system, social decisions lack fast feedback not because the feedback does not physically exist, but because the pyramid mutes it: bad news that travels upward hurts the messenger, so it does not travel; errors the decision-maker knows about but cannot admit must be actively covered up. A brain that can mute the pain signals it dislikes will walk on a broken leg. When the record does not depend on any single node, feedback can no longer be muted, and dynamic weights receive the error signals they need for the first time. Use it first in the fields where feedback is fast enough; record first in the fields where feedback is slow. That is a sequence, not an obstacle.
