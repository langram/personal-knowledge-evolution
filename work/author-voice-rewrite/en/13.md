## Chapter 13: FPM, Typed Uncertainty, and an Evolution Space That Can Be Learned

### The vocabulary of large models mixes thousands of years of semantics

In today's large language models, the token set and the semantic vector space built from it mix together semantic units from every stage of human civilization's evolution: some are primitive words from thousands of years ago with vague meanings, some are the precise concepts of modern science, some are the most basic seed vocabulary, some are high-frequency everyday words, and there is a great deal of specific knowledge that ought to be looked up in an external knowledge base but, because it appears repeatedly in the training text, has become the model's internal knowledge, occupying precious parameter resources.

In any domain, the process of composing complex semantic objects from basic semantic objects, as it grows in scale, becomes unsustainable through rising construction complexity if the basic semantics are even slightly vague or the rules of construction even slightly defective. Without a complete system of notation, relying only on the way humans recorded music early on, complex symphonies could not be constructed; without the formal descriptive languages of mathematics, physics and chemistry, relying only on natural-language words that came from early sensory experience, the vast knowledge systems of those fields could not be supported; after the computer was invented, programming languages were all precisely defined formal languages, or complex programs could not be built. So what I want to establish is a unified, general intelligent semantic knowledge system, built from strictly formalized standard semantic nodes through strict logical deduction.

The content identifiers in a Merkle-DAG can be layered and labeled from the bottom up according to their level of composition, their frequency of reuse, and the domain they belong to, that is, which subtree of the knowledge tree they sit in. Using this layering and labeling information, together with its mapping onto the large model's natural-language token set and internal vector space, one can compute in reverse the layering and domain membership of the model's internal knowledge, and thus distill and prune it on demand, customizing models for different needs. In the long run, let the model built from Merkle content identifiers become the general model with standard semantics built into the agent; I call it FPM. Then on top of it, customize the large language models of the cognitive front end for the human-machine interaction of different groups of people and different fields.

Then, in the whole system, the large models based on natural-language tokens and the programming languages that use natural-language words as keywords and identifiers are no longer the core. The entire complex knowledge system built from semantic nodes rests on the new architecture; natural language serves only as the human-machine interface, appearing as the presentation layer at the input and output ends, and not as an intermediate result, converting back and forth between binary data and code and natural-language representation through the many internal stages of the deterministic processes of interpretation, compilation and execution and the nondeterministic processes of reasoning and induction.

### Distance between hashes is not distance between meanings

Treating hashes as tokens has a problem: objects that are close in semantic space may be far apart in the numeric space of hashes; and as objects multiply, the tokens explode. Pure hash tokens give unsatisfactory results; and doing a full structural encoding for every object makes the code construction and computation load far too great.

The solution is to introduce a mechanism like the namespaces of programming languages: define a namespace hash token for each piece of hardware, software platform, programming language, software subsystem, program library, program module and function, and let these tokens form the data set of the Merkle tree. The construction of programs is bottom-up, from simple to complex; the construction of namespaces is the reverse, top-down. The more macroscopic the category, the more basic the node: hardware platforms x86 and ARM, operating systems Windows, Linux, macOS, iOS, Android, programming languages C, Java, Python, graphics interfaces DirectX and Vulkan, game engines Unity and Unreal. The finer the software module or function, the more complex and higher-level the node, and its namespace is constrained by the tokens of its sub-nodes; a sub-function in some game, for instance, has its namespace constrained by the more basic subspaces of game name, engine, graphics interface, operating system and hardware platform. In this way, from both the bottom-up and the top-down directions, a more constrained and more precise definition of semantic distance can be established for the distance between hash tokens.

My experiments in the Structoken project showed that, in a small model trained with typed Merkle-DAG nodes as tokens, static probes could not detect a complete graph topology stored in the model's hidden states, yet the model's dynamic changes during layer-by-layer updates matched the message passing of graph neural networks closely. In other words, the model does not need to permanently store the graph structure at every layer; it only needs, at each update, to be able to read information along the graph's topology. And when the tokens themselves are content-addressed DAG nodes, the addressing obstacle to that reading is cleared away, and attention can collapse at very low cost into the graph's adjacency matrix. Structure is not stored; it is computed. This also confirms the judgment of Chapter 4: intelligence is the process of decomposing high-order complex structures into low-order components and then restoring and emerging them through pushouts and pullbacks.

### Uncertainty can have types too

The current software ecosystem sits at two extremes when handling objects. At one extreme are problems whose uncertain variables themselves have precise, fixed definitions; this deterministic uncertainty is solved deterministically by programs written in programming languages, passing state parameters through branches, loops and other structures. At the other extreme are all the other problems that traditional programs cannot solve, which get described wholesale in natural language and handed to a large model with tens of thousands of tokens of context and tens or hundreds of billions of parameters. The trouble is that the uncertainty of many problems actually lies in the middle: neither as simple as a conditional database query nor as complex as natural-language understanding.

This uncertainty is typed. It can be described by the composite structure of the attributes of different objects; the uncertainty of those attributes can in turn be described recursively by the composite structure of the attributes of simpler sub-objects; and this structure can likewise be described with a Merkle-DAG. The uncertainty of any complex object can be estimated by composing the uncertainties of the sub-objects in its composite structure, just as a program's benchmark and profiling are estimated through the Merkle-DAG: by recording the uncertainty of nodes, estimate the probability interval of the uncertainty when nodes combine into complex structures. With this data, different means can be adopted according to the object's uncertainty characteristics: treat uncertainty as a type, provide quantified estimates of uncertainty for different types, and dynamically adjust the model's embeddings and attention-head settings according to these metrics. This is a more unified, more elegant, more fundamental means of providing dynamic solutions for different scales of uncertainty between deterministic programs and large models.

### Code that was once written and code that ought to be constructed

The training objective of today's large models mistakes "code that was once written" for "code that ought to be constructed". What the model learns is the distribution of code text, not the energy function of program construction. The problem is not only that public repositories are full of bad code; it is that the excellent code, the code that ought to be constructed, is certainly also in the training sources and embedded in the model's vector space, but because it is scarce, it does not rank at the top of the output probability list.

So the model must be trained to play a more fundamental game of construction: under constraints, find the constructive fixed point that passes verification, costs less, takes the shorter path and is more reusable. This is exactly the evaluation function of Chapter 3: construct the greatest external connection at the least internal cost. In constructing hot paths, always prefer the approaches that satisfy this rule, so that "code that ought to be constructed" overrides "code that was once written". The negative result recorded in Chapter 12 was one run of this evaluation function: the conversion preserved the result but cost more, so the original implementation was kept.

### Let the model learn how theories come into being

Why are large models good at solving problems and poor at creating theories? After discussing this with several AIs I reached a few points of consensus. First, the lack of a clear reward function: current training uses problems with definite answers, the reward is getting it right, and theoretical innovation appears to have no definite correct answer. Second, the sources of learning are mostly just semantic descriptions of theories already formed; data on the process of a theory's formation is missing, so the model can only learn the static semantic structure of a theory and knows nothing of its dynamic evolutionary path. Third, a mathematics problem is a search for a path in a computable, static semantic space, whereas creating a theory is creating a path in a dynamic, evolving semantic space that is essentially non-computable.

Here is what I think.

First, the reward function exists. Its ultimate answer is essentially non-computable, but the function itself can be progressively approximated toward the optimum along the direction of evolution. Occam's razor, Newton's four rules, Kolmogorov complexity: these are the names of this reward function in different historical periods. In the age of large models, perhaps we can compute the vector-space complexity of different theories when expressing the same semantics, and take the minimization of complexity as the reward function.

Second, since the training sources are deficient, fill the gap. Traditional training texts and their annotations all attend to the associations and paths among nodes in static semantic space, say the association between a question and an answer; this is more like pulling one version of the code out of a git repository and inducing its semantic structure by learning from it. Can we add to these training sources their node associations and paths in evolution space, like the complete modification history in git, except not a history based on plain-text diffs but an evolutionary history based on Merkle-DAG semantic nodes? Even the token for one and the same mathematical concept, say the limit, has undergone change in the course of the evolution of mathematical knowledge. This idea is not limited to mathematics; every human knowledge space can be annotated the same way: add to the training text its coordinates in dynamic evolution space, compute the distances between texts in evolution space, and examine the paths that theoretical evolution has taken. The distance in evolution space I am speaking of is not necessarily distance in physical space-time: in early civilization, limited by the means of communication, the distance of theoretical evolution was governed by geographical distance and time intervals, and collaboration between scientists was confined to the distance at which they could meet; later it became the distance of the circulation of books and letters; in the information age it is determined by distance in cyberspace. The Merkle-DAG framework can annotate all existing and ongoing information, code being written, data being created, ideas being proposed, with this evolution-space labeling, and can also attempt to label the notes and books of pre-information-age scholars with the time and place of their creation. Training a model on this foundation may let the model learn the experience and the laws of theoretical evolution.

Third, although mathematical problems and mathematical theories, and by extension all scientific problems and scientific theories, differ in the way described in the third point above, considering that the human brain uses one and the same mechanism to deal with both classes of problems, I have ample reason to believe that the training and inference mechanisms of today's large models can also deal with both.

### One space, two directions

So each person's knowledge system has two interconnected directions: semantic space, a tree-like structure resembling a mind map in which nodes are arranged by logic and subordination; and evolution space, a timeline that, like git, records the shifts, inheritance and iteration of ideas in the chronological order of utterances and sessions. Together they are the space-time record of a person's development of knowledge. Beneath the tree directory there must be a graph, because the same function may belong to several domains at once, and forcing a single membership would rebuild the walls of classification. The original words, the distillation and the judgment must all be kept, and kept separately: an AI's reply keeps the AI's identity and is not automatically converted into the author's view; an editor's explanation and whether the author accepted it are recorded separately. The purpose of recording is to let ideas keep growing.

This is not a small trick of knowledge management. On one side, annotate all the knowledge and information in humanity's digital space with its static semantic space and its dynamic evolution space, and on the basis of everyone's theoretical consensus, train a common knowledge model foundation recognized by all of humanity. On the other side, record everything in each person's digital space through Merkle-DAG, annotate it with that person's cognitive and knowledge evolution space, and use it as post-training data to fine-tune the weights of the common foundation on the basis of personal information, preferences and cognition. That model is the rational intelligence of Chapter 9, the one that represents this person's personal interests and opinions. As the user keeps learning and taking part in decisions in digital space, the continually revised data of cognitive evolution keeps fine-tuning the model through ongoing post-training, and the model in turn influences the decisions that follow.

Today's large models from the various companies are each an enormous, independent silo of data and code, and the enormous value of their intermediate results is sealed inside the silo and cannot be released. This framework should penetrate into the interior of model training: let different models share this internal information, learn from one another's strengths, and let an excellent iterative-evolutionary property be rapidly applied to other models. If the universe is regarded as one enormous complex system, the minimum number of parameters describing the states and trajectories of all the objects in it depends on the effective dimension of a high-dimensional space defined by an optimal set of mutually orthogonal basis vectors; the internal vector spaces of today's large models, trained on natural-language text, are highly redundant and wasteful. Through collaboration between AI and humans, we can iterate step by step to raise the models' compression rate, accuracy and speed. The feedback-evolution loop of an AI model's learning, training and inference practice is itself a digitized complex dynamic system that keeps iterating and evolving, and Merkle-DAG can equally be applied to this system.
