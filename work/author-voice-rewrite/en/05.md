## Chapter 5: Relativity: Cognitive Reference Frames and the Foundation of Social Science

### Reference frames from physics to cognition

Is a reference frame a mathematical concept or a physical one? It is mathematical first: a set of coordinate axes, an origin, a metric. But physics tells us that all reference frames are relative, and no reference frame is entitled to call itself absolutely at rest. Galilean relativity says the laws of mechanics are the same in all inertial frames; Einstein extended it to all physical laws and pointed out that simultaneity itself depends on the reference frame.

This concept can be extended all the way down the line. In chemistry, the direction and rate of a reaction depend on "reference conditions" such as temperature and pressure, and the same reaction has a different equilibrium position under different conditions. In biology, the environment perceived by every cell and every individual is relative to its own receptors and metabolic state; the bat's world is a world of echoes, the dog's world a world of smells. In these fields relativity holds just the same: the result of observation depends on the state of the observer, but between different observers there exist laws of mutual conversion.

So why is it that when we reach the social domain, especially the domains of morality, politics and ideology, everyone becomes self-centered, takes their own cognitive coordinate system as the standard of absolute correctness, and treats everything inconsistent with themselves as wrong?

My answer: this self-centered reference frame of humans is a basic property of any evolvable, emergent system at every level of its evolution. From the four-dimensional spacetime born of the Big Bang, to the perception of a biological individual, to human cognition, the fixed point at every level observes the world from itself; the previous chapter showed that distinguishing "me" from "not me" is the first classification by which an observer comes to exist. Today's large AI language models can be seen as a high-dimensional semantic space of human cognition. Language and action based on this cognitive space are like motion in physical spacetime, except that each person's cognitive-space reference frame is different, and this is why trust and estrangement, cooperation and betrayal arise between people; and the interactions among human individuals in these different reference frames form more complex structures and properties at the higher level of civilization and society.

### A social science version of relativity

So, after the fixed point principle and the principle of minimization and maximization, the mathematical principles part of this book adds one more: the principle of relativity.

Its content is this: among all human worldviews, epistemologies, political leanings, moral conceptions and cognitive systems, the differences in position, inclination and motion along every cognitive and behavioral dimension at the level of semantic space are relative. There is no absolute right and wrong, no absolute good and bad. When one person thinks another is "wrong", it is like a passenger on one train thinking that another train is moving backward: he is describing the relative motion between two reference frames, not an absolute property of either side.

This principle does not abolish facts. The relative velocity of two trains is an objective, measurable quantity, and so is the difference between two people's cognitive coordinates. What it abolishes is the entitlement to treat one's own coordinate origin as the center of the universe. The goal of social science is to find the laws of conversion between different cognitive reference frames, just as the Lorentz transformation connects the measurements of different inertial frames. The reason today's various social theories are incompatible with one another is precisely that each sits on its own train and this transformation does not yet exist.

### Refusing mystification, sanctification and demonization

In literature, in poetry and fiction, you may emotionalize, sanctify and demonize the objects of nature: the gentle wind, the yielding grass, the holy light, the demonic darkness. But such expressions must be kept out of theoretical research and technical practice in natural science. The objects of human society need to be treated the same way: please do not use expressions of this kind in social science research or in the political practice of social games. Every cognitive and behavioral phenomenon at the social level of humanity is, like a natural phenomenon, an objective phenomenon that can be observed, studied, quantified, recorded, analyzed and modeled, and we refuse to mystify, sanctify, demonize or emotionalize them.

This leads to something I call meta-cognition, which is the psychological premise for everything argued in Part II of this book:

All the differences and conflicts among humans, between individual and individual, individual and group, group and group, in appearance, physiology, personality, cognition, language, behavior, needs and understanding, these differences at the higher level, are structural differences at various lower levels that have gradually evolved over the long course of evolution and in the game of human society. Every one of these differences can be given a reproducible causal-chain explanation built from scientific theory. People should not be driven by emotions such as the fear produced by ignorance and the anger produced by confrontation. The so-called fierce binary oppositions of angels and demons, justice and evil, are in fact nothing more than extreme irrational emotions, in the manner of moral and religious judgment, produced when the forward and reverse chain reactions of the tit-for-tat strategy in a long social game catalyze, color and amplify them.

By "lower-order structural differences" I do not mean attributing a higher-order conflict to some single clean lower-order source. A property like violence does not exist at the biological level. It is like the common saying that red is joyful, green is peaceful and blue is melancholy, but that does not mean a light wave in some range of wavelengths has the property of joy. The violent properties of a person at the psychological and social levels are not caused by any single factor; they are the product of many factors compounded together, genetic inheritance, living environment and so on. The properties of any lower-order object are not good or bad; they are merely different characteristics, and if they are constructed well, an optimal combination can be achieved. If we understand the mechanism, we can combine these factors correctly and optimize the properties of the higher-order object to the maximum. Reducing plague to pathogens, reducing epilepsy to neuronal discharges: reductions of this kind eliminated a class of narratives that moralized and ontologized human differences, and they historically reduced actual conflict. What I want to do is extend this to the whole of social conflict.

### The table of correspondences

Mapping the concepts of mathematics and basic physics onto the concepts of social science is the basic method of this book. Here is the table of correspondences; the later chapters develop each entry in turn:

- Tensors, vectors, dimensions: the position and direction of an individual's cognition in semantic space; the multiple influencing factors of a social structure.
- Objects, morphisms, categories: people, relationships between people, social structures.
- Duality: active and passive, rights and obligations, freedom and constraint, individual and environment, each pair being two faces of one coupled system.
- Symmetry: equality; symmetry breaking is the starting point of division of labor, differentiation and hierarchy.
- Relativity: cognitive reference frames.
- Fixed point: stable social structures, Nash equilibrium, institutions, alignment.
- Minimization: the lowest-cost way of resolving conflict, Pareto optimality, cutting costs and raising efficiency.
- Maximization: the maximization of relationships and cooperation, freedom, the maximization of interests.
- Duality of aspect: knowledge and intelligence, data and code, static structure and dynamic process.

The pair active and passive deserves an extra sentence: your active behavior causes someone else's passive reaction, and your passive reaction is caused by someone else's active behavior. Version A, where the individual defines its own objective function, and version B, where the outside designs the objective function for the individual: the freedom and constraint, the power and obligation between these two are likewise dialectically unified dual concepts. So my version is neither A nor B, but an objective function in which the individual and the external environment are mutually related and mutually coupled, and it is not a static evaluation function but a dynamic game process that keeps changing at run time. This point will be made concrete in Chapter 9 when discussing decision weights: at every moment of evolution the individual must retain the actual ability to change the direction of its trajectory, otherwise "coupling" becomes a rhetoric for domination.

### Alignment is a kind of fixed point

Later in this book I will use a concept: alignment. Let me first give it a mathematical position here, but only a position, not a definition. My intuition is that alignment too should be studied from the angle of fixed points, as the fixed point to which the process of updating the parties' strategies and institutions in an evolutionary game converges. That intuition has not been developed. One thing can be seen already: a fixed point by itself does not tell good from bad. Mutual defection in the prisoner's dilemma is also a stable state that no party wants to change unilaterally, and it is exactly what this book wants to escape; so "any unilateral change would only make that party worse off" describes stability, not alignment. Which kind of fixed point counts as alignment is a question left to Chapter 17, and there too it can only be left open. Alignment can be between people, between humans and AI, or between a civilization and its ecology.

### From cognitive reference frames to joint construction

Acknowledging relativity does not mean nothing can be said. Acknowledging that two trains each have their own reference frame does not stop us from calculating precisely when they will collide. Acknowledging that each person's cognitive coordinates have their own conditions of formation does not stop us from jointly verifying facts and jointly setting rules of cooperation. Quite the opposite: only once relativity is acknowledged can the argument move from "whose coordinates are absolute" to "how do our coordinates convert into one another", and only then can moral judgments be reduced to facts that can be discussed and rules that can be revised. What Part II does is to construct society, step by step, on this foundation.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Does saying "there is no absolute right or wrong, good or bad" abolish responsibility?**

A: No. The principle of relativity abolishes the entitlement to treat one's personal coordinates as absolute coordinates; it does not abolish facts, still less harm. That a person considers something justified within his own reference frame does not change the loss that thing causes in another reference frame, nor does it change the measurable relative relationship between the two. Chapter 7 will show that under a verifiable, traceable framework of common knowledge, the assignment of responsibility is actually more precise than under a framework of moral judgment: what gets recorded is this act, this decision, this loss, rather than a label of "evil" stuck on a person.

**Q: You treat the phenomena of human society as objects of study just like natural phenomena, but social systems have a peculiarity: the participants can understand the laws of the system and change their behavior accordingly, and a law changes as soon as it is known. This is unlike physics.**

A: It is indeed unlike physics, but it is not an obstacle to unification; it is the content of unification. Reflexivity is itself a feedback loop, and feedback loops are exactly what can be incorporated into a dynamical description. Chapter 1 said that a self-referential fixed point is a point of emergence; a social system that can understand its own laws and change itself is precisely a system with the capacity for emergence. The social science this book sets out to build is not "objective laws independent of the participants' cognition" but "meta-laws that still hold under cognitive reflexivity". Fixed points, minimization and maximization, and relativity are meta-laws of exactly this kind.
