## Chapter 8: Two Kinds of Resources and Two Kinds of Distribution

### The interval of optimal marginal utility

Before continuing with the plan for remaking civilization, that digital, dynamically evolving complex system, I want to put forward a few observations.

The Industrial Revolution, by gradually replacing human physical labor with automated equipment, raised productivity enormously, and the abundance of material goods brought the basic material and spiritual needs of individual human survival to a state of basic satisfaction. As AI develops, intellectual labor will achieve higher efficiency too, and in the not distant future, every material and spiritual need of individual human survival can be supplied in full within the range of optimal marginal utility.

I mention optimal marginal utility because while too little supply is harmful to a living individual, too much supply is harmful as well. The water, food and nutritional balance a person needs to survive all fall within a range; the external information the brain takes in, the intensity and duration of thinking, the quality and quantity of outward communication through speech and writing, all have an optimal interval too. Seen at a more abstract level, every person is a dissipative structure, dissipating energy and resources with the other objects of the outside world. Excessive distribution does not bring better results: fine food is good, but letting go of control and taking in too many calories damages health; however good novels, films and games may be, drowning in them every day is certainly not good either. The biological instinct of a human being exists for survival; it drives him to take in as much food as possible and store it, but in today's world of enormous material abundance, if physiological instinct is left in charge, the great majority of people just eat and get fatter. Instinct does not adjust with age: when you are young and your metabolism is high you can eat that much and stay healthy; eat by instinct when you are older and the result is excess calories and rising inflammation. In fact the best way to live long is to restrict calories. The same holds at the spiritual level: information and spiritual resources are now so vast that many people are instead trapped in the ocean of information, staring at short videos on their phones all day. Driven entirely by instinct, controlled by physiology and emotion, a person usually cannot make the best choices about material intake or spiritual intake. Many people with restraint and self-discipline are more successful in life and career precisely because they are driven by reason rather than by primitive instinct.

So when productivity is strong enough to produce sufficient material and spiritual wealth, distribution in appropriate measure according to each individual's different needs and in keeping with his psychological and physiological health is clearly the more reasonable and fairer outcome.

### Where power-law distribution comes from

But in the present system of human civilization, once individuals and states gain power in the social, economic, political and even military fields through free competition or through power games and violent conquest, the inequality of power produces an inequality in the distribution of gains from the cooperative and competitive game; add the repetition of the game over and over, and the gains of the side advantaged by that inequality produce a compounding effect, finally forming a power-law distribution of power, wealth and resources. A power-law distribution does not grow out of human needs and abilities; it is the product of balance-breaking unequal behavior run through a long chain reaction. The inequality in any single round of the game is small; compounding stretches it into the long tail.

This power-law distribution does have a cause. For a group, wealth and resources serve two purposes: one part supports the survival of each individual in the group and satisfies those individuals' material and spiritual needs; the other part is consumed by the group's survival and operation as a whole. But how the group operates, how it distributes and consumes this wealth and these resources, needs an intellectual center to take charge. And in the traditional system, whether the authoritarian dictatorship of one political form or the alternation of parties in a democracy, the structure of government, and likewise the power structure of a commercial company, is in essence a pyramid, a hierarchy with a power gradient. The inequality of power between levels, and the compounding effect after repeated games, cause both power and wealth to take on the long-tailed distribution of a power law. The political and economic elites standing at the apex of the pyramid, by virtue of their advantage in professional ability and the power and capital advantage accumulated through long games, gain the decision-making power over the operation of the whole group.

It must be admitted that in this situation the decision-making leader who wins repeated competition is an individual of relatively greater ability and a higher probability of deciding correctly. Having him lead the group plainly works better than the lottery and the equal vote of early human organization. The pyramid did not come from nowhere; under the conditions of limited human capacity for information processing, communication and coordination, it is a solution that works. The question is: is there a better system of group organization and decision-making, with a higher probability of correct decisions? In the age of computers and AI, there is. It is the dynamic weights of Chapter 9.

### Two kinds of resources, two kinds of distribution

So the principle of distribution in the new architecture is this: at the level of individual need, wealth and resources are distributed according to need, fairly, healthily and reasonably, and this part relates only to the individual's own physiological and psychological structure, not to his position in the game; at the collective level, wealth and resources are distributed and decided by the collective's dynamic-weight system. In power, the aim is a decision system of equal, dynamically weighted power like that among the billions of neurons in the brain.

On one important aspect of this division, China's system does better than the West. Take one important resource as the example: land. In China, land is owned by the state; in the West it belongs to individuals. But land is the most fundamental resource for a civilization's infrastructure and economic development. Airports, stations, roads, high-speed rail, energy, minerals, factories, urban planning are the equivalent of a civilization-organism's blood circulation, respiratory system and metabolism, and none of them can do without the expropriation and planning of land. Do you know why Chinese products have such a price advantage? Besides lower labor costs, state ownership of land cuts out the wrangling and haggling over private land expropriation, the demands for high prices, the drawn-out attrition, or the flat refusal to sell, which is one of the major reasons the time and capital costs of running an entire industrial chain stay stubbornly high.

Of course I am not saying China's system is the best system. Because although it separates the two kinds of capital better, first, the distribution of the first kind of resource to individuals is still the unequal distribution produced by unequal power in the game of the pyramid power structure under the traditional political authoritarian system and the economic capitalist system; on this point there is no essential difference between China and the West. Second, the power to control the second kind of capital is still the static, power-gapped, pyramid-style decision-making, not a dynamic-weight decision system like the neurons of the brain. So although its decision-making efficiency is far better than the United States' two-party alternation, with its swinging from left to right, policies made only to court votes, and inability to form long-term plans, it is still not the optimal solution, and it is still trapped in the stubborn chronic diseases of graft, corruption and bureaucracy inherent in the pyramid structure of the unequal-power game.

### How machines level the gaps in ability

Completely eliminating inequality used to be an impossible task. Everyone differs in age, intelligence, physical strength and ability, lives in a different geographical environment, has different friends and relatives around him. A strong adult man is plainly far superior in physical labor to a small, frail elderly woman; a person raised from childhood in a scholarly family plainly has the advantage in mental work over a farmer who deals with crops all day.

But since the Industrial Revolution, science and technology have kept strengthening every human ability and making up every shortfall. Whenever we invent a tool that replaces some human ability, whose performance exceeds the human one, and whose output is large enough for everyone to have one, we can say the gap between people in that ability has been leveled. Differences in physical strength made farmers unequal in farming ability, but once agriculture was fully mechanized, the gap between farmers in farming ability was erased. The spread of navigation software eliminated differences in people's ability to find their way; the spread of self-driving will erase differences in driving ability. People's physiological differences in ability are being erased bit by bit. When AI surpasses humans in intelligence and becomes so widespread that everyone can access it freely, people's differences in intelligence will be eliminated too.

But there is one precondition for this: all the knowledge needed for the principles and the manufacturing process of every tool that exceeds human capacity must be public knowledge shared by all humanity, not privately owned by any person, organization, company or state. Once it is privately owned, it is no longer a tool for erasing the gaps in ability across humanity; it becomes a tool by which some individual or organization strengthens its own ability and gains a greater advantage in competition and confrontation with other players. To remove this hidden danger, the possibility of such knowledge being monopolized must be ruled out at the root. That is the problem that the language of Part III is meant to solve.

### Equality does not require everyone to become the same person

People's abilities differ. That is a fact, and there is no need to dodge it. Innate genetic differences even affect whether a person shifts preferences easily, whether he can keep investing effort in one direction, so that some people are narrow but extremely accomplished, while others know something of everything but lack depth in any one direction. So what matters is not making ability approach some particular distribution, but making the system's mechanism able to let individuals of these different abilities build the most effective cooperation, let specialists and generalists communicate effectively, and complete deep cross-disciplinary cooperation.

But a difference in ability and a difference in domination are two different things. The old system binds them together: a person is good at something, so he gets more resources; the resources buy more people's time and more opportunities to train; so his ability and his power over others compound together. In the process by which a human individual trains and acquires ability, compounding factors such as resources and capital play a large part, and the more the training of an ability depends on external resources, the more these factors widen the gap. Among sports, the running and jumping events that rely more on biological instinct leave little to buy beyond talent; the aristocratic events that require more capital and resources, golf, skydiving, equestrian sport, tie results ever more tightly to family resources.

A rational social system should aim at two goals. On one side, make external factors such as opportunities for training, equipment and basic living security fairer, eliminate the influence of external compounding factors, and let the distribution of people's abilities depend more on the two factors internal to the individual, innate genetic difference and acquired preference; differences in ability then arise from innate differences in ability plus the time and repetitions invested afterward. On the other side, let everyone have sufficient information about himself and the outside world, know his own strengths, and have sufficient free time and freedom to develop the abilities he prefers. The time an individual can spend on learning and practice is limited, while holding capital lets one person continuously mobilize the time and resources of many. If training conditions, disposable time and access to tools are fairly guaranteed, these two kinds of accumulation are no longer bound tightly together as they are in the old mechanism: a person's being good at something can help him do the task better, but it need not automatically give him more power to control other people's opportunities to grow.

In this way, although everyone's innate genes differ and their strengths differ, with everyone's available time the same and each taught and developed according to his own strengths, the differences in ability in different directions will be large, while the probability of the system coming to be dominated by particular individuals will be far smaller than under the old system. The symmetry of the carbon atom's abilities does not limit the diversity of organic compounds; the identity of the DNA in every cell does not limit the diversity of cell functions; in the same way, equality of ability and power among human and AI individuals does not mean uniformity of function. On the contrary, it is exactly this equality that truly releases the functional diversity of every individual.

Cross-disciplinary cooperation needs two equally important functions: going deep into a problem, investing continuously in one field, mastering the details, and discovering the difficulties that general discussion easily overlooks; and connecting different fields, understanding each side's problems, conditions and ways of expressing themselves, discovering connections, and breaking the overall goal into tasks that can be worked on cooperatively. These are different roles within a task; the same person can take different roles in different situations, and there is no need to fix people permanently into specialists and generalists. In developing an energy storage device, the materials researcher attends to material properties, the engineer to structure and operation, the manufacturer to production conditions, the user to cost and actual need; the key is to let these conditions pass between them. The accumulation of individual ability thereby becomes a source of growth in common ability, without having to turn into power over others.

### Equality in the sense of the rules

The equality the new system guarantees is equality in the sense of the rules, not equality of outcomes. Video games offer a ready comparison. A well-designed multiplayer game, whether strategy, role-playing or adventure, guarantees by its rules, from the moment a player creates an account and logs in, that all players are equal in rules and in starting resources: the same starting point, the same rules, and every difference afterward comes from what the player did in the game. Pay-to-win online games are the opposite: paying players bring their own starting wealth, VIP players hold special privileges, the rules themselves are unequal, and so the rankings in the game reflect not play but the wallet outside the game. Players' attitudes toward these two kinds of game are exactly people's attitudes toward the two kinds of institution: in the first, you lose and accept it; in the second, you win and nobody respects it.

The game of society, today, is a pay-to-win game. What the new system has to do is turn it into the first kind: when each person enters, the first kind of resource is distributed according to need, decision weight in every field starts from the same initial value, the rules are common knowledge, and every difference afterward can come only from his contributions and judgments in the game. This requirement rests on no assumption about the distribution of ability. It is only a rule.

This example brings something to mind. This chapter handles individual need and collective resources separately, and one thing in the old system sits exactly astride the two: inheritance. Where should the first kind of resource, distributed to a person according to need, go after he dies? If it can be inherited, then VIP paying players born with a silver spoon reappear in the social game under the new system, and the fairness of starting resources is broken from the second generation on. So I lean toward a new rule: under the new system, personal resources carry no right of inheritance; the first kind of resource held in a person's name returns after him to the collective pool, to be redistributed by the dynamic-weight system; what he leaves to his descendants is the whole of his record and contribution in the Merkle-DAG, honor, not starting wealth. This is of course only my own opinion about a future institution, not an authoritative statement of its rules; like every other rule, it has to be tested in the experiments of the free zones.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: However strong the ability of a few, they can never be a majority in numbers, but in the old system a few used wealth to control the time of the many. What does the new system rely on to guarantee that ability does not turn into the same kind of control?**

A: On the separation of the two kinds of resources. However strong a person's ability, the first kind of resource is distributed to him only according to his own need; he does not get an extra meal for being capable. His influence over the second kind of resource shows up only as decision weight in the fields he is good at, and weight attaches to concrete problems and adjusts with every feedback from results; it cannot accumulate into a stock of capital that can be taken off and used to mobilize other people's time. Wealth can compound because it can circulate independently, detached from the person who created it and the problem it solved; weight cannot. For a power law to grow it needs a channel through which advantage is exchanged for command over other people's time, and that channel is exactly what the new system cuts off by rule. This is not a prediction about distributions; it is a rule that can be checked: the moment anyone can exchange weight for the first kind of resource, or for weight in another field, the rule has been broken. The expansion process described in Chapter 20 has to check this at every step.
