# Part III: The Information-Science Foundation

## Chapter 11: Language: From Phonemes and Binary to Hash

### The evolutionary history of communication tools

The evolutionary history of humanity is, in a certain sense, an evolutionary history of communication tools. From the emergence of spoken and written language to the birth of computer languages, every deeper understanding of the basic building blocks of information has vastly increased the flexibility and combinatorial inventiveness of the systems built on top of it, and brought a leap in civilization.

From ideographic script to alphabetic script: by discovering a more basic unit, the phoneme, we invented the alphabet, and the capacity to construct vocabulary grew enormously. Language was no longer confined to a finite set of meaning-bearing symbols; it could freely combine an unlimited number of new words, to meet the need for naming new concepts during the knowledge explosion. From natural language to binary language: the 0 and 1 and the Boolean operations that computers use are a form with higher efficiency and higher capacity. This way of building a language is isomorphic to the basic structures of category theory, initial objects, terminal objects, limits, colimits, duality. It strips away all specific semantics and reaches straight to the essence of logical construction, which is why it can describe and simulate everything in the universe.

Draw a horizontal analogy between the human individual, spoken and written language, the system of thought and consciousness, and the system of social organization on one side, and the digitized computer-network system on the other: the human individual corresponds to the single computer, language and writing correspond to computer language, the individual's system of thought and consciousness corresponds to the single-machine operating system, and the system of social organization corresponds to the architecture of the internet. This correspondence is, in fact, the carbon-based ecosystem of biological intelligence using its own wisdom to create a more efficient silicon-based system of computer intelligence; and the union of carbon and silicon will unleash a far more powerful evolutionary force. What this Part sets out to do is design the new language that the fusion of these two kinds of intelligence requires, uniting human natural language and computer language in one framework, and then, on the basis of that language, design an entirely new operating system, software ecosystem and internetwork: the social neural network.

### Energy efficiency ratio: a method for comparing complex systems

First, a method. Written languages are complex systems with many advanced features. If you evaluate two such systems by comparing those advanced features one by one, you will very likely run into a situation where each has its own strengths and no judgment is possible; different angles of examination yield different standards, and the argument never ends. The other way is to start from first principles and take the underlying physical properties of the thing as the standard: for two systems with the same function, have them complete the same task, compare the output first, then compare the energy consumed in producing that output, and get the energy efficiency ratio. The one with the higher energy efficiency ratio wins.

Use this method to compare Chinese and English. Measure the output by information entropy: the statistic for a Chinese corpus is 4.24 bits, for English 4.14. Chinese has a slight edge, but they are in the same class, and that edge is largely owed to the great quantity of classical poetry and four-character idioms in the literary corpus; if you look only at informational corpora, Chinese drops to 4.04, below English. Widen the scope to all the languages of the world and information entropy clusters around 4 bits, with no language standing out. At the level of speech, a study published in Science in 2019 found that different languages all transmit information at about 39 bits per second: languages with high information density are spoken slowly, those with low density are spoken fast. Since the output is roughly the same, the energy cost alone settles the matter. We cannot directly measure the energy the brain spends processing language, but the computer, as an implementation of the Turing machine, simulates the pattern of human thinking, and we can assume that the energy consumption of the two is positively correlated when performing the same task. And the computing power, memory, storage and time required for Chinese natural language processing exceed those for English by several times to more than ten times. Under this assumption, on the metric of energy efficiency ratio, English beats Chinese by an order of magnitude.

I give this example not to belittle Chinese; Chapter 6 said that the ellipsis and cadence of Chinese are local advantages that the energy efficiency ratio cannot measure. I give it to demonstrate a method: from here on, whenever this book compares two institutions or two architectures, it uses the same ruler.

### A hash is both a name and an address

Now we can conceive an entirely new, unified symbolic framework.

The hash value obtained by running a hash algorithm over a string is unique with a high enough probability to serve as that string's identifier. The irreversibility of the hash makes it secure: it can detect any tampering with the original string. A digital signature is made over the hash, but the hash by itself only proves that the content has not changed; proving who published it still needs a signing key. Its uniqueness and uniform distribution allow fast indexing and lookup through a hash table, so it can also serve as the string's address. Through a distributed hash table, you get distributed storage, and the system's scale can be extended without limit. Through a mechanism like git's, storing the modification history as a tree of hash values, you get version management. Hashing is already the core technology of many pieces of software, but so far it has only been widely used in the domain of application software; it has not yet shown its power at the level of the computer language itself.

My proposal is this: design an entirely new programming language that uses hash values as the identifiers of all code and data objects in the language. A constant of a simple type is represented directly by the hash value of its string expression. A constant of a complex type is represented by a key-value structure in which both keys and values are hash values; the string of the whole object thus formed is the content, and its hash value is the identifier. A function header takes as its content the sequence of identifiers of the function's name, parameters and return value, and its hash value is its identifier. A function body takes as its content the function's string expression, and its hash value is its identifier. Because these identifiers are in hash form, they are guaranteed unique, so they can simultaneously serve as addresses for reading and writing: a single table mapping virtual addresses to physical addresses suffices for fast access. Because the hash value is both identifier and address, a distributed hash network can be used for decentralized deployment, storage and execution. The whole scheme resembles git's data storage format, with version management built in.

This language has three direct consequences.

First, uniqueness and address. Any concept, any piece of code, any datum, once its content is fixed, has a globally unique hash address, so a vast number of objects can be named without negotiation and in a distributed manner, with no collisions.

Second, in traditional text-based programming languages, cross-file function dependencies, cross-software dependencies, and the complicated operations of compiling and linking are all reduced to key-value lookups in a hash table.

Third, openness becomes a requirement for running. Once content is bound to its hash identifier, an identifier is valid only after the original content has been obtained and verified against the hash. This has to be stated precisely: the hash function itself forces no one to publish content; publishing a digest is not publishing the original, and ciphertext can be addressed too. The force comes from the ecosystem's rules of admission: any code or data of a public nature that wants to enter the public dependency graph, to be referenced, called and run by others, must make its content resolvable and verifiable, or else nothing that references it can pass verification, and nothing that references it can run. So "closed but public" becomes, in this ecosystem, a thing that cannot run. Apart from personal private data, this rule, at the lowest level of the language mechanism, eliminates the possibility of public knowledge being closed off and privatized.

The third point is precisely the solution to the premise set out in Chapter 8. The existing software and hardware ecosystem is not up to this task. Even if the computer language itself is open source, you cannot stop people from using it to build closed-source software. Even if a piece of software is free and open source, many open-source licenses do not forbid closing the source after modification. Even under the strictest GPL, you cannot stop someone from building closed-source software on top of its platform. And such licenses only forbid in the legal sense; they cannot do it in substance: when a powerful autocratic empire violates an open-source license, secretly develops a revolutionary closed-source product, and gains an enormous competitive advantage, other countries, lacking the strength to match it, cannot defeat that product by law. A language in which the hash is the name, together with this rule of admission, turns open source from a legal requirement into a running requirement: what does not publish its content cannot be referenced by others, and so cannot enter the public ecosystem.

### A container language

This language built on hash identifiers is a container language. It takes everything humanity has digitized or will digitize, all the data and code that computers and the internet have created or are about to create, and the encoders, decoders, operating systems, human-machine interfaces, databases and runtime libraries that read, write and run that data and code, and represents all of it by the hash value of its binary content as identifier and address, realizing one grand unified platform. Then a single table suffices, mapping these hash key-value pairs one to one onto the vocabulary and grammar of human natural language, building an index, and this language can handle every problem of computers and of humans.

A unified software ecosystem: name the functions and modules of software with unified hash identifiers, and escape the chaos in which one function is built by many organizations each fighting its own battle and reinventing the wheel. As JavaScript runtimes, there are already node, deno, bun and several other implementations, roughly identical in function, differing only in how certain sub-functions are implemented. Under the hash system, different implementations of the same sub-function each have a unique identifier, all the diverse branches are organized and managed under hash identifiers, and the optimization of a function is decoupled from the private interests of the individual programmer or team. Moreover, this unified organization of diversity makes it easy to measure the efficiency of different implementations by standard methods, optimizing the system more effectively through standardized benchmarks. As long as the interface is unchanged, you simply swap the old implementation module for the new one; for an upgrade that changes the interface, the unique and immutable nature of the hash value lets you quickly locate and modify every module that uses that interface.

Restructuring economic industry: bring every product in human economic life, from a single screw to a chip design, into this hash system. In the past, products were divided by trademark, brand and model, with assorted companies and organizations behind them; the evolution of products became entangled with human private desire, and monopoly, unfair competition and disorderly involution arose in the development of technology and the running of supply chains. Adopting an approach like the one in the software domain can accelerate technological progress just the same.

The entirely new software ecosystem, human-machine interface and internet architecture brought by this language will ultimately trigger a revolutionary change in the organizational form of human society. The thing that connects all computing resources, human resources and intellectual resources, on equal terms, into one enormous social neural network is built on top of it.

### Everything is a tree

All code and data, as semantic objects, are tree-structured in essence; it is only that this tree structure is organized differently in traditional storage systems. At the lowest level, inside a file, source code defines and organizes its tree structure through begin and end, brackets of all kinds, spaces and indentation; in executable files or data files, it is defined by the rules of a specific encoding format; on a single storage device, it is defined by the nesting hierarchy of directories; Windows even assigns drive letters to different storage devices, while Linux maps multiple disks to virtual directories under the device directory; on the network, it is defined by domain names, and inside a domain by subdomains or the virtual directories of a path. In essence they are all different representations of a semantic tree structure.

Moreover, whether those semantic structures should be addressed separately and independently, or packed into one file, or compressed into a smaller file, actually depends on the usage scenario and the calling pattern of the semantic object. During development, a large game keeps its many scattered resources as independent small files for quick modification, addition and deletion; once production is finished and the whole thing must be transferred and distributed at once, or read from external storage into memory at once at runtime, those nodes get packed into one large serializable object. In the past, humans decided when to scatter and when to pack according to their own preferences and inadequate investigation, and once decided it became a relatively fixed, static form. Different levels organize the tree by different rules: code by the grammar of the programming language, data by encoding rules, file systems by different directory systems; Windows and Linux do not even use the same path separator, and AI, when performing system tasks, often makes laughable errors over this that require rework. Across machines on a local network it becomes IP addresses, with naming systems that all differ; at the internet level, the naming system becomes DNS and assorted protocols; at the level of individual internet applications, the protocols differ yet again.

If you examine all these semantic objects, at every level from low to high, they are nothing but tree structures of code and data. With Merkle-DAG, all addressing methods and protocols become one: from cross-file object access, to cross-directory and cross-application access, to cross-host access on local networks and the internet, the content identifier obtained by hashing can be the name and at the same time the address, with no worry about files being tampered with, and with packing and unpacking done more automatically and more scientifically according to how and where the object is used. We have simply never taken this design idea as a global strategy and used it to thoroughly rebuild and cover the entire system; much of the earlier work applied it in local scenarios. What I intend to do is take this mechanism as a single mechanism and thoroughly reconstruct the whole system, so that the fragmented, mutually independent mechanisms for organizing tree structures are unified under the new framework.

### The diversity of naming is the diversity of people

The inconsistency of the same data and code object at the level of naming and presentation is not confined to this one domain. Different computer instruction sets: designers, out of personal preference, assign different binary instructions to the same hardware circuits. Different computer languages: their creators use different words and symbols for the same function and design different syntax; function bodies are delimited by indentation in some, curly braces in others, begin and end in others. The same data representation: some use json, some use xml. The naming of variables and functions comes in every variety, underscores, hyphens, capitalization, two_word, two-word, twoWord. Beyond satisfying different people's arbitrary preferences, do these different names have anything to do with the essence of the data? Operating-system command-line parameters: some people start them with a slash, some with a dash, some with a bare single letter. In graphical interfaces, the same single-choice selection is a drop-down menu here and a radio button there; the keyboard shortcuts of Windows and macOS were each arbitrarily designed by their respective designers; in CAD, 3D animation and image-processing software, the operations and shortcuts for the same function all differ.

This diversity born of differing human preference exists everywhere across software and hardware. It is determined by the biological diversity of humans, and by the process of civilizational diversification that evolved over the long term in different geographical and social environments, that is, the Babel effect. For a long time, the developers and users at every link in the computer ecosystem were humans themselves, so human diversity in naming and presentation has soaked into every vessel, every muscle, every cell of the entire computer software and hardware ecosystem.

But with the arrival of the AI era, things have changed. Humans have been progressively withdrawing from the construction of this software and hardware ecosystem; its construction is being, and will inevitably be, carried out by AI-capable computers iterating on themselves. Humans retreat to the outside of the ecosystem and interact with it through input and output systems. The current trend is that humans not only no longer write code, they no longer even do unit tests or code review; the author of Clean Code has publicly stated that he now does not read AI-generated code at all. That being so, why keep using human natural language as the syntax rules and naming rules of programming languages? Why keep using the human-habituated form of text files and directories to structure code? Are Merkle-DAG and hashing, as a unified mechanism for naming and constructing programs, not better suited for computers and AI to handle? Tamper-proof and collision-proof, usable as both name and address, convenient for distributed storage, fast to index, able to name vast numbers of objects without negotiation: are these properties not far more reliable than human natural language?

It is like a fully automated production line: if the operator is no longer human, there is no need to design the robotic arm after the structure of human limbs; in a fully self-driving car, there is no need to put a steering wheel in the cockpit. Natural language and graphical interfaces will henceforth serve only as input and output interfaces for interacting with humans; the internal operation has no need to preserve the human interface. The computer use and browser use of today's AI agents will also gradually be replaced by more efficient interfaces between AIs; these capabilities for controlling graphical interfaces and browsers will in future be useful only for demonstrating to humans. Is today's MCP interface not exactly this kind of interface? Only its syntactic architecture still carries the residue of code forms modeled on human natural language, and it likewise needs to be thoroughly rebuilt with the Merkle-DAG and hash mechanism.

### Content identity guarantees behavioral difference

Someone will say that unification must distinguish between differences of representation, which can be eliminated, and differences of behavior, which must be expressed: something called addition might be unlimited-precision integer addition, fixed-width addition that wraps on overflow, addition that raises an error on overflow, or floating-point addition with a specific rounding rule, and these distinctions must be preserved.

In fact, the naming scheme of Merkle-DAG plus hash is precisely what guarantees this. If the data and code composing two objects differ essentially in behavior, that necessarily changes their names; any slight difference in internal mechanism is reliably reflected by the mechanism as a change of name. The traditional naming of human natural language, by contrast, because its vocabulary cannot expand without limit, always tends to give the same name to additions with different internal implementations, and does not rename them when the precision or method of the internal implementation changes; the more common practice is to bump the version number of the library the function lives in, and then have other functions that depend on it annotate in their dependency table that the version must be greater than some value. Merkle-DAG can automatically guarantee, from the bottom up, the consistency of name and implementation; through its traceability it can automatically establish accurate dependencies; it can even, after a low-level object is upgraded, automatically trace and modify the code in higher-level objects in a chain, completing the upgrade iteration automatically.

This is what I mean by the information-science foundation of the new civilization: a language in which content is identity, identity is address, dependency is structure, and version is history.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Content addressing can only confirm byte identity; it does not decide whether two programs are semantically the same, nor does it prove that the original code is not malicious. Does "automatically guarantee" hide unsolved problems inside the word "unify"?**

A: What the hash guarantees is this: the same content gets the same identity, different content gets different identities, and given the content you can verify whether it has been replaced. It guarantees these three things cleanly, more cleanly than any human naming. Whether semantics are equivalent, which version should currently be chosen, whether the source is trustworthy: these are another layer of work built on top of identity. The equivalence of different implementations of the same function is decided by benchmarks and verification evidence; which implementation to choose is decided by contract, load, hardware and permissions; whether the source is trustworthy is answered by the traceable contribution history recorded in the same DAG. Each of these layers can be done, and each can only be done once the identity layer is clean. Take the problem of names and addresses out of human hands first, and only then do the remaining problems become, for the first time, something you can get your hands on.
