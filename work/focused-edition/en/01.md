# Part I: Principles

## Chapter 1: Minimization, Maximization and Fixed Points

### The foundation is already there

This book does not start from a new mathematical object. It starts from the three most reliable things in natural science: the variational principle, fixed points, emergence. Each of the three is textbook material, and each has been tested for several hundred years. What I do is set them side by side, bring out one and the same structure, and then carry that structure all the way into society.

### Minimization is everywhere in natural science

From the microscopic to the macroscopic, from physics to chemistry to biology, the principle of minimization is visible everywhere.

At the level of elementary particles, the motion of all particles and fields obeys the principle of least action; the paths they choose make the action stationary, usually a minimum. At the chemical level, electrons outside the nucleus preferentially occupy the lowest-energy orbitals; at constant temperature and pressure, reactions always proceed spontaneously in the direction of decreasing Gibbs free energy; molecules always adjust their spatial structure to minimize total potential energy, which is why the water molecule takes its V shape with an angle of 104.5 degrees. At the biological level, the folding of an amino acid chain into its native structure is a search for the lowest point of free energy; phospholipid molecules spontaneously form bilayer membranes in water in order to minimize the free energy of the system; metabolic pathways and neuronal connections are all optimized under evolutionary pressure toward states that consume less matter and energy. At the level of gravitation, a celestial body made of free-flowing fluid spontaneously gathers toward its center, so that its total gravitational potential energy reaches a minimum, which is why large bodies collapse into spheres. General relativity interprets gravity as geometry: an object acted on by no external force moves along a geodesic, the path between two points in spacetime along which proper time is stationary; making the Einstein-Hilbert action stationary yields the field equations.

### Every minimization is a maximization

Nearly all of these minimization principles can, mathematically, be seen equivalently as the maximization of some corresponding physical quantity.

In relativity, minimizing the action is equivalent to maximizing proper time: a freely falling object's own clock runs fastest. In thermodynamics, minimizing Gibbs free energy at constant temperature and pressure is maximizing the total entropy of system plus environment: phospholipids packing tightly into a membrane is a local decrease in entropy, and what it buys is the complete liberation of the surrounding water molecules. In chemistry, minimizing the total potential energy of a system is equivalent to maximizing bond energy: the more energy released, the more firmly the atoms are bound. In optics, Fermat's principle says light takes the path of least time, and from the path-integral viewpoint this means that only near this path do the phases add constructively, while the other paths interfere destructively.

Minimization and maximization are two faces of the variational principle. This is not my discovery, and strictly speaking what these principles require is a stationary value, a minimum being only the most common case. What I have done is set them side by side to bring out the common structure, and then take it out of physics.

### Fixed points: the mathematical name for stable structure

A state of a system that no longer changes under the repeated action of some rule of change is called, in mathematics, a fixed point. The equilibrium states of physics and chemistry are fixed points. The attractors of dynamical systems are fixed points and their generalization: nearby states are all pulled toward them. The Nash equilibrium of game theory is also a fixed point: each person's strategy is the best response to the strategies of the others, and no one can gain by changing unilaterally; what Nash used to prove its existence was precisely a fixed point theorem.

There is another class of especially important stable structures that are not in equilibrium. A flame, a cell, a living organism, a city all maintain themselves by continuously consuming energy, and fall apart the moment the flow of energy is cut. Prigogine called them dissipative structures. When this book later speaks of people, organizations, nations and civilizations, it treats each of them as a dissipative structure: a stable structure that acquires and converts energy through its internal structure and external tools, and by doing so maintains and develops itself.

So "things that stably exist" have the same mathematical name at every level. The atom is a fixed point under the electromagnetic interaction, the molecule is a fixed point of chemical bonding, the species is a fixed point of the evolutionary game, the institution is a fixed point of the social game.

### Emergence: more is different

The fixed point describes existence; emergence describes change. Philip Anderson made this clear in "More Is Different": basic objects at a lower level, through combination, form stable structures at a higher level, and the higher level has laws of its own that cannot be simply derived from the laws of the lower level. Chemistry is not applied physics, and biology is not applied chemistry. But the existence of the higher-level structure still obeys every constraint of the lower level.

Put emergence and fixed points together: the fixed points of each level are the basic building blocks from which the next level is constructed. Atoms make up molecules, molecules make up cells, cells make up living organisms, people make up society. No link in this chain needs an extra, supernatural rule.

### A working principle that runs through every level

Put the three things above together and you get the working principle of this book:

**For a stable structure, the internal cost of maintaining its own existence tends toward minimization, while the paths by which it establishes connections outward with other objects tend toward maximization. The maximization of connections among objects at a lower level is, seen from the level above, the minimization of the internal cost of the newly emerged structure. The two processes complement each other and advance in alternation.**

The first half is the common form that the variational principle and thermodynamics take at every level; the second half is a summary of dissipative structures and of the whole history of evolution. At the physical level it has an exact mathematical form; at the biological and social levels it is a generalization made by this book, an organizing framework, not yet a theorem. Whether it has value depends on whether it can yield operational definitions and testable inferences at the social level. Chapter 3 gives the first one.

Scientific research itself is an example of this principle: the minimization of basic theory, Newtonian mechanics relative to natural philosophy, gave people a far stronger ability to transform the external world and construct things that did not previously exist; and new tools in turn promote progress in basic science.

### From the theory of evolution to society

Darwin's survival of the fittest, described with this principle, says: in the functions required to maintain their own existence, living things always tend to evolve toward forms that consume less energy; in connecting with the outside world, they always tend to evolve in the direction of being able to relate to more objects. An example of the former is the increase in the efficiency of digestion and metabolism; examples of the latter are the passage from single-celled to multicellular organisms, and from solitary living to social cooperation.

Human society is the same. The individual's instinct to maintain its own survival, the instinct to develop social relationships and economic interests, Nash equilibrium, Pareto optimality, the cost-cutting, efficiency-raising and profit-maximizing of firms: these phenomena of minimization and maximization are everywhere. Even the demands made at the level of politics can be interpreted by this principle: the right to life corresponds to a stable structure's instinct to maintain itself; freedom corresponds to the maximization of relations with other objects; democracy corresponds to the maximization of the emergent higher-order group organization; and equality is the precondition that truly allows individual interest and group interest to be maximized at the same time. Eliminating the inequality of power is the best way to eliminate the waste of resources in the whole system: it minimizes the internal friction of the whole system while maximizing the free connections among the individuals within it. Marx's sentence, "the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all", is, I believe, saying exactly this.

### A ruler: the energy-efficiency ratio

This principle gives the book a ruler that it uses from beginning to end. When comparing two complex systems, if you compare their advanced features one by one, you will very likely find that each has its own strengths and the argument never ends. The other way is to start from the bottom: have two systems with the same function complete the same task, compare the output first, then compare the energy consumed in the process, and get the energy-efficiency ratio. From here on, whenever this book compares two strategies, two institutions or two architectures, it uses this ruler.

Theories can be measured with it too. Occam's razor says: among the theories that explain the same phenomena, the shortest is the best. In the language of the theory of computation, a theory is a program, the phenomena are its output, and the length of the shortest program is called the Kolmogorov complexity. This optimum is itself uncomputable, but it can be approached step by step. So the evaluation function for a good theory is the construction of the greatest external connectivity at the least internal cost. Chapter 14 will use it again when discussing how humans and AI do research together.

### Evaluation is not a judgment on the right to exist

This ruler has a limit that must be stated clearly. The evaluation function is a descriptive tool, not a judgment on the legitimacy of existence. We can judge that the hunting ability of felines far exceeds that of rodents, but we cannot say the existence of felines has higher value. Everything that exists is a fixed point produced by some converging minimization process; the value of every direction of evolution lies in enriching the space of evolution and preparing for the next emergence.

So later in this book I will compare institutions and strategies at great length, better and worse, higher and lower, but please remember: what is being compared is the energy-efficiency ratio and the capacity to evolve, not the right to exist. Having a better option does not mean the old option must be eliminated. Maintaining diversity is the safer and faster way to achieve evolution. This point will come up again in Chapter 3 when discussing lenient and strict game strategies.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: In physics, "action" and "free energy" have precise mathematical definitions and measurable values. In social science, "least cost" and "internal friction of the system" do not. Without a precise definition, it is not a falsifiable proposition but a rhetorical frame that can be explained any way you like after the fact.**

A: The value of Newton's framework in 1687 lay not in how much it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided an architecture within which work could accumulate; at that time no one could write a mathematical description of how steam acts after water boils, and that does not show the framework was wrong. But I also accept the reasonable part of the objection: Newton in 1687 did give operational definitions of mass, force and acceleration, and that is the minimum bar for a theory in its infancy. So the demand this book places on itself is this: every time this principle is applied to a specific field, it must come down to an operational definition. Its first operational definition in the social domain is the strictness of Chapter 3: the intensity of punishment and the interval before forgiveness in the tit-for-tat strategy, as the control parameter for the convergence speed and stability of social structure.

**Q: This merely reorganizes what is already known. Does that count as something new?**

A: Reorganizing language to express the same thing is not a meaningless act. It makes previously invisible relationships visible, lets bridges be built between previously independent fields, and lets later generations do work in the new language that could not be done in the old one. The entire wager of this book lies here: whether it lets people, for the first time, ask questions that could not be asked in the old language of social science. When you have finished Part I, you can judge for yourself.
