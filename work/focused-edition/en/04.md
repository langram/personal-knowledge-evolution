## Chapter 4: Common Knowledge and the Two Chain Reactions

### What common knowledge is

Human social structures that rest on law, religious codes, political programs, and the moral standards and rules of the game that people have agreed on and jointly observe can become strong, stable and complex; structures that rest on the will of a few or the unwritten rules of a small circle can only be simple, fragile and temporary structures. The difference is that the former are common knowledge: everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on recursively. Game theory gives this concept a strict definition, and it is the precondition for any coordination to happen at all: the same fact leads to completely different behavior when each person knows it separately and when everyone knows that everyone knows it.

A human organization of any size, from a family at the small end up through villages, cities and nations to the whole global village, is founded on the intersection of consensus its participants can reach. And tit-for-tat is precisely the most basic piece of common knowledge that all of humanity knows and takes for granted. Under this piece of common knowledge, trust and cooperation form the positive chain reaction, and distrust running into betrayal and conflict forms the negative chain reaction.

### The negative chain

Insufficient or concealed exchange of information leads to mutual suspicion; suspicion further obstructs exchange, making both sides deliberately conceal more information; this plunges both sides into greater suspicion, until it develops into distrust; distrust leads to refusal to communicate and all-out concealment, and from there to intense emotional hostility; then, out of fear of the other side's attack, you start strengthening your own offensive and defensive capacity, and that behavior of yours leads the other side to strengthen theirs; events escalate to resistance and confrontation in action, from local, low-intensity acts of violence step by step upward until the situation is completely out of control, turning the ordinary people inside it from friendly cooperators full of love and reason into impulsive killers full of hate. The study of international relations calls the second half of this chain the security dilemma.

This chain is not a theoretical derivation. Boeing's concealment of the 737 MAX flight control system and the whistleblower crisis that followed, Volkswagen's Dieselgate, both were caused by it. Imagine if all the information from design to testing to production to sales were like version-tracked open-source code in a git repository: this suspicion and distrust could hardly arise. Business is only one class of example; some cases carry the risk of destroying civilization: from the outbreak of the two world wars, to the US-Soviet Cold War and the Cuban missile crisis, to the confrontation between the United States and China today.

The more both sides fear that something will happen, the more likely it is to happen. The endpoint of this negative chain, whichever possibility you take, is not a global optimum: either the two sides are evenly matched and reach a local equilibrium of mutual deterrence; or one side pulls far ahead and uses its lead to utterly destroy or suppress the other. Either way it carries the risk of losing control and self-destruction. This is also why humanity fears nuclear technology and fears AI.

### The positive chain

Now the reverse: every bit more of information exchanged, every bit less concealed, increases mutual trust; increased trust makes both sides more willing to share information, more willing to cooperate; cooperation further strengthens trust and brings more cooperation. This positive chain finally leads every party to turn all information relevant to promoting cooperation into common knowledge, making the probability of reaching the globally optimal equilibrium larger and larger.

The loss caused by concealment exceeds the concealed fact itself. The opacity and asymmetry of information not only keep the two sides from reaching the optimal game, but also keep the cost of mental and material confrontation between cooperating parties stubbornly high.

The theory of repeated games gives a key conclusion here: under the same payoffs, long-term cooperation and long-term mutual defection are both equilibria. The payoff structure determines which equilibria exist, not which one you land in; what decides where you land is the initial expectation and the direction of the first move. So if we want the human social game driven by tit-for-tat to reach its optimum, the initial direction of the game matters enormously; it is the single most critical step in whether the chain reaction runs positive or negative.

### The optimal rules of the game

So the optimal rules of the game are to make the following rules common knowledge for all of humanity:

1. Everyone follows the tit-for-tat strategy.
2. Everyone's initial strategy is the positive strategy, that is, to share all information that is needed to reach external cooperation and that will not harm oneself.
3. All shared information is recorded and shared as common knowledge in the form of a Merkle-DAG, while a dynamic-weight decision system guarantees that no party's decision power ever grows large enough to use that information to harm the one who shared it.
4. If, because of the complexity and unpredictability of the system, the execution of the weighted decision system damages the interests of an individual in the system, compensation is made immediately and the game's decision strategy is immediately revised to repair it. All those who suffered loss treat the situation as a loss produced by the system's own capacity limits or by external force majeure, not as the malice of some decision-maker.
5. Where a temporary loss is suffered for the sake of a better long-term result, the decision system supplies an estimated plan and expected outcome, monitors the progress of the game at all times, adjusts the plan as circumstances require in order to succeed, or stops execution when failure is expected. On success, those who suffered loss treat it as a temporary sacrifice for long-term benefit; on failure, they treat it as a loss produced by the system's capacity limits or external force majeure, not as the malice of some decision-maker.

Two points in this rule system matter most. First, the verifiable common-knowledge storage system, and the social intelligent neural network of dynamic-weight decision-making, eliminate at the level of mechanism the possibility of individuals using the power in their hands to harm one another; Part III and Part II describe these two things respectively. Second, the point "everyone's initial strategy is the positive strategy" must be guaranteed on both the emotional and the rational side. The meta-cognition of Chapter 2, once it becomes everyone's consensus, gives people a calmer attitude toward the world and toward others, and more tolerance toward people who think differently; it also raises tolerance, reinterpreting a provocation from "the other side is evil" to "the product of the other side's structural differences in this situation," and a certain degree of tolerance is precisely the necessary condition for avoiding echoing vendettas in a noisy repeated game.

Meta-cognition is responsible for willingness; verifiable history is responsible for credibility. An initial cooperative attitude that is only declared is what game theory calls cheap talk: the other side cannot tell sincerity from disguise. A portable, checkable record of cooperation makes "I am ready to cooperate" no longer a sentence but a record that others can check. Only the two together make a first step that the other side can credit. So the new system does not need to first convince everyone and first dissolve all hostility before cooperation begins; what it needs is to make the first step a cooperative one, and to let the other side see it.

### The bottleneck is not technology but willingness

These two chains are idealized extremes, and the great majority of situations in reality lie between the two. But one thing is certain: humanity's ability to study and control complex systems keeps growing; computers gave people the ability to predict geology, oceans and weather; computers and AI gave people systematic understanding of, and the ability to modify, protein folding and the genetic system. For all of these complex systems, the data to be collected is not about us humans ourselves.

For the complex system that is human civilization, the information to be collected is the information of all people about themselves. For human beings, who have subjective agency, the capacity to collect and process information is not the bottleneck; the subjective willingness to provide that information is. Collecting information by force, through the power gap of the pyramid structure and the deterrence of the organs of violence, is the worst strategy: what is collected is data distorted under the pressure of power, deliberately concealed or falsified, neither complete nor true. This is the most important reason why humanity's capacity to understand and remake nature runs so far ahead of its capacity to understand and control its own social system.

The bottleneck the new mechanism must solve is exactly this one: eliminate the power gap, limit at the level of mechanism the abuse of power and the occurrence of harm, guarantee that the information people provide will not be turned against them, let people get positive returns from sharing, and guarantee that this positive feedback keeps running reliably and continuously.

### Behind privacy is power

What people fear in surveillance is not the technical means but the person who controls the means: whether that person's power is locked in a cage, whether that person will do something to harm them.

The so-called privacy and security problems all have their root here. Between any two objects in human society, whether the state against the individual, the company against the individual, or different individuals at the same level, the information barriers and the wariness all come from the fear that once the other side knows, it will use the power in its hands to gain the advantage in the game and inflict harm. Whether information becomes private is fundamentally tied to whether power is equal and whether the game is fair. The more equal, the more open the information, the higher the trust between individuals, and the stronger the willingness to cooperate. The same holds between person and person: how much two people know about each other is proportional to the degree of their cooperation and intimacy.

Push this mechanism to its end: the weak, in order to play against the powerful, refuse to provide personal information so as to strengthen their own capacity for defense, even though this lowers the cooperative efficiency of the whole system; these refusals and defenses in turn force those above to strengthen surveillance and their capacity for suppression in response. The inequality of power and information produces an unfair distribution of benefits, unfairness produces greater distrust, distrust raises the tension of conflict, the rise in tension leads those above to need a larger power gap to guarantee structural stability, and the larger power gap further produces unfair distribution. This is the same loop as the positive feedback of the previous chapter, at the level of information. It has produced the world we have now: science and technology are raising productivity fast, yet the gaps in class, in power, and between rich and poor grow ever wider. If we continue along the old system, AI will not become the tool that levels everyone's differences in ability; it will instead become the machine by which a few suppress and exploit the many.

### Locking power in a cage

The better way to lock power in a cage is a better mechanism for distributing power, rather than hoping that the person or group holding great power is a wise ruler or a saint. Under the pyramid system, however detailed the rules, the power to make the rules and the power to enforce them still sit at the apex. So what the cage must lock up, first of all, is the power to make the rules.

Conversely, as long as the mechanism guarantees that harm cannot happen, a virtuous circle arises, the exact opposite of the vicious circle of the old system: if no party has the ability to use the power in its hands to harm others, then harm does not happen in cooperation; the more certain it is that harm will not happen, the longer people cooperate and the more they trust each other; trust brings more sharing of information and deeper cooperation. Even if an accident in cooperation produces a temporary loss, since the mechanism rules out malicious harm, neither side will think the other acted out of malice, and cooperation will not end because of one accident. Friends and partners who get along well in real life relate to each other in exactly this way; the difference is that it depends on the personal character of both parties, not on an institutional guarantee.

"Open" here refers to information that concerns other objects in the outside world; data that can be encapsulated inside the individual and has no effect on the outside is not included. Chapter 10 will turn this boundary into concrete rules for viewing permissions.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: Eliminate misunderstanding and hostility first, then cooperate: isn't the order reversed? A verifiable history can by itself sustain cooperation under distrust; contact and understanding should be the product of cooperation, not its precondition.**

A: I did not make the elimination of hostility a precondition of cooperation. The first move of tit-for-tat is to cooperate, and that move is not derived from the payoffs; it is an assumption about the other side. That is exactly what meta-cognition does: it does not change the payoffs, it changes the selection of equilibrium. The verifiable record does a different thing: it makes that assumption checkable. One is responsible for willingness, the other for credibility, and the order is: simultaneously.
