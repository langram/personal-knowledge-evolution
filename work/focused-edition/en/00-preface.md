# Nova Principia: Mathematical Principles of Social Science

## Relativity, From Physical to Social Theory

**新原理：社会科学的数学原理**

**从物理相对论到社会相对论**

Author: Tangram Liu (langram)

## Preface: The Cruel Lesson

Let me be frank: the resemblance between this book's title and the title of Newton's classic, between its subtitle and the title of Einstein's popular book, and between the title of this preface and the title of Sutton's essay, are all deliberate. In an age of information overload, attention is a scarce resource, and without an eye-catching title a book has almost no chance of being noticed. Clickbait is a necessity.

But the real justification for the title lies in the content.

The title of this preface is adapted from Sutton's "The Bitter Lesson". That essay looks back over seventy years of AI research, which is the history of humanity's attempt to reproduce in machines the complex system that is the mind, and finds the same thing happening again and again: every approach that relied on experts hand-writing their own knowledge and experience into the system worked in the short run and lost in the long run to approaches that relied on general mathematical models, enormous data and immense computing power. Sutton draws two conclusions. First, general methods have enormous power, and he names two of them: search and learning. Second, the contents of minds are too complex to be simplified, and we should not write our own understanding of them into the system; the only things that should be built in are the meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity.

Human civilization is a higher-order and more complex system built on top of human minds, and each of its units is itself a complex mind. If a single mind is too complex to be hand-written into a system, a civilization made of billions of minds is all the more so. So Sutton's conclusion extends quite naturally to civilization. In fact, has humanity's exploration of civilizational institutions over thousands of years not been living through this very lesson all along? To this day humanity solves the problems of constructing and organizing society by relying on political, economic and academic authorities who, on their personal experience and learning, each design by hand the institutions of states, the articles of law and the policies of economics, politics and diplomacy, and through these cooperate, compete and confront one another. A hand-designed institution has written into it the right and wrong of its designer's own reference frame; with no way of converting between frames, disagreements can only be settled by confrontation. The lesson of humanity's study of the mind is bitter, to be sure, but its cost was no more than detours taken and years and money wasted. On the road of exploring civilizational orders, the conflicts between people of different cognition and different ideologies have cost humanity blood and lives. This lesson is cruel and bloody; "bitter" is not a strong enough word for it.

Replace the complex system of the mind in Sutton's essay with the complex system of human civilization, and the conclusion should hold just the same. So there are two key questions: which are the meta-methods that can find and capture the complexity of human civilization? And on which most basic principles of mathematics and science do these meta-methods rest? To the first question this book's answer is: search is institutional experiment that forks, runs in parallel and is compared on a unified base; learning is dynamic weights, updated again and again by the feedback of real-world results. To the second, the main title and the subtitle of this book are the hint.

On top of a few basic laws, using rigorous logical reasoning and mathematical calculation, Newton's *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* built a complete and self-consistent physical system. Later generations shortened its name to the *Principia*, as if it had described the principles of everything. In fact it could not do that. But this does not diminish its value in the slightest. Its value lay not in how many phenomena it could explain at the time, but in the fact that it provided a unified architecture within which work could be accumulated. The next two hundred years of physics were all carried out inside that architecture.

Social science today is in exactly the state natural philosophy was in before Newton. The various schools of economics, sociology and political science contradict one another, are incompatible with one another, and lack the kind of unified axiomatic foundation that physics has. Most of them are less sciences than philosophical structures built on psychology, morality and experience, borrowing at most a few mathematical tools for statistical work. You might ask: is there any mathematical principle in existing political science? The different social theories are more like observers riding on multiple trains, each moving at a different speed and in a different direction, each creating a theory from its own observations, with no framework for converting between them or referring one to another.

So what this book sets out to do can be said in one sentence: to place social science on the same foundation of mathematical principles as natural science. This foundation does not need to be reinvented. The few principles that natural science has verified again and again over several hundred years, the variational principle, fixed points and equilibrium, symmetry and relativity, reduction and emergence, together with the game theory, information theory and machine learning of the twentieth century, are already enough. What this book does is take them out of their separate disciplines, line them up into one continuous chain of reasoning from physics to society, and then construct the theoretical framework of social science along that chain. The system of social science should be a natural extension of the system of natural science, not some additional set of rules outside the laws of cosmic evolution, governed by supernatural factors such as a creator god, consciousness or the soul.

The subtitle has two reasons.

The first reason is in the content. Symmetry is a basic feature of everything in the universe, and what it means mathematically is this: remaining unchanged under some transformation. The principle of relativity in physics is one such symmetry: the laws of physics remain unchanged under a change of reference frame. In Newtonian mechanics position and velocity were already relative, while time was still absolute; Einstein extended the principle of relativity to all the laws of physics, and time became relative too. Relativity abolished absolute time, absolute space and the absolute reference frame, and reduced the measures of time and space to relations between reference frames that can be converted exactly, while the laws themselves are the same in every frame. Since social science stands on the same foundation of mathematical principles as natural science, there is no reason for this symmetry to fail in the social domain. Each person's cognition is a reference frame formed by his genes, experience and environment, and none of them is privileged. So the relativity of the properties of space and time at the physical level, extended into the social domain, is the relativity of cognitive reference frames: there is no absolute right and wrong, good and bad, justice and evil, only coordinates formed under different conditions, and rules for converting between them; what does not vary is the laws that hold in every frame and the facts that can be checked. What the observers on those trains lack is exactly a Lorentz transformation. This is also what the lesson means here: no reference frame is privileged, so no one's reference frame should be hand-written into institutions.

With the mathematical models and computational tools of mechanics, humanity analyzes the interactions between objects, brings unstable factors such as collision and friction under control, and has built complex buildings, equipment and tools; the same methods and tools can also be used to analyze the interactions between people of different cognition, to resolve conflicts and contradictions, and to construct complex mechanisms and organizations of human cooperation.

The second reason is practical, and urgent, and it is why this book cannot wait: humanity needs relativity to dissolve and remake the extreme binary oppositions in its own civilizational genes, and there is not much time left to do it.

In today's world the evolution of technology is accelerating while the evolution of institutions has stalled, and the structural gap between the two is approaching a critical point. Since the Second World War, the rules of the game between nations and the political and economic systems within them have seen almost no fundamental change; over the same period information technology has passed through five waves: the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet and AI. We can already manipulate single atoms, edit the human genome and build ships bound for space, and we have begun to automate intellectual labor on a large scale with AI, yet we still settle our conflicts with pyramid institutions designed centuries ago, and decide policies that touch billions of people by emotion and position. A civilization whose power over the outside world outruns its control of itself is a child of enormous strength and fragile mind, swinging a chain flail that weighs a ton: sooner or later it hits itself. Nuclear weapons were the first flail; it came down twice, on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and in the decades since, humanity has been lucky enough not to let it fall again. AI is the second, a revolutionary technology that frightens and excites the whole of humanity at once, and double-edged like nuclear technology.

But the deeper crisis is in the humans who wield these technologies. Human history is an evolutionary record full of violent conflict, driven over and over by the tit-for-tat strategy, and that history has deposited in traditional religion and in morality and ethics a mass of emotional genes of extreme binary opposition, such as angels and demons, justice and evil. The conflicts that play out again and again between individuals, between groups and between nations are all these genes acting as catalyst and accelerator: define the other side as evil, and then every violent means becomes justice. In the nuclear age humanity was lucky enough to escape all-out nuclear war. In this age empowered by AI, the lethality of the state's machinery of violence will be multiplied many times over. How likely is it that we get lucky twice? The framing of relativity is precisely the dissolution and remaking of this civilizational gene: it reduces "justice and evil" to "differences between reference frames that have conditions of formation", and reduces moral judgment to facts that can be checked and rules that can be changed. Once that remaking is done, the same tit-for-tat strategy turns from the catalyst of violent conflict into the catalyst and accelerator of positive cooperation under a new civilizational order. So what the subtitle describes is both the foundational thesis of this book's whole theory and the means by which humanity can dissolve and remake this civilizational gene.

The rapid development of AI in the last few years has given everything said above the objective conditions for becoming real:

First, large AI language models have given the human space of consciousness a mathematical model for the first time. The whole of human knowledge has been integrated into a high-dimensional semantic space of billions of parameters: any property of an object can be represented as a multi-dimensional vector, and the system formed by all objects and their properties can be described as a multi-dimensional tensor, which can be measured, recorded and processed, and on which higher-order social models can be designed, constructed and evolved. A Cartesian coordinate system and a Newtonian reference frame for social science have become technically possible for the first time.

Second, silicon-based computing devices can now possess human intelligence, and this fact provides the basis for a consensus: problems in the domain of human society can also be solved by mathematical and scientific models, just like problems in the natural domain. Every cognition and behavior of a person at the social level is an objective phenomenon that, like a natural phenomenon, can be observed, studied, quantified, recorded, analyzed and modeled.

The book has four parts. Part I is the principles: the minimization, maximization and fixed points that natural science has already given us, the extension of relativity from physical reference frames to cognitive reference frames, tit-for-tat as the iteration operator that constructs social structure, and common knowledge and the two chain reactions. Part II is the construction: two kinds of resources and equality in the sense of the rules, the social neural network with dynamic weights, artificial intelligence as public infrastructure, and the Large Civilization Model in which training and inference are one. Part III is the foundation: a carrier of common knowledge in which content is identity, and every person's digital space and data sovereignty. Part IV is the path: the gap between technology and institutions, alignment and the fork in the road, peaceful evolution starting from one person's cloud storage, and science, culture and shared creation.

On method, I want to make three things clear first.

First, I have had no professional training in mathematics, and rigorous formal proofs are beyond my ability. The path this book takes has two stretches: the first sets existing scientific and mathematical knowledge that commands broad consensus side by side, to bring out its common structure; the second starts from that structure and deduces the construction of society step by step. In the second I have tried to make the premises reliable enough and the reasoning simple and intuitive enough that every step can be followed by a reader with a university education, and every step can also be refuted by the reader at a specific link.

Second, a word of warning in advance, with an example from chess. The evolution of a complex system often gets stuck in a locally optimal state of play, like a chess position in which both sides' pieces are tangled together, neither willing to give anything up, as if whoever wins this skirmish wins the whole game. A true master has a higher intuition, and the courage to sacrifice a piece and enter a temporarily worse position in order to reach the global optimum. The proposal in this book is counterintuitive, and as you read on you will be unable to resist denouncing it as the reckless audacity of an outsider, like a child casually sending the queen into the enemy's line of fire. Please have a little more patience. Once the first move of the sacrifice is played, the position often suddenly opens up. When trying to reform a civilizational order that has fallen far behind technological evolution, one must have the courage to break every stale habit of thought and step outside the existing framework. Do not let the concepts of nation, regime and corporation, those traditional pyramid structures, confine your imagination of the future. In the evolving system that is the universe, every entity, structure and evolving object, along with its rules, has a beginning, and will have its moment of extinction or transformation.

Third, the parts of this book differ in how reliable they are, and I keep them apart. The foundation in mathematics and science uses theories that are already settled; the generalizations and institutional designs I build on it are inferences that can be questioned and ought to be tested; the part on information systems is an engineering proposal premised on current and foreseeable computing and AI capabilities. The appendix at the end of the book spells out item by item which parts are grounds, which are inferences, and which could be started on today. As for the hypotheses I have thought about for a long time but that still lack broad consensus, category theory and the dualoid, the mathematical roots of consciousness, and many more distant ideas in engineering and civilization, I have put them separately into a document, "Extensions: Experimental Hypotheses and Ideas", and they are not premises of the argument in this book.

Last, let me stress that I have not discovered and defined the specific rules and structures of a new human civilizational order. What this book proposes is a foundation of mathematical principles for social science, extended naturally from the mathematical foundation of natural science, together with some meta-methods, meta-strategies and a plan for getting started on building a new civilizational order. They are like the mathematical principles of the early days of large language models: those theories and designs were a compass, a telescope, a chart, but not the destination; the day they were written down did not mean that a powerful AI model at once appeared in perfect form. In the same way, these theoretical ideas of mine are only a torch held up to scout the road toward the singularity of civilization. The traditional strategies by which human civilization evolved over the past several thousand years have achieved a great deal, to be sure, but they were also full of setbacks and failures, and were paid for with countless blood and lives. I hope that on the new road people can travel a little more smoothly and a little faster, and need no longer pay for their lessons in blood and lives.

Good. Now let us begin.
