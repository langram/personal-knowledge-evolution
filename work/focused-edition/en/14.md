## Chapter 14: Science, Culture and Shared Creation

### Why prior experience is needed

Chapter 11 said that people in the old system have never truly experienced the new mechanism, and the instinct toward the unknown is resistance. Past transitions from old system to new, apart from violent revolution, were also completed through a prior experience at the spiritual level: the heaven of religion, the description of the future society in manifestos, the rendering of the American dream. The new system too needs to give people a prior experience, and the difference is this: its prior experience can be participated in and verified.

Three levels must be kept apart. Science fiction is responsible for expanding imagination: novels and film let people experience the fates of characters and the possible shapes of future life. Simulation is responsible for exploring possibilities: multiplayer civilization games let people experience, in a virtual world, different rules of collaboration and their consequences. Real-world practice is responsible for providing evidence: real collaboration experiments observe participants' costs, returns and satisfaction on real tasks.

These three levels can be joined into one road. A player plays a builder of the new civilization in the virtual world and takes part in a simulated task; later they discover that a certain task in the game has a real-world counterpart, and they can choose to join a real open-source project; they switch from virtual to real, not because the story demands that they accept some belief, but because they already understand the task, the rules and the risks and are willing to try; the results of the real project then feed back into the game's historical record and become data for the next round of simulation. From taking part in virtual scenarios in spare time, to solving real problems and earning income, to gradually putting more into the new system, there is no bloody conflict or revolution anywhere in the process.

I call this kind of work self-fulfilling science fiction. Traditional science fiction is responsible only for imagining, not for realizing; whereas the authors of this kind of work must at the same time be the first builders of the civilization it describes, otherwise it is only fantasy.

### The virtual social laboratory

Chapter 6 said that virtual tit-for-tat turns real damage into virtual damage; Chapter 13 said that institutions must fork, run in parallel and be compared on a unified base. These two things need the same thing: an AI-driven virtual social laboratory in digital space.

It lets institutions be experienced and tested before they are adopted. The cost of trial and error for a distribution rule in reality is real loss; in the laboratory it is virtual points. Validating a decision mechanism in reality takes years; in the laboratory, thousands of rounds can run in days. Results in the virtual world do not automatically predict real society. Its role is to screen out the plainly unworkable proposals before they reach reality, and to screen in the proposals worth piloting at small scale in reality. For it to have genuine research value, it must allow different institutional designs to coexist, fork, compete and iterate within it.

### Everyone is a student, and also a teacher

The new system is not one where people hand their happiness and their hopes to a Big Brother high above, just as the cells of the human body do not pin all their hopes on one super-cell. At the political level, everyone is a manager and also one of the managed; at the economic level, everyone is a producer and also a consumer; at the level of knowledge, everyone is a student and also a teacher. What governs everyone in the civilizational body is the intelligence produced by the civilizational brain that everyone forms as its neurons.

Under this system, education is no longer a pyramid that passes knowledge down from authorities to students, but a network in which everyone both absorbs and contributes. One person's depth in some field becomes a capability that others can use directly; one person's question in some field becomes the starting point of someone else's deep dive. The creation of knowledge and the spread of knowledge are two faces of one process.

### Bold hypothesis, rigorous reasoning

I once said to the AI I was discussing with: unless the most basic principles of physics and mathematics are violated, do not use current or outdated frameworks of social science to evaluate the infinite possibilities of future institutional rules. Because this body of theory is still in its pre-Newtonian period, and the different theories look more like expressions within their own frames of reference. I have found that AI, as an intelligent agent, tends to reason within the fixed frameworks already in its knowledge base. It tends toward marking the boat to find the sword, like the man who notched his boat to mark where his sword fell overboard, rather than daring to question the existing frameworks.

But after the bold hypothesis must come rigorous reasoning: accept the premise in full, do not cherry-pick the conclusions. Take an idea seriously and push downward using only physics and mathematics, and the constraints that follow often help the idea itself far more than an objection that presupposes different premises.

Good rules have one more criterion, the symmetry described in Chapter 2: they stay invariant under a change of unit of action. The unit can be an individual, an organization, a combination of human and AI. A rule about veto rights, if it still holds when the individual is replaced by an organization, is a rule about any collaborative system rather than an accidental fact about one kind of participant.

### How humans and AI research together

This book is itself the product of joint research between a human and several AIs. My way of working is this: on the same question, discuss separately with several AIs and hand each one's answers to the others; on a project, have different models act as researcher, planner, coder and evaluator. A few lessons came out of this process.

First, when multiple intelligences cooperate, they must avoid believing the same mistake twice over. Several models may share the same systematic bias, in which case their agreement is not evidence but noise; what is truly valuable is disagreement from independent sources. So I ask the AI for the strongest objection. The question-and-answer sections at the end of the chapters of this book came from exactly this.

Second, how research begins when there is no standard answer. Theoretical innovation has no clear right answer the way problem-solving does, but it has the ruler of Chapter 1: a shorter description, a less effortful construction, more external connections. Whether a new concept deserves to be kept depends on whether it makes previously invisible relations visible, and whether it creates operable bridges between previously independent fields.

Third, a grand vision must be tested by phenomena it played no part in designing, and these tests are mostly small, concrete, and capable of failing. So the research record must include failures, disagreements and costs, not only results. This book's knowledge base preserves, from every round of discussion, the AI's objections, my rebuttals, the corrections I accepted and the corrections I rejected, each marked separately. These negative results and disagreements are precisely the kind of data Chapter 10 said the old system cannot produce.

Fourth, the research process itself should be solidified into a reusable capability. I am still at the stage of interacting manually with several AIs. The next step is to automate this process, letting the roles of research, planning, development and evaluation advance automatically, with results shared, branched and merged through the version repository; once this mechanism works properly, extend the project from individual development to community collaboration. The mark of a project's maturity is that its development process is ultimately completed, through iteration, by its own capability.

Science, education and creation are one and the same thing in this ecology: every exploration by every node becomes a capability the whole network can reuse; every failure becomes the whole network's memory; every person is a student of this process, and its teacher, and its author.
