## Chapter 10: Personal Digital Space, Records, and Data Sovereignty

### A dashcam for one's life

The foundation of the previous chapter, besides carrying public code and data, must also carry every person. Every trace of behavior and every data product that a person leaves in digital space, what he said, what he did, what assets and abilities he has, what ideas he has, everything that left a digital imprint, whether notes, articles, the audio and video recorded by the devices he carries, or conversations with agents, is all recorded. It amounts to a dashcam and black box for one's life.

This person's proxy agent knows all of his interests, beliefs, demands, rights and obligations, and his relationships with other people; when he takes part in economic, political and social activity, it uses the reasoning power of AI to give him equally powerful judgment and game-playing ability. Only then can the cooperation and competition of the whole society become a fully equal, fully rational game.

A person's digital space is part of that person's capability. Whoever controls this space controls that person's capability in the game.

### The rules of viewing permissions

The design goal of this scheme is to preserve as much information as possible that benefits the evolution of the whole system, on the premise that no substantive harm is done to individual interests. So viewing permissions are the core. The rules are as follows:

1. By default, each person has the right to view only the historical information he could himself see and hear in his own life. No one has the right to view information he could not, by his own subjective capacity, have collected. This is fundamentally different from the present way of doing things: at present, the collectors and managers of information can view other people's information at will.
2. Viewing of public information is event-driven. Only when an event has more than one participant, when the event requires a fair judgment and decision to produce a resolution, and when this cannot be done with one person's information alone, does a fair algorithm compute the set of individuals involved, and only after obtaining the consent and authorization of those individuals is the right to view obtained. And the whole process, from viewing the information to reaching the judgment and ruling and the resolution, is preserved as common knowledge.

These two rules put into practice the boundary described in Chapter 4: a person's historical field of view becomes a concrete permission; a multi-party event obtains evidence without generating unlimited access rights. Collection devices obey the same constraint: on entering a setting where the new mechanism is not in effect, they default to off, and when switched on manually a conspicuous signal lights up, giving other people the right to know and to object; the device's keys and firmware version can be verified, which constrains the device's manufacturer and administrators.

### Why people will trust it

Because the entity behind it is not a traditional company but an organization that runs by the new rules, which can guarantee two things. First, all information and all behavior are preserved as traceable, tamper-proof records, so any action that infringes on a user's interests is reliably recorded. Second, everyone's personal income is distributed according to need, so no one can obtain any extra return from encroaching on users' interests. The former guarantees that no one has the ability to infringe on users' interests without leaving a trace; the latter guarantees that no one has the motive to.

How does a new organization prove it deserves trust? Not by promises, but by a record that begins growing on the first day of its founding and that no single node can control.

### How records change both before and after

For deliberate, malicious harm, "behavior being reliably recorded" serves not only for detection and accountability after the fact; it also has a preventive effect beforehand. Tit-for-tat is common knowledge: every person knows that his malicious behavior will be recorded, and that it will bring punishment. As long as the loss inflicted by punishment after the fact exceeds the benefit gained, this rule has a preventive effect beforehand on any rational person. In the future everyone will have his own AI agent to supplement his rational judgment, and the great majority of behavior will be carried out or mediated by agents; with this layer of buffering, emotional acts of harm will also be greatly reduced.

For harm that is not deliberately malicious, the kind caused by one's own limitations or by external force majeure, this mechanism is equally important. The occurrence of harm can never be completely eliminated, but behavior being reliably recorded and processed is the precondition for the whole system to reduce repeated mistakes and to gain in wit from every fall.

What people fear is not that their personal information and behavior are recorded; what they fear is that the ability to view and exploit that information falls into the hands of a few and becomes a weapon against them.

One more point determines the new system's advantage in data. However fierce the old system's collection, it can only get what people are willing to expose: finished products, successes, the outward story. What it cannot get is failures, abandoned plans, reasons for changing one's mind, because under competition and accountability, hiding these is rational. And that is precisely the data most lacking for building theory and correcting errors, and it is also the training material that the Large Civilization Model of the previous Part needs most. The data advantage of the new system lies not in "more", but in a class of data the old system is structurally unable to produce: decisions, outcomes, reversals, and the "why" alongside them.

### Unalterable history, with retraction and deletion

Records are append-only, rules can change, and the archive will outlive any version of the rules. So on top of the unalterable history there must be retraction and deletion: a record can be marked as withdrawn, an authorization can be revoked, a piece of data can be removed from public view, and these operations are themselves recorded. What unalterability guarantees is that "what happened" cannot be forged, not that "anyone can see everything forever". Once these two things are separated, a person can live, as a continually changing human being, inside a traceable memory: your record of today will not be tampered into something else, and your choice of tomorrow will not be locked down by the record of yesterday.

### Taking data back from the giants

I once applied to a large-model company to export all of my conversation data, and the reply was that it might take several days. If there is computing power to support massive volumes of real-time conversation, how can it fail to support a simple package-and-download? This difference in treatment of computing power and storage between the paid inference service and the user's data export shows that the service provider cares more about the business that makes it money than about the user's rights. This is the unfairness that arises when power between the service provider and the user is unequal.

The first thing the new mechanism does is to establish, for every user, local and cloud computing and storage space that the user can completely control, and take back the data that was scattered among the information silos of the various commercial giants. This involves three successive tasks. Recovering the historical accumulation: obtaining personal records from different platforms, clients and formats while preserving original content and provenance. Restoring usability: rebuilding conversational relationships, timelines and semantic connections so that the recovered data can continue to be searched, computed on and collaborated with. Establishing a continuous, autonomous mode of production: newly produced records promptly enter the user-controlled space, and when switching models or service providers one can carry the existing state and keep working.

There is only one criterion for testing this direction: after cutting the connection to any one original service provider, can the user keep his records and knowledge structure, choose a different set of tools, and continue the original work?

My own knowledge base is the first landing point: more than a year of sessions and projects scattered across different computers and different AI platforms, organized into a personal knowledge base in Merkle-DAG, where every original utterance has a content identifier, a source and a time, AI replies and my own statements are marked separately, and every version can be checked. This book grew out of that base.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: A complete record of a person's cognition, plus a model that can predict him, is the most perfect target for manipulation.**

A: That is why this chapter's permission rules are not "record everything, publish everything", but "record completely, with the person controlling the scope of disclosure". By default only you can see your own field of view; multi-party events have their scope computed by algorithm and require authorization; every act of viewing is itself recorded. Manipulation relies on information asymmetry: one side can see, the other cannot. When viewing is symmetric, recorded and authorized, the manipulator is first seen by his target. As for threats from outside the system: content identity and distributed storage mean the record is not attached to any single node that can be taken over; and personal data sovereignty is a basic guarantee that is not conditional on how much one has contributed or whether one agrees with the philosophy.
