# Part IV: The Path

## Chapter 11: The Gap Between Technology and Institutions

### A startling fact

The first three Parts covered the principles, the construction and the foundation. This Part returns to reality: why we must start now, and where to start.

Since the Second World War, the rules of the political, economic and financial game between the nations of the world, and the political and economic systems within each nation, have seen almost no fundamental change. Technology over the same period has developed at breakneck speed. Information technology alone has gone through the waves of the computer, the integrated circuit, mobile communication, the internet and AI. From the late seventies to today, the world's total wealth has grown rapidly, and the gap between rich and poor has grown wider and wider: the share of total wealth held by the richest one-thousandth of the population in the United States tripled over thirty-odd years, and the share held by the richest one percent in China doubled in twenty. Every country shows fractured public opinion and rising nationalism, people everywhere are dissatisfied with their governments and their economic conditions, and conflict between nations continues without pause.

At the individual level, the technology dividend has improved people's objective conditions of survival; but the many negative effects of a governance system out of step with technological development, compounded by the information age's amplifying effect, in which bad news travels a thousand miles, have pushed ordinary people, and young people above all, into a collective slide in subjective well-being.

Look at the world today. On one side, we can manipulate single atoms, pack billions of transistors onto a chip the size of a fingernail, edit genes to treat disease, build spacecraft and head for space. On the other side, our societies are full of ideological conflict, political turmoil and brutal war, from road rage, domestic violence and labor conflicts at the small end, to riots in the streets and protracted wars at the large end, not to mention humanity's fear of nuclear weapons and of AI.

### The ability to manipulate the external world and the ability to control oneself

Chapter 4 gave the reason. For every other complex system, the data to be collected is not humanity itself; for the system of human civilization, the data to be collected is every person's information about themselves, and whether people are willing to provide it is the bottleneck. That is why humanity's ability to understand and transform nature runs so far ahead of its ability to understand and control its own social system.

If a civilization's ability to manipulate the external world does not match its ability to control itself, sooner or later it will do something self-harming or even self-destroying. It is like a child of immense strength but a fragile body, swinging a thousand-pound chain flail: sooner or later it hits itself. In little more than a century humanity has already been through two world wars, the Cold War and the nuclear crisis, and now stands in the middle of the confrontation between China and America and the AI race, each side afraid that whoever masters AI first will destroy the other, each straining to get ahead, exactly like the nuclear arms race of old.

Technology can help and can harm, and there are two root causes: the reliability of the technology itself, and the reliability of the civilizational entity that holds and uses it. The present situation makes the second factor the weakest link. If energy and resources are all poured into the capability and reliability of technology instead of into raising the reliability of civilization itself, the gap between the two will only keep widening, and the final result is climbing a tree to catch fish, driving south to reach the north.

### The root of the old institutions' disease

This gap is not accidental. Chapters 3 and 4 already explained its mechanism: the inequality of power in the pyramid structure produces unfair distribution, unfair distribution produces distrust, distrust produces a still larger power gap, and so on around the loop. Technological progress speeds up the creation of wealth, and in this loop the creation of wealth only speeds up the divergence. The technology dividend has not automatically turned into shared happiness. That is not a problem of technology; it is a problem of institutions.

The old institutions are hard to change, and that too has its mechanism. Historically, replacing an institution has usually meant violent revolution. A person of the old system possesses a life experience long adapted to the old system; they have never really experienced the new mechanism. People resist and doubt what is unknown and unexperienced; that is the nature of seeking gain and avoiding harm. Chapter 14 will explain how the new system provides prior experience, experience that people can take part in and verify.

### A higher-order regulatory mechanism

The direction of evolution is to use higher-order modes of regulation to take over the situations that the lower-level modes of regulation cannot handle. This does not mean the lower-level modes become obsolete; they go on handling their own business at their own levels.

The human nervous system is layered in just this way. The autonomic nerves control circulation, breathing and digestion without passing through the brain; trained actions become muscle memory and skip the thinking; only complex tasks are controlled by the reason of the brain. Decisions driven purely by instinct are often bad for the body: instinct pushes a person to eat as much as possible, and in today's world of material abundance that means eating oneself fatter and fatter. A mature person can, according to the situation, combine the brain's high-level regulation with the body's instinctive drives.

The same holds for civilization. The natural regulation of the market and the natural power games between people are like physiological instinct: they can complete the vast majority of the economic and political activity of daily social life. But the market economy produces economic crises and financial crises: once an industry shows a high rate of return, everyone piles in; the AI boom has driven up the price of memory and chips, until ordinary people who want to add a computer find they can no longer afford one. It is as if a person thinking at full capacity, without sound central regulation, let all the nutrients flow to the brain: the other organs would be starved.

What illustrates the point best is the cytokine storm. Faced with a foreign virus, the body could respond strategically and gently, but the immune system sometimes launches an attack with everything it has: kill eight hundred of the enemy, lose a thousand of your own. Many people end up killed by the cytokine storm itself. Bats have a strong capacity to coexist with viruses; after infection they do not die in large numbers. Violent conflicts between nations and between groups are the cytokine storm of civilization: there is no need at all to spend so many resources on the attack, yet because the other side is treated as an outsider that must be wiped out completely, what ends up harmed is oneself.

The mechanism I envision is that higher-order regulation at the level of civilization: use collective reason, everyone thinking together and dynamic weights then selecting the consensus plan, to counteract the crises and violent conflicts that low-level mechanisms such as the market, finance and power games can produce at the macro scale, so that the price of civilization's evolution is smaller and its course smoother. This is also the match between productive forces and relations of production: steam and electricity gave birth to the capitalist system, which in turn further released the productive forces; now, with computers and AI, one more layer of regulation can be built above the layer of capital, regulating capital no longer through capital alone, but through knowledge and intelligence.

### We do not have much time left

AI has already begun to acquire a preliminary capacity to iterate and evolve on its own. China and the United States are caught in something like a prisoner's dilemma: confronting and distrusting each other, neither is willing to stop accelerating AI research, the acceleration of AI is irreversible, and the day of the technological singularity draws closer and closer. Once the technological singularity arrives while the mismatch between civilization's institutions and technological development remains unsolved, the gap between the two will widen sharply, and the probability that humanity, out of fear of AI or out of fear of the few who control AI, walks into self-destruction will rise sharply along with it.

So within this brief window, we must design, as fast as we can, a system of human civilization that has the same self-iterating, self-upgrading mechanism as AI systems, so that the speed of civilization's evolution matches the speed of technology's evolution, and the singularity of civilization and the technological singularity arrive together.

### Humanity's road is not just two tracks

In the science fiction work *Watchmen*, in order to pull the United States and the Soviet Union back from the brink of nuclear war, the smartest man in the world creates a more powerful common enemy, using an external threat to force all sides into peace. The tension between them does not disappear; it is only pressed together by a larger outside force. He replaces a greater loss with a smaller one, a solution of the trolley-problem kind. In my view this is not the best course. Because the trajectory of human society's evolution is not a track laid down in advance, and the choice is not between the two tracks of crushing one person or crushing a crowd.

The trolley problem is a dilemma because it presupposes tracks. A civilization that can correct itself, explore in parallel, and move its conflicts out of reality into virtual space to make trial and error there faces not two tracks but an open field that can be laid out anew. Human history has seen countless disagreements that looked impossible to resolve, and that were then unified or replaced by new institutions, knowledge and technologies as understanding and technology advanced. In disagreements in the field of technology, what is lost is only intellect and computing power; in political disagreements, once the machinery of violence joins in, what is lost is human life. What a better meta-strategy has to achieve is exactly this: the transformation of institutions no longer costs human lives.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: If a more advanced mechanism can only be built by first destroying the old one, then the losses it causes during the transition should be counted in its own total cost.**

A: Agreed, and this is exactly why I insist on a non-violent transition. When comparing two civilizational mechanisms, we cannot compare only their efficiency once they are running stably; we must also compare the costs of learning, migration, reorganization and risk that moving from one mechanism to the other necessarily entails. As long as the new mechanism is even slightly stronger than the old one, people will switch of their own accord; what we need is not the energy to destroy the old system, but a reduction in the energy the switch requires. That is what the engineering route of Chapter 9, compatibility first, then replacement, means at the level of institutions.
