## Chapter 3: Tit-for-Tat: The Iteration Operator That Constructs Social Structure

### What social science lacks is a basic building block

The reason social science lags so far behind natural science in precision and reliability is that it has never found the properties and laws of its basic building block. What this chapter sets out to do is find that building block, and show how it constructs the various structures of society.

Take the analogy between human society and a chemical system. The basic object of a chemical system is the atom, and atoms connect through chemical bonds to form all kinds of molecules; the rules for forming chemical bonds are determined by the properties of the atom's outer electrons, the whole process follows energy minimization, and it finally reaches a stable fixed-point state. In human society, the basic object is the person, and the simplest connection between one person and another is cooperation. Cooperation is reached through repeated games driven by each side's own interests, and that process likewise follows the principle of Chapter 1: the Nash equilibrium is a fixed point in strategy; Pareto optimality is the minimized state of resource allocation, where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

So what inner property of the human individual corresponds to the outer electron structure of the atom? The answer is a piece of common knowledge rooted in our physiology and psychology and universally observed in games: tit-for-tat. The strategy was proposed by Rapoport, and it won twice in Axelrod's computer tournaments of the repeated prisoner's dilemma: cooperate on the first move; after that, if the other side cooperates, I cooperate; if the other side defects, I punish; if the other side comes back, I forgive. It is nice, provocable and forgiving, and so clear that the other side understands it at a glance.

### Reward and punishment are the evaluation function, tit-for-tat is the iteration operator

Apply the principle of Chapter 1 here: the game between people is the dynamic process that constructs the fixed-point structures of human society. The mechanism of reward and punishment is an evaluation function; tit-for-tat is the iteration process of that function. It makes structures which satisfy the evaluation function tend to converge, and structures which do not tend to separate, instead of combining and separating at random without ever forming a stable structure. Through tit-for-tat, people reward cooperation and punish defection, which sets off chain reactions that keep strengthening or weakening their ties, continually constructing every kind of relational structure between people, just as atoms form chemical bonds by losing, gaining and sharing their outer electrons. This chain effect of positive and negative feedback is the basic reaction of human social construction.

Its operation is controlled jointly by three layers, physiological, psychological and rational. At the bottom is the physiological hardware: when an individual gains from cooperation, the brain's reward circuits produce pleasure; when his interests are damaged by betrayal, the stress system produces fear and anger. In the middle is emotion, which is a fast heuristic algorithm for decision-making and supplies the motivation for rational thought. At the top is reason: driven by emotion, it runs a cost-benefit analysis and finally returns reward for goodwill and punishment for malice.

### Strictness: the first operational definition

Tit-for-tat strategies of different intensity affect the speed of convergence, the strength and the stability of the fixed-point structure. This is the first operational definition this book gives to social science: **strictness, meaning the intensity of punishment for defection and the time interval required before forgiveness.**

Strictness is a continuous spectrum from lenient to strict. The universality of this spectrum shows itself in the fact that human structures, at whatever level and in whatever concrete organization, always differentiate from lenient to strict. The members of a family, a corporate board, the parliament of a democracy, the politburo of an authoritarian state, any association at all: among the individuals taking part there is always a spectrum from doves to hawks.

This differentiation has ready-made mathematics. The hawk-dove game of evolutionary game theory tells us: when the contested gain is smaller than the cost of conflict, the group stabilizes in a mixed state of hawks and doves, and the proportion of hawks equals the ratio of the gain to the cost. The proportion is set by the payoff structure, regardless of who the individuals are.

### Who stands on which side: genes determine the ordering, the game determines the cut-off point

So what does who the individuals are decide? It decides who crosses the line first.

I split a person's decision into two parts: the part that is rational thought, and an emotional amplification factor. The latter is a physiological parameter that sets the intensity and duration of emotional response. Different people respond to betrayal with emotions of different intensity and duration; in the execution of tit-for-tat, that difference shows up as different punishment intensity and different forgiveness intervals; in an actual game, one kind behaves more like a hawk and the other more like a dove. But the effect size of a single gene is tiny, and it depends heavily on the environment a person grows up in; taken directly as an explanation of political systems, it does not stand.

The correct position is this: genes determine the ordering, the game determines the cut-off point. Run a thought experiment: cut out every hawk in a group; the remaining people get re-ordered by the relative intensity of their emotional responses, and as the game iterates in chain reaction, the relatively more reactive portion differentiates into hawks again, and the proportion drifts back to the neighborhood set by the ratio of the gain to the cost of conflict. Genetically identical mice in identical environments still form stable dominance and subordination hierarchies, which shows that differentiation itself happens even without genetic differences: iterated games amplify tiny random differences into stable roles, which is symmetry breaking; when genetic differences exist, they set the starting point of the amplification. The type is individual; the proportion is game-theoretic.

### Why authoritarianism is a stable attractor

At the level of the group, the acquired environment of the game matters far more than genes.

By the principle of minimization, people always tend to resolve disagreements in the way that costs least. In an environment that is already under authoritarian rule, the power gap is enormous, violent suppression is the cheapest method, and so people tend to solve problems by violence. This happens again and again. On one side, those inclined to such methods become more successful within the system and are rewarded, and the reward encourages more people to adopt such methods; on the other side, violent suppression builds up enormous rebound tension in the suppressed, and that tension pushes those in power to keep widening the power gap in order to increase structural stability; the widened power gap takes us back to the iteration we started from. The whole structure converges to an authoritarian system of enormous size, enormous power gap and extremely high structural strength. This is a positive feedback loop, with a clear mechanism at every step, relying on no narrative of "evil rulers," derived purely from the principle of minimization, selection pressure and feedback loops. It explains why authoritarian systems are so stable: they are attractors, and every iteration reinforces them.

When hard strategies are cheap in an authoritarian environment, cheap for whom? Cheap for those above, extremely expensive for those below. So that environment does not just push everyone's baseline toward hardness; it binds strategy to position: hawk downward, dove upward. An arrangement that decides who yields by position rather than by strength saves the cost of fighting, which is why it is stable, and that is exactly where the pyramid is cheap. The same logic explains the hawks in the democratic camp: violence is expensive at home and cheap abroad, because outsiders have no channel for appeal or retaliation, and so the same people are lenient inside and hard outside. The history of colonialism is the product of this payoff structure.

Conversely, in the environment of a democratic system, violent suppression is the more expensive method, and people tend more toward peaceful communication, playing against each other with lenient tit-for-tat; even a person whose emotions are more intense will tend toward lenient methods in that environment. The payoff structure of the environment sets the baseline of the group's strategy and the proportion between the two camps; individual differences decide who crosses the line first at the critical state.

### Two higher fixed points

In a space shaped by the overlapping influence of geographical, physiological, psychological, economic and political factors, human civilization has evolved two important higher fixed-point structures: the authoritarian system and the democratic system. They correspond to the strict version and the lenient version of the tit-for-tat strategy.

The structural strength and stability of the strict strategy are enough to support a mighty empire. But an overly strong single structure usually slows the pace of a civilization's evolution, and the collapse and reconstruction of such a structure is enormously costly: in China every dynastic change has been accompanied by massive loss of life, and even so, it was only a change of dynasty. High strictness means fast convergence and strong stability, and it also means being easily trapped in a locally optimal fixed point and unable to climb out; a structure that is too fragile will not do either. How to construct a sensible mechanism of reward and punishment is the fundamental factor in how strong a human civilization's capacity to evolve will be.

Geographical parameters have to be counted too. In the relatively isolated geography of East Asia, with its large continuous farming regions, the population was tied to the land and exit was extremely expensive; only these factors combined with the positive feedback above produced the continuous development of the authoritarian system. In the fragmented, open environment of the Mediterranean, with its diverse and mobile mix of farming, herding and sea trade, exit was cheap, cooperation had to invent portable reputation, and so horizontal institutions grew up: merchant law, credit, guilds. The parameter of exit cost will be used again in Chapter 13, when I discuss the free zones. Only with these concrete parameters substituted in does this theory become a system of social science that can be tested against the data of human history and is falsifiable.

### Why preserving difference is more powerful than eliminating it

Leading in energy-efficiency ratio only means leading overall in most situations; it does not mean leading in every local respect. Nor is the strict strategy inferior to the lenient strategy in every situation: when forces must be concentrated to complete quickly a large task that is structurally complex but highly deterministic, the former is more efficient. Even the armies of democratic countries are based on the strict strategy; in wartime, governments tighten centralization. So while tilting overall toward the strategy with the higher energy-efficiency ratio, diversity must be preserved, and the lenient strategy preserves diversity better.

Chapter 6 will describe a virtual version of tit-for-tat: it moves reward and punishment from material goods and violence in reality to points in a digital space, turning real damage into virtual damage in a game.

### Crime is a disease, not evil

From the mechanism of this chapter a conclusion about crime follows. If you regard crime as evil and the criminal as a demon, then at the very bottom of your cognition you are already bound by the emotion of moral judgment; you will be strongly inclined to wipe it out directly by violence and to refuse to understand it and correct it through scientific research. Simple violent elimination can only eliminate the individuals already produced; it cannot eliminate the mechanism that produces them. This and "cutting out half the hawks" are two faces of the same thing: what changes is the individuals, not the proportion.

To regard crime as a disease is to put this phenomenon into the framework of science, where treatment and prevention aim at the mechanism: reduce the gains that can be seized, raise the cost of harmful acts, insert a delay between punishment and emotion, leave room for appeal against misjudgment. "Disease" here means a phenomenon that can be studied, prevented and corrected; what gets recorded is the harmful act, not a category pinned on the person. This is the direct application, in the criminal field, of the meta-cognition from Chapter 2.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The conditions under which Axelrod proved that tit-for-tat wins, a two-player game, a fixed payoff matrix, long repetition, no communication, are not strictly satisfied in most real organizations.**

A: That is exactly why I do not treat "tit-for-tat" as a concrete strategy table but as the iteration operator of the evaluation function. In any sufficiently repeated multi-party game, the participants evolve some mechanism of reward and punishment to stabilize cooperation; conditional cooperation, indirect reciprocity, reputation mechanisms, third-party punishment are all different instances of this operator under more complex game structures. They all present a strict-lenient differentiation precisely because any reward-punishment operator can be parameterized along a strictness dimension. Strictness is the control parameter of the operator; convergence speed and stability are its outputs. As for the differences between a family and a parliament in kinship, symmetry of power, number of repetitions and exit cost, those are exactly what I called the concrete parameters: they change the operator's values in that situation, not the operator itself.
