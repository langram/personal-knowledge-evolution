## Chapter 8: The Large Civilization Model: A Civilization in Which Training and Inference Are One

### Resources are limited, so attention is necessary

How does the social neural network of Chapter 6 run? We can borrow a concrete shape from the most successful architecture of intelligence today.

The attention mechanism of the Transformer resembles the human brain in three ways. First, resources are limited, and weights are used to focus selectively on the factors that matter more: reading a book in a noisy cafe, the brain automatically filters out the background noise; an AI model processing a long text likewise does not treat every word alike, but concentrates its weights on the content most relevant to the current task. Second, out of a vast, complex and changing body of information, it builds accurate contextual associations through dynamic weights: on reading "that apple is really sweet," a human and an AI both connect it with fruit, not with a phone. Third, it links an enormous number of information objects by matching queries against content.

The architecture of human civilization built on decision-making by dynamic weights can be given the same structure. On one side, the root agent belonging to each person maps out a personal space covering that individual and his private resources; the person and his own agents work together to construct an attention mechanism at the personal level, and carry out self-management and self-evolution. On the other side, these personal spaces together make up the whole civilizational space, and at that level the attention mechanism of civilization is constructed. In human civilization, we cannot have every person and every model think equally deeply about every problem at the same time; what the attention mechanism has to organize is the meeting of the relevant people, knowledge and tools under limited time, computing power and communication cost.

### Objects change, so we must enter the runtime

But we cannot copy the algorithms of AI models the way the man in the old story carved a notch in his boat to mark where his sword fell overboard. The objects a model handles are static: the semantic nodes in the vocabulary do not increase, decrease or change. The real world is not like that. The people and external objects of human society change as decisions and behavior change; they can diminish, multiply, change their properties, even be destroyed. Non-renewable resources shrink by exactly what is consumed; the rewards and punishments a person receives change his future decision tendencies. The former is like a collection of programs; the latter is a more complex system that contains the entire runtime. So besides the algorithms of probability and statistics, we need the principles of game theory; and whichever model is used, computing its variables requires sufficiently abundant and accurate data on the objects in the system. This brings us back to the bottleneck of Chapter 4: the active cooperation of all participants.

The minimal process of a common affair has five steps: the relevant objects are discovered from the problem; the relevant capability nodes each propose candidate plans, carrying their weights; a plan executes once it has the authorization of the parties it touches; the result of execution is recorded; and the result feeds back into the weight of every node that took part. Discovery, proposal, authorization, execution, feedback: this is the concrete shape of the dynamic-weight mechanism inside the runtime.

Weights must attach to problems, not to people, and they must expire. Before a technology appears, no node has any record of judgment about it; after a kind of resource is exhausted, all the weights built around it lose their object. A game has an ending; a runtime has none. It has only persistent state and a constant arrival of new things.

### The answers lie in the future: training and inference cannot be separated

Set this civilizational brain, with humans and AI agents as its neuron nodes and dynamic decision weights as its algorithm, beside an AI large language model, and you find that the two processes of learning, training and inference in practice are fractals of one structure at different levels: the civilizational body is the same kind of thing as a large language model, at a higher order and a higher level. The difference is that at the level of the civilizational body the input is not text but every problem, large and small, that the whole civilization meets and needs to solve.

These problems are of two kinds. For one kind, the knowledge system of human civilization already has the correct answer, and it is enough to call directly on the intelligence already integrated in the large language models and solve it by inference. The other kind matters more: the problems human civilization has met and is meeting in its development that still have no correct or ideal answer. For this kind, training and inference cannot be separated the way they are for a large language model, with pre-training done off the job. In conventional training the answers to all the training material are already known; the model's inference can be compared with the answer again and again, the error back-propagated, and the model corrected over and over until training is complete.

The training material of a civilizational body is not like that. The correct answers to all its problems lie in the future. The result of one inference is not the final answer but one branch of a trial strategy on the way to the final answer, one state node on one branch of the whole search tree of evolutionary paths. In machine learning, the theories that deal with this kind of problem are called online learning and reinforcement learning; the best precedent is the AI that plays board games: it has no standard answers, it generates its data by playing against itself, and its training and search are interwoven.

In this higher-level social intelligent neural system the nodes are not the semantic nodes of a large language model either; they are every human and every AI agent. Each person has a dedicated AI, used to build a personal knowledge base and to produce a personal cognitive model: it takes the civilizational body's consensus knowledge as its base semantic model, adds this person's own semantic and cognitive preferences, and represents the structure of this person's cognitive space. These dedicated personal models act as individual agents, either with the person taking part actively or by automatic proxy in a default mode; they join the game, give their opinions, and become neurons in the brain of the whole civilization. The civilizational system assigns different weights to the decisions of these different nodes, then gathers the decisions of all the neurons to make collective inferences and decisions.

The reward function used for back-propagation therefore cannot be set by "the distance from the final answer". It is set by reality: how the results these decisions produce when practiced in the real world compare with the earlier plan, how far they fall from what people foresaw and expected, how large the gap is between the results of different decision plans, how satisfied all the people taking part are, and so on.

So this is not an off-the-job pre-training process. It is a loop in which learning, training and inference iterate again and again against the feedback of productive practice. Civilization lives inside this loop, practicing, constructing new knowledge and evolving all at once. Back-propagation in this process updates three things: the decision weights of the nodes across the whole social intelligent neural network; the civilizational body's consensus knowledge model, updated with the new knowledge; and the weights of the semantic nodes in every person's dedicated AI model, and, once brain-computer interface technology matures, in every human brain itself.

### The Large Civilization Model: every person has a vector in civilization space

By this point you will have noticed that this civilizational system is itself a model, a Large Civilization Model. In the high-dimensional space of civilization, each person's dedicated cognitive-semantic model is an object like a token in a large language model, and the relations among these objects are like the relations among meanings in a large language model. Every meaning has an embedding in a language model; every person has an embedding in civilization. This is exactly the cognitive reference frame of Chapter 2 given coordinates for the first time. So we can construct a large model of a higher order than the language model, at the level of civilization.

When this civilization-level social intelligent neural network runs, it has two outputs. First, it can train a better large language model that belongs to all of humanity. Second, and more important, in the very act of running it collects all the training material the Large Civilization Model needs: every problem, every node's judgment, every collective decision, every real-world result is a training record. As the new civilizational order continues to iterate and evolve, the Large Civilization Model is constructed within it, and it in turn helps us construct more complex, more advanced civilizational structures and institutions.

### Data and labels: every person's judgment is a label

In training a large language model, besides the collection of data, the labeling of data is just as crucial; it is even one of the factors that decide the level of knowledge and ability of the model that comes out. The same holds for the Large Civilization Model. Everything that each person in the social intelligent neural network produces over the course of his life, all the audio, video and text, together with the personal cognitive model that his dedicated AI generates on that basis, is data the Large Civilization Model needs for training. Then where do the labels come from? From these same people: each person's assessments and evaluations of this data, his own and other people's, and his decisions and answers to the problems he meets, are the labels on this data. The collection of all this labeled data from the whole of humanity is the best training data for civilization's common knowledge model and for the Large Civilization Model.

So the social intelligent neural network under the new civilizational order is two things at once: it is the best system for mining and collecting this data and these labels, and it is the best system for learning, training and inference in practice. How this data and these labels are collected without harming the individual is the business of Chapter 10.

### From computing power to social mechanics

Humanity in fact already possesses the most powerful tools for studying complex emergent phenomena. Chapter 1 said that "more is different": basic objects of a lower order, through combination, give rise to complex structures of a higher order. How do we model and study such phenomena, then propose a theory, and finally put it into practice and create new emergence? The growth of computing power in recent decades has given humanity a way: large numbers of high-performance computers doing computation on enormous high-dimensional tensors, carrying the whole process from mathematical modeling and analysis through theory-building to practice. Quantum mechanics, fluid mechanics, condensed matter physics, the construction of complex engineering works, and the development of AI itself have all benefited from this.

So far, the training of large AI models is the most computationally demanding task humanity has ever accomplished. In the Large Civilization Model we face next, every unit is itself a high-dimensional semantic model, and the complexity will certainly be several orders of magnitude higher. But I believe that humanity, with the help of AI, will be able to accomplish it. In the course of this social neural network's evolution, we will gradually work out the principles and models of the "mechanics" by which human nodes of different cognition, in the high-dimensional space of civilization, act on one another in real society, and then use these principles of "social mechanics" to construct more advanced and more complex social structures in human civilization, just as people use the principles of mechanics to build ever more complex high-rise buildings. Newton's principles gave humanity the mechanics to raise great buildings; the "mathematical principles" in this book's title are meant to arrive, in the end, at this social mechanics.

This is what the "bitter lesson" of the preface means at the level of civilization. Solving the problems of constructing and organizing society by the personal experience and learning of authorities amounts to hand-writing expert knowledge into the system; relying on mathematical modeling, enormous records and computation, and training in the feedback of practice, is the approach that wins in the long run.

### Freedom, equality and democracy: from slogans to computable models

Chapter 1 said that even the demands made at the level of politics can be interpreted by the principle of minimization and maximization: freedom corresponds to the maximization of the individual's relations with other objects, democracy to the maximization of the emergent higher-order group organization, and equality is the precondition that allows individual interest and group interest to be maximized at the same time. With the Large Civilization Model, that sentence can be taken one step further.

Under the old system, freedom, democracy and equality are philosophical concepts and political slogans. Every school has its own interpretation and none can convince another, because these things cannot be measured, and so cannot be compared. The social intelligent neural network under the new civilizational order makes it possible, for the first time, to turn these concepts into quantifiable mathematical models and computable strategy algorithms.

First, what each of them is. The three concepts are three different types of mathematical object: freedom is a quantity, equality is a symmetry, democracy is an aggregation operator.

Freedom has two layers. The first is sovereignty: within the kind of resources that serves individual need, the objective function that each person and his dedicated cognitive model maximize in all their decisions is defined by that person himself, and everyone else's weight here is zero. The second is the size of the reachable future. The objective function says what a person wants; freedom says how much of the future lies within his reach. A person who has been steered into choosing "voluntarily" can score high on his objective function and still not be free. For this layer the mathematics already exists. Research on agents has a quantity called empowerment: the channel capacity between an agent's actions and the states it will later find itself in, a measure of how many distinguishable futures it can bring about. Sen's "capability" in economics says the same thing: freedom is the set of lives a person is actually able to achieve. This quantity can be computed and can be compared between one person and another, and it is the measure of that sentence in Chapter 2: at every moment of evolution the individual must retain the actual ability to change the direction of its trajectory. It also agrees with the optimal marginal utility of Chapter 5: resources beyond that interval add nothing to the reachable future. So the full statement is this: the individual maximizes his own objective function within his personal domain; the civilizational body takes each person's reachable future as one term in its own objective function and maximizes it without reducing anyone else's; and the job of the dedicated AI is to widen each person's feasible region.

Equality is the symmetry of the rules. Chapter 2 already gave the formulation: swap any two people within a rule, and the content of the rule stays unchanged. Social choice theory calls this property anonymity. In the Large Civilization Model it has a very clean counterpart: a Transformer applies the same set of parameters to every token, and tokens differ only in their embeddings. Equality is parameter sharing; diversity is the difference between embeddings. Division of labor and roles are the result of symmetry breaking as the system runs, not something written into the rules in advance.

Democracy is the aggregation operator that, in affairs concerning the collective kind of resources, takes individual judgments to a collective decision. Its most classical mathematical ground is Condorcet's jury theorem: when many judgments that are independent of one another, each slightly better than a random guess, are aggregated by majority, the probability of being right approaches one as the number of people grows. The theorem has a weighted form: when people differ in accuracy, the optimal aggregation is a weighted majority in which each person's weight is proportional to the log-odds of his accuracy. In other words, the dynamic weights of Chapter 6 are not a departure from democracy but the optimal form of Condorcet's principle; one person, one vote is the special case in which everyone's accuracy is the same or cannot be told apart. Democracy here is three conditions: every person's judgment has a path into the result; the result responds positively to its inputs; and no single node can determine the result alone. The theorem also requires that people's judgments be independent of one another, which is exactly why this book insists on diversity again and again.

Taken together, the three form a constrained optimization problem: under the constraint that the rules are symmetric, maximize each person's reachable future, with decisions in the collective domain given by aggregation weighted by track record. Chapter 1 said that equality is the precondition for maximizing individual interest and group interest at the same time; here that is literally true: symmetry is the constraint that makes the two levels of optimization compatible. Nor is the qualifier "personal resources" dispensable. Sen proved an impossibility theorem: if each person's preferences about other people's private affairs are also counted in collective decisions, then reserving for just two people the right to decide one private matter each already conflicts with the weakest collective standard there is, that an option everyone prefers should be adopted. The division into two kinds of resources in Chapter 5 is precisely what takes the personal domain out of the aggregation.

### The initial values need not be correct; the correcting signal must be there

Whether the models and algorithms above are correct does not, in fact, matter. What matters is that the concepts and slogans that used to rest on philosophical thought can now become quantifiable mathematical models and computable strategy algorithms. In the cooperative social game of human civilization, in the course of the Large Civilization Model's iterative upgrading, we can let these models and algorithms come closer and closer to the correct form, and let the results of the game come closer and closer to the global optimum. Conversely, to pick one quantity for freedom and one set of weights for democracy and then announce that this is the answer is exactly what the "bitter lesson" describes: hand-writing expert knowledge into the system. What is written above is only a set of initial values.

There are precedents for this path. The thermometer came more than a hundred years before thermodynamics. The earliest scales were set arbitrarily, at a time when people had not yet worked out what heat even is; but once "hot and cold" could be measured, different definitions could be compared with and corrected against one another, and only at the end did absolute temperature emerge. Measurable first, correct later. Machine learning has a guarantee of the same form: the algorithm of Chapter 6 that weights by past performance does not need to know in advance who is right; it guarantees only that in the long run it will not be much worse than the one who, in hindsight, performed best.

Whether the initial form is right does not matter, but for the iteration to converge the correcting signal must be there from the first day. No one can supply a standard answer for "freedom", so whether one definition is "more correct" can only be judged in the way of this chapter: write the candidate definitions into the rules and run them as forks in different free zones; use the real-world results and the evaluations of all participants as labels, and compare the forks; what remains is the one that is still endorsed after everyone knows about it. This is the kind of law Chapter 2 spoke of: laws that still hold after everyone knows them.

So each of these three concepts has two layers. The fine layer, how the quantity is computed and how the weights are set, is left to the iteration. The minimal layer cannot wait to be learned by the iteration, because it is the precondition for the iteration itself to be trustworthy. This layer is three meta-rules:

- Equality: every person has the same standing under the rules in this process.
- Freedom: every person can exit and fork.
- Democracy: every person's judgment enters as a label.

They are the same kind of thing as the five rules of Chapter 4: they do not prescribe the outcome; they prescribe only how this game is played and how it corrects itself. The forks on a unified base and the free entry and free exit described in Chapter 13 are what these three look like at the starting stage.

Finally, "approaching the global optimum" should be understood as continuously tracking a moving optimum. As this chapter said earlier, a runtime has no ending; the objects change, and the optimum moves with them. What civilization needs is not to compute the end point once, but to keep up at all times.

### Artificial general intelligence is the whole civilizational body, not any one model

Chapter 7 said that true artificial general intelligence is not a large model in a data center but the whole network of dynamic weights. The chain of reasoning behind that judgment can now be completed.

Artificial general intelligence must have the ability to solve every problem that human civilization meets, including the self-evolution of civilization's own institutions. What that ability depends on is the civilizational body's own Large Civilization Model and humanity's common knowledge system. Their whole process, from data collection and labeling through learning and training to inference in practice and feedback, plainly requires the joint participation, in brainpower and computing power, of all human and AI agents and of every digital device involved in collecting and processing the data. So any supercomputer cluster in any super data center is no more than one local computing node in the whole civilizational body.

It is the same as when we call a human being a higher intelligent agent: we mean the whole living organism, not some super neuron or some super lobe in its brain.

### Start testing from the smallest cooperative scenario

This runtime does not need to be tested starting from a nation. The smallest cooperative scenario is two people, one task, and one verifiable record. Start there and observe: can the relevant people find each other, can misunderstandings be detected in time, can expert judgment be tested, can the results of cooperation be developed further by others. Then three people, one project, one organization. Chapter 13 describes exactly this road.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: The brain's dynamic weights work because there is a massive, fast stream of prediction-error signals. Most social decisions lack exactly this fast feedback. Without feedback on outcomes, dynamic weights only add noise.**

A: This is exactly why this book puts the record before the decision. The recording and permission system of Chapter 10, together with the rule from Chapter 4 that failure means compensation, correction and a record, exists precisely to manufacture prediction-error signals for social decisions. Under the old system, social decisions lack fast feedback not because the feedback does not physically exist, but because the pyramid mutes it: bad news that travels upward hurts the messenger, so it does not travel; errors the decision-maker knows about but cannot admit must be actively covered up. A brain that can mute the pain signals it dislikes will walk on a broken leg. When the record does not depend on any single node, feedback can no longer be muted. Use it first in the fields where feedback is fast enough; record first in the fields where feedback is slow. That is a sequence, not an obstacle.
