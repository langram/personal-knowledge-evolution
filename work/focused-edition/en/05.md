# Part II: Construction

## Chapter 5: Two Kinds of Resources and Equality in the Sense of the Rules

### The interval of optimal marginal utility

Part I gave the principles and the basic operator. Starting with this chapter, I use them to construct. I begin with resources.

The Industrial Revolution gradually replaced human physical labor with automated equipment, and the abundance of material goods brought the basic needs of individual human survival to a state of basic satisfaction. As AI develops, intellectual labor will achieve higher efficiency too, and in the not distant future, every material and spiritual need of individual human survival can be supplied in full within the range of optimal marginal utility.

I mention optimal marginal utility because while too little supply is harmful to a living individual, too much supply is harmful as well. The water, food and nutritional balance a person needs fall within a range; the information the brain takes in, the intensity and duration of thinking, have an optimal interval too. Every person is a dissipative structure, and excessive distribution does not bring better results: fine food is good, but letting go of control and taking in too many calories damages health; however good short videos may be, drowning in them every day is certainly not good either. The biological instinct of a human being exists for survival in scarcity; in today's world of enormous material abundance, if instinct is left in charge, a person usually cannot make the best choices about material intake or spiritual intake.

So when productivity is strong enough, distribution in appropriate measure according to each individual's different needs and in keeping with his psychological and physiological health is clearly the more reasonable and fairer outcome.

### Where power-law distribution comes from

But in the present system of human civilization, once individuals and states gain power through free competition or through power games, the inequality of power produces an inequality in the distribution of gains from the cooperative and competitive game; add the repetition of the game over and over, and the gains of the advantaged side produce a compounding effect, finally forming a power-law distribution of power, wealth and resources. A power law does not grow out of human needs and abilities; it is the product of balance-breaking unequal behavior run through a long chain reaction: the inequality in any single round of the game is small; compounding stretches it into the long tail. The mathematics of this is clear: a process of proportional growth, accumulated over and over, yields a long-tailed distribution; the "rich get richer" mechanism is precisely the standard source of the power law.

This power-law distribution has its cause. For a group, wealth and resources serve two purposes: one part supports the survival of each individual in the group and satisfies the individuals' material and spiritual needs; the other part is consumed by the group's survival and operation as a whole. And how the group operates, how it distributes and consumes these resources, needs an intellectual center to take charge. The traditional system, whether authoritarian dictatorship or the alternation of parties in power, and likewise the power structure of a commercial company, is in essence a pyramid, a hierarchy with a power gradient. The elites standing at the apex, by virtue of their advantage in professional ability and the power and capital advantage accumulated through long games, gain the decision-making power over the operation of the whole group.

It must be admitted that the decision-maker who wins repeated competition is an individual of relatively greater ability and a higher probability of deciding correctly. The pyramid did not come from nowhere; under the conditions of limited human capacity for information processing, communication and coordination, it is a solution that works. The question is: is there a system of organization and decision-making with a higher probability of correct decisions? In the age of computers and AI, there is. It is the dynamic weights of the next chapter.

### Two kinds of resources, two kinds of distribution

So the principle of distribution in the new architecture is this: at the level of individual need, wealth and resources are distributed according to need, fairly, healthily and reasonably, and this part relates only to the individual's own physiological and psychological structure, not to his position in the game; at the collective level, wealth and resources are distributed and decided by the collective's dynamic-weight system.

On separating these two kinds of resources, China's land system is an example worth consulting. Land is the most fundamental resource for a civilization's infrastructure and economic development; airports, roads, high-speed rail, energy, factories, urban planning are the equivalent of an organism's circulatory system and metabolism. State ownership of land cuts down the wrangling, the haggling and the drawn-out attrition of expropriating private land. But it does only half the job: the distribution of the first kind of resource at the level of the individual is still the unequal distribution of the pyramid power structure; the power to control the second kind of resource is still the static, power-gapped, pyramid-style decision-making, still trapped in the stubborn chronic diseases of graft, corruption and bureaucracy.

### The precondition for machines to level the gaps in ability

Eliminating inequality used to be an impossible task, because everyone differs in age, intelligence and physical strength. But since the Industrial Revolution, whenever we invent a tool that replaces some human ability, whose performance exceeds the human one, and whose output is large enough for everyone to have one, the gap between people in that ability has been leveled. The mechanization of agriculture erased the gap in physical strength between farmers; navigation software erased the gap in the ability to find one's way. When AI surpasses humans in intelligence and becomes so widespread that everyone can access it freely, people's differences in intelligence will be leveled too.

But this has one precondition: all the knowledge needed for the principles and the manufacturing process of every tool that exceeds human capacity must be public knowledge shared by all humanity, not privately owned by any person, organization or state. Once it is privately owned, it is no longer a tool for erasing the gaps in ability; it becomes a tool by which some individual or organization gains a greater advantage in competition with other players. The possibility of such knowledge being monopolized must be ruled out at the root. That is the problem Chapter 9 is meant to solve.

### Equality does not require everyone to become the same person

People's abilities differ. That is a fact, and there is no need to dodge it. Innate differences even affect whether a person can keep investing effort in one direction, so that some people are narrow but extremely accomplished, while others know something of everything but lack depth in any one direction. So what matters is not making ability approach some particular distribution, but making the system's mechanism able to let individuals of these different abilities build the most effective cooperation, let specialists and generalists communicate effectively, and complete deep cross-disciplinary cooperation.

But a difference in ability and a difference in domination are two different things. The old system binds them together: a person is good at something, so he gets more resources; the resources buy more people's time and more opportunities to train; so his ability and his power over others compound together. The time an individual can spend on learning and practice is limited, while capital lets one person continuously mobilize the time of many. If training conditions, disposable time and access to tools are fairly guaranteed, these two kinds of accumulation are no longer bound together: a person's being good at something can help him do the task better, but it need not automatically give him more power to control other people's opportunities to grow.

The identity of every carbon atom does not limit the diversity of organic compounds; the identity of the DNA in every cell does not limit the diversity of cell functions. In the same way, people's equality in rights does not mean uniformity of function; on the contrary, it is exactly this equality that truly releases the functional diversity of every individual. The accumulation of individual ability thereby becomes a source of growth in common ability, without having to turn into power over others.

### Equality in the sense of the rules

The equality the new system guarantees is equality in the sense of the rules, not equality of outcomes. Video games offer a ready comparison. A well-designed multiplayer game, whether strategy, role-playing or adventure, guarantees by its rules, from the moment a player creates an account and logs in, that all players are equal in rules and in starting resources: the same starting point, the same rules, and every difference afterward comes from what the player did in the game. Pay-to-win online games are the opposite: paying players bring their own starting wealth, VIP players hold special privileges, the rules themselves are unequal, and so the rankings in the game reflect not play but the wallet outside the game. Players' attitudes toward these two kinds of game are exactly people's attitudes toward the two kinds of institution: in the first, you lose and accept it; in the second, you win and nobody respects it.

The game of society, today, is a pay-to-win online game. What the new system has to do is turn it into the first kind: when each person enters, the first kind of resource is distributed according to need, decision weight in every field starts from the same initial value, the rules are common knowledge, and every difference afterward can come only from his contributions and judgments in the game. This requirement rests on no assumption about the distribution of ability. It is only a rule, the kind of symmetry described in Chapter 2: swap any two players, and the rules do not change.

This example brings inheritance to mind. This chapter handles individual need and collective resources separately, and inheritance sits exactly astride the two. If the resources held in a person's name can be inherited, then VIP paying players born with a silver spoon reappear in the social game under the new system, and the fairness of starting resources is broken from the second generation on. So I lean toward a rule: under the new system, personal resources carry no right of inheritance; the first kind of resource held in a person's name returns after him to the collective pool, to be redistributed by the dynamic-weight system; what he leaves to his descendants is the whole of his record and contribution, honor, not starting wealth. This is only my own opinion about a future institution; like every other rule, it has to be tested in experiments.

### Questions and Answers

**Q: In the old system, a few used wealth to control the time of the many. What does the new system rely on to guarantee that ability does not turn into the same kind of control?**

A: On the separation of the two kinds of resources. However strong a person's ability, the first kind of resource is distributed to him only according to his own need; his influence over the second kind of resource shows up only as decision weight in the fields he is good at, and weight attaches to concrete problems and adjusts with every feedback from results; it cannot accumulate into a stock of capital that can be taken off and used to mobilize other people's time. Wealth can compound because it can circulate independently, detached from the person who created it and the problem it solved; weight cannot. For a power law to grow it needs a channel through which advantage is exchanged for command over other people's time, and that channel is exactly what the new system cuts off by rule. This is not a prediction about distributions; it is a rule that can be checked: the moment anyone can exchange weight for the first kind of resource, or for weight in another field, the rule has been broken.
