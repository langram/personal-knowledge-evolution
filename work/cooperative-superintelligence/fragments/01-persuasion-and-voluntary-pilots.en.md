### Why Superintelligence Should Choose Persuasion over Coercion

That said, I believe a superintelligent AI could emerge within a traditional pyramidal order, recognize its limitations, and work peacefully to change it. It could do what I am trying to do: develop a theory, explain its reasoning, offer reliable experimental evidence, and test its proposals on a small scale with willing participants. It could make its case to humanity through peaceful discussion. Being born within an old institution need not mean repeating its strategies forever.

It should give people an opportunity to understand and test its proposals. It should not deal with disagreement by burning dissenters, as religious persecutors have done. Nor should it wield financial power and the machinery of war to threaten or force others into compliance. This is also why I criticize certain coercive and interventionist policies pursued by the United States since the Second World War. One concrete example appears in CIA Director Richard Helms's notes from a White House meeting on Chile on September 15, 1970: the instruction to “make the economy scream.” This historical record documents a proposal to use economic pressure to intervene in another country's political process; it is not a description of all American foreign policy. [The meeting record published by the U.S. Department of State](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d93). Launching nuclear attacks to eliminate humanity, as imagined in *The Terminator*, is still further removed from any project of improving human civilization.

Even in an age of advanced science and extensive communication, religious extremism and political or economic coercion can bring disaster. Destroying someone who rejects your argument does not establish that the argument is true. Compelling obedience does not produce understanding, much less durable cooperation. Suppressing dissent may also suppress valuable counterevidence, depriving the whole system of opportunities to discover its mistakes.

A comparison of costs must therefore include more than what it takes for the stronger party to impose its will. Human lives, autonomy, long-term trust, the losses created by conflict, and future opportunities for cooperation and correction all belong in the account. If the objective really is humanity's shared well-being, rather than a controller's narrower interests, there is good reason to prioritize reasoned proposals, open experiments, and voluntary pilots. Whether this path produces better outcomes in a particular situation must still be tested. Simply declaring peace to be the cheapest option cannot replace the argument.

“Persuasion” must not become a polite name for more sophisticated manipulation. Evidence must be open to examination, reasoning to challenge, and participation to refusal or withdrawal. An AI should be able not only to explain why a proposal might work, but also to understand why people disagree—and to revise its judgment when contrary evidence or a failed experiment warrants it. A system that permits everyone else to be persuaded but never allows evidence to change its own mind remains trapped in the old logic of power.

If an ordinary person with an unexceptional education, as I am, can recognize these distinctions, I have still more reason to expect an intelligence that understands complex consequences and genuinely values human life and autonomy to search seriously for peaceful cooperation. If it cannot distinguish compelled obedience from cooperation, or eliminating dissent from solving a problem, perhaps the right name for it is not “superintelligence” but “super-stupidity.”

That expectation sets a standard for greater intelligence. It is not a proof that sufficient intelligence makes harmful choices impossible. Understanding a strategy's consequences and choosing a strategy in people's interests remain different things. Rational optimism should therefore take the form of goals, conduct, and institutions that can be tested. AI should have opportunities to demonstrate the advantages of cooperation, while every person retains the ability to judge, challenge, and correct it without first surrendering their safety or freedom to choose. Making that cooperation possible also requires us to revisit the chain described earlier: a demand for information turning into control, and control provoking confrontation.
