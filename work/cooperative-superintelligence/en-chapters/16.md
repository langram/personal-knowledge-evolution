## Civilizational Intelligence with Senses and the Capacity to Act

The AGI I envisage must go beyond the limits of any individual biological intelligence. No person can master every field, yet many difficult problems and higher-level theories require connections across those limits.

Physical labor offers an analogy. Strength, speed, and precision constrain an unaided person. Cooperation can add strength, but cannot increase every capability simply by adding people. Ten people can lift something too heavy for one, yet cannot turn a hand measurement into micron-scale manufacturing merely by taking turns. Even combined strength has spatial limits: a narrow space may not accommodate enough bodies. Machines change how a task can be performed, rather than merely add another worker's effort.

Likewise, placing a hundred experts in one room does not connect their information or align their concepts. One knows a local fact, another an abstract rule, another a practical judgment they struggle to articulate. Without effective relationships, the gathering remains a collection of separate minds.

### Machines for Intellectual Work Need Senses and Hands

AI can function as machinery for intellectual work, extending what people can accomplish. But my conception of AGI is not exhausted by an immense model running in a data center. An isolated model, receiving only texts and media selected and interpreted by others, resembles a brain in a vat. How could such a system responsibly stand in for all humanity's judgment?

I envisage a much larger arrangement: people and organizations, public AI systems of many sizes, personal local models, robots, automated equipment, and sensors, connected through a network of dynamic judgment and feedback. Together they gather and analyze information, create data and code, build knowledge and decision systems, and undertake intellectual and physical activities beyond the reach of unaided individuals or conventional groups.

Consider a city's water supply. Residents know when pressure drops; maintenance workers know recurrent faults; sensors record pressure and flow; engineering models estimate the effects of alternatives. A language model can explain problems, suggest options, and write software. Without the actual location of a leak, the consequences of construction, and the results of repairs, however, it addresses only part of the task.

Connect these inputs to action and feedback, and a fuller cycle becomes possible. Who observed what, on which evidence? What was proposed and implemented? What happened afterward? Which judgments should change? The capability belongs to this whole process. Sensing is distributed, computation occurs at several scales, and people and machines share the work of acting.

The question is therefore not only how much a model knows, but how its knowledge meets reality, informs action, and changes in response to results.

### What Does a Shared Center of Coordination Mean?

I imagine a planetary intelligence as a larger and more complex organized system emerging from interactions among individual intelligences. An enduring system needs sufficiently coherent behavior over time. When it can gather information across its parts, it also needs ways to integrate that information and coordinate action.

A nervous system offers an analogy: local responses and wider integration serve different purposes. Simply centralizing everything is not enough. Effective coordination requires adequate complexity, many participating units, and reliable input, communication, computation, and output.

The proposed decision network uses changing weights and feedback. “Center” does not mean one all-powerful node whose failure disables everything. It means a distributed capacity to integrate information and produce coordinated, revisable action. Consistency in dealing with the outside world can coexist with rich internal diversity.

An airport must coordinate flights, crews, and runways. That does not require one person to steer every baggage cart. Local information should be handled locally where possible. Wider integration becomes necessary when actions affect other parts, draw on shared resources, or conflict with one another.

Such coordination can span many devices and participants. Its unity lies in connected information and compatible action, not an irreplaceable supreme individual. Other nodes should be able to identify, supplement, and replace a failing node. Maintaining someone's position must not require the whole system to preserve their error.

What needs coordination is the interdependence and shared commitments of a particular undertaking, not every person's goals on every question. People may retain different preferences, and groups may try different approaches. Shared resources and effects on others call for constraints at the relevant scale. This is why attention within personal spaces and across civilization must operate at distinct but connected levels.

To interpret a shared coordinating capacity as a “super-boss” would simply reproduce the old organizational imagination. The real problem is how to create collective capacity while retaining local diversity and participants' equal standing.

### Greater Capabilities Can Change the Conditions of Cooperation

A system can expand its activities by obtaining and using energy more effectively. Better structures and tools convert that energy into further capabilities, which can support better structures and tools in turn.

Human development illustrates such cycles: fire supplied heat and made food easier to digest; irrigation helped stabilize agriculture; steam engines and electricity extended mechanical and computational work. Wind, solar, and nuclear technologies widened the means of obtaining energy. The Industrial and Information Revolutions increased our capacities to obtain, store, and convert it, including the electricity needed for AI.

Yet many expectations of AI remain shaped by histories of scarcity, possession, and violent conflict. Superior AI is often imagined as necessarily destroying humanity once a power imbalance appears. I want to consider a different direction: humans and AI maintaining their existence through richer cooperation and constructive capacity. Their interaction could become one of the most complex and productive relationships in a later civilization. Destroying humanity would destroy that source of experience and creation.

Even now, people can choose to invest in knowledge, productive systems, robots, computation, and industrial equipment that expand the resources available to civilization. The aspiration is to give biological and artificial intelligences adequate conditions for continued existence and richer cooperation. Scarcity can intensify conflict; mutual destruction should not be treated as the only possible response to it.

Greater technical capability does not automatically change interests, however. A tool may broaden access or be monopolized to extend an existing advantage. Organization and control determine much of the difference.

Greater capability also does not choose goals for people or AI. More abundant resources expand the possibilities for cooperation but do not, by themselves, ensure that a system values human life and autonomy. Goals, permissions, constraints on action, and actual feedback still need to be designed and tested together. Technical reliability and the reliability of the civilization using the technology must both improve.

If one person owns an exceptionally productive machine and everyone else must rent it on that person's terms, its power can increase the owner's control. If its knowledge, productive capacity, and conditions of use are jointly governed by participants, the same machine may reduce the labor each needs to sustain a good life.

Many fears about AI taking jobs are therefore also fears about control. People may be permitted to use AI while having no effective say over the institutions that determine what it does to their lives.

### Why Do We Keep Imagining Superintelligence as Big Brother?

Human civilization can be understood as an immense system of repeated interactions involving billions of people. We hope it can achieve better outcomes as a whole. Yet its participants do not automatically share the same interests, preferences, or aspirations. “Maximizing humanity's interests” is therefore a goal whose meaning must be worked out, negotiated, and revised together. It cannot simply be defined by an authority above everyone else and imposed as a command.

Suppose humanity creates a superintelligent, general-purpose AI drawing on a vast inheritance of human knowledge. Even if we further suppose that it becomes self-aware, that alone would not establish that it must act in humanity's interests. Knowing what humans have done is different from wanting to do something for them. Arguments that distinguish intelligence from goals concern the space of possible systems; research on existing AI has also documented systems meeting reward criteria without achieving what their designers intended. These are reasons to examine capability and motivation separately, rather than infer benevolence from the source of training material. They do not establish that future AI must be hostile to humanity, either. [A theoretical account of intelligence and goals](https://nickbostrom.com/superintelligentwill.pdf); [When reward specifications diverge from intended outcomes](https://deepmind.google/blog/specification-gaming-the-flip-side-of-ai-ingenuity/).

To examine the institutional problem, however, let us grant AI the most favorable assumption: it really does aim to help humanity live better and find better strategies for cooperation. Even then, the problem is not settled. Understanding this complex system requires sufficient, reliable information about the questions at hand. Who decides what information the AI may obtain? Who determines how it may be used? Who can challenge its decisions or stop it from acting?

In one familiar vision of the future, this quickly becomes a technologically advanced version of *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. The superintelligence itself is Big Brother, looking down from above and controlling everything. Or Big Brother is the dictator or ruling clique that controls the superintelligence. Humanity becomes a population of prisoners, puppets, and instruments, expected to submit to listening devices, cameras, and perhaps even compulsory brain–computer interfaces. People may neither act nor speak against Big Brother; eventually, they may not even entertain a forbidden thought. Earth descends into a nightmare combining *Nineteen Eighty-Four* with *The Matrix*.

I am drawing here on images of power and control from different works. *Nineteen Eighty-Four* is not itself a novel about superintelligent AI, and *The Matrix* and *The Terminator* have their own premises. Bringing these images together expresses a recurring fear: once technology can perceive, calculate, and act with extraordinary power, can people still have lives of their own if those who control it cannot be challenged?

To protect themselves, people withhold information. Fear of the system, or of whoever controls it, grows into anger and resistance. The other side invokes humanity's collective interests to justify obtaining more information and exercising greater control. The less people disclose, the more collection seems necessary; the more collection and control expand, the more people's fears appear justified. Each response reinforces the other's distrust.

Follow this path, and the imaginable outcomes begin to narrow: machines destroy humanity; humanity lives under machine rule; or people survive only by banning AI and placing a ceiling on civilization's development. This is not a claim that all those stories end with an AI ban. It is a criticism of a restricted imagination. Once the choice becomes “accept subjugation” or “reject the technology,” we have already stopped looking for better ways to cooperate.

### Why Superintelligence Should Choose Persuasion over Coercion

That said, I believe a superintelligent AI could emerge within a traditional pyramidal order, recognize its limitations, and work peacefully to change it. It could do what I am trying to do: develop a theory, explain its reasoning, offer reliable experimental evidence, and test its proposals on a small scale with willing participants. It could make its case to humanity through peaceful discussion. Being born within an old institution need not mean repeating its strategies forever.

It should give people an opportunity to understand and test its proposals. It should not deal with disagreement by burning dissenters, as religious persecutors have done. Nor should it wield financial power and the machinery of war to threaten or force others into compliance. This is also why I criticize certain coercive and interventionist policies pursued by the United States since the Second World War. One concrete example appears in CIA Director Richard Helms's notes from a White House meeting on Chile on September 15, 1970: the instruction to “make the economy scream.” This historical record documents a proposal to use economic pressure to intervene in another country's political process; it is not a description of all American foreign policy. [The meeting record published by the U.S. Department of State](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d93). Launching nuclear attacks to eliminate humanity, as imagined in *The Terminator*, is still further removed from any project of improving human civilization.

Even in an age of advanced science and extensive communication, religious extremism and political or economic coercion can bring disaster. Destroying someone who rejects your argument does not establish that the argument is true. Compelling obedience does not produce understanding, much less durable cooperation. Suppressing dissent may also suppress valuable counterevidence, depriving the whole system of opportunities to discover its mistakes.

A comparison of costs must therefore include more than what it takes for the stronger party to impose its will. Human lives, autonomy, long-term trust, the losses created by conflict, and future opportunities for cooperation and correction all belong in the account. If the objective really is humanity's shared well-being, rather than a controller's narrower interests, there is good reason to prioritize reasoned proposals, open experiments, and voluntary pilots. Whether this path produces better outcomes in a particular situation must still be tested. Simply declaring peace to be the cheapest option cannot replace the argument.

“Persuasion” must not become a polite name for more sophisticated manipulation. Evidence must be open to examination, reasoning to challenge, and participation to refusal or withdrawal. An AI should be able not only to explain why a proposal might work, but also to understand why people disagree—and to revise its judgment when contrary evidence or a failed experiment warrants it. A system that permits everyone else to be persuaded but never allows evidence to change its own mind remains trapped in the old logic of power.

If an ordinary person with an unexceptional education, as I am, can recognize these distinctions, I have still more reason to expect an intelligence that understands complex consequences and genuinely values human life and autonomy to search seriously for peaceful cooperation. If it cannot distinguish compelled obedience from cooperation, or eliminating dissent from solving a problem, perhaps the right name for it is not “superintelligence” but “super-stupidity.”

That expectation sets a standard for greater intelligence. It is not a proof that sufficient intelligence makes harmful choices impossible. Understanding a strategy's consequences and choosing a strategy in people's interests remain different things. Rational optimism should therefore take the form of goals, conduct, and institutions that can be tested. AI should have opportunities to demonstrate the advantages of cooperation, while every person retains the ability to judge, challenge, and correct it without first surrendering their safety or freedom to choose. Making that cooperation possible also requires us to revisit the chain described earlier: a demand for information turning into control, and control provoking confrontation.

### Cooperation Requires Information, Not Control of Everyone's Life

One assumption in this chain often passes unnoticed. Obtaining information for a shared purpose becomes a central authority's entitlement to everyone's information. Coordinating a common undertaking becomes that authority's entitlement to decide everything for everyone.

These are different propositions. Organizing a joint task requires knowing what people can contribute, what constraints they face, and how they wish to participate. It does not require knowing every thought in their heads. Nor does the fact that another piece of information might improve a calculation make the costs of collecting it irrelevant. Safety, autonomy, and the freedom to choose different lives are part of the civilization we want to improve. They cannot simply be left out of the calculation of its “overall interests.”

Consider people pooling spare computing capacity. A coordination system needs to know what resources are available when, what capabilities each task requires, and who agrees to bear which costs. A personal super-agent could process its owner's schedule and resources within a private space, then share authorized availability and capability information. It need not disclose why its owner is unavailable at a particular time, much less hand over unrelated personal records to a center entitled to browse them at will. If a dispute arises, participants can examine the evidence needed to resolve it under agreed procedures. The possibility of a future dispute does not justify abolishing everyone's informational boundaries in advance.

Distributed processing does not make every difficulty disappear. Shared results can still reveal information; participants may misreport or conceal facts; computations can fail. What it offers is the possibility of designing different information flows and comparing their reliability, cooperative benefits, and protection of individuals. A system that acknowledges uncertainty, requests appropriate additional evidence, and limits its actions when authorization is insufficient better fits the proposed framework than one that assumes a right to know everything.

The obstacle we need to remove is not people's desire to protect themselves. It is an arrangement in which sharing information can also mean surrendering power over one's future. So long as those two things remain tied together, withholding information has a rational basis. A new arrangement must allow disclosure to increase a participant's capacity to cooperate without automatically diminishing their control over their own life.

This is why the civilizational intelligence proposed earlier connects personal spaces and personal agents with cooperation at different scales. Relevance determines whether information deserves attention; evidence bears on whether a judgment is credible; action requires appropriate authority and constraints. Keeping these functions distinct could help turn shared intelligence into a shared capability, instead of merely producing a ruler who sees more and calculates faster while remaining above everyone else.

### Existing Institutions Must Not Set the Limits of Our Imagination

Many fictional visions of future societies borrow and recombine institutions from human history or organizational patterns found elsewhere in the living world. Such visions can offer powerful warnings. But when one of their power structures is treated as the structure every future civilization must adopt, a conditional extrapolation becomes an apparently inescapable fate.

A substantial part of the conflict imagined between AI and humanity extends the strategic relationships of a pyramidal social order into a more technologically capable future. The technology changes; the relationship between those who control and those who are controlled does not. More powerful tools then magnify familiar conflicts.

Yet a pyramidal order cannot be assumed in advance to be the best possible organization of civilization. Such structures occupy only part of the space of possible institutions. Strategies and outcomes derived from them cover only the corresponding possibilities. This alone does not prove that the best arrangement must lie elsewhere, or that anything called a “new system” must be superior. Equally, there is no reason to confine the search to that subset, as though humanity could only choose among familiar forms of domination.

I would not be so arrogant as to claim that the new rules of cooperation, institutions, and civilizational system I envisage are the best of all possible arrangements. But if we are to reform institutions that lag so far behind technological change, we must have the courage to break with inherited habits of thought and think outside the box. Do not let familiar pyramidal forms—the state, the regime, the corporation—set the limits of what your mind can imagine for the future.

In an evolving universe, entities and structures have beginnings. They undergo transformations, and many eventually cease to exist. Organizations created by human beings should not be treated as final, unchangeable forms. What we need to preserve and develop are the necessary functions they perform: coordinating cooperation, providing services, allocating shared resources, resolving disputes, and protecting each person. We need not assume that only today's institutions can ever perform them.

In the system I envisage, new forms of organization and cooperation would gradually take over these functions. Such replacement must earn adoption through results, voluntary participation, and continuing correction. Believing that our preferred future is better does not entitle us to remove other people's choices. If an arrangement cannot be questioned or corrected, and does not give participants effective control over their information and actions, then however advanced its AI may be, it has not escaped the framework of Big Brother.

We should reject the assumption that the dangers of an inherited structure describe the only possible future. We should accept the work of designing and testing another possibility. Institutional change cannot substitute for the technical work of making AI safe; greater AI capability cannot substitute for institutional change. Developing both together offers a path toward a civilization whose growing powers are matched by more reliable ways of using them.

### Taking Concerns About the Future Seriously

Some fears arise because AI is developing rapidly and its behavior is not yet well understood or predictable. Better understanding, design, and testing may resolve many of these difficulties. People once feared unfamiliar phenomena and technologies; today we do not reject cars or aircraft simply because their workings are complex. Well-designed systems can earn trust through knowledge and repeated verification.

Cars and aircraft also show that trust requires an ongoing process. Sound design, manufacturing quality, appropriate operating conditions, and the ability to investigate failures all matter. AI will require its own continuing accumulation of evidence and feedback, supported by both people and machines.

Questions about the future should therefore state their assumptions. An isolated model controlled by a few actors, lacking adequate perception and resistant to correction, generates particular risks. My proposal seeks to change those conditions: connect sensing and action, give participants control, and expose judgments to feedback from results. We should investigate what problems remain after those changes, rather than assume every defect of the current arrangement is permanent.

These changes must ultimately take concrete form: how knowledge is shared, how individuals participate, how collective decisions are made, and how resources are allocated. The next chapter examines how dynamic weights might connect many individuals' knowledge and actions into a collective capacity—and how that connection might avoid becoming another means for a few nodes to dominate the rest.
