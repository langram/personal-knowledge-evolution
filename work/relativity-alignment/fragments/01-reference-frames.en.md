## Relativity, Cognitive Frames, and a Mathematical Language for Social Science

### Where are we observing from?

Two people stand at opposite ends of a street. One says a car is approaching; the other says it is moving away. Neither need be mistaken. The question is: approaching whom, and moving away from whom? Once we specify their positions, directions of observation, and times of measurement, apparently conflicting descriptions can become parts of a single account.

Suppose, however, that one person says the car has struck a pedestrian and the other says no collision occurred. Saying that everyone has a different frame of reference will not settle this disagreement. We need to examine the scene, timing, recordings, and other evidence. The first difference may concern how an event is described; the second concerns what happened. Bringing relativity into questions of knowledge begins with distinguishing the two.

A frame of reference has physical content and requires mathematical expression. Which instruments observers use, how they synchronize clocks, and what they measure motion relative to are physical questions. How they label events and translate between descriptions are mathematical questions. In special relativity, the principle of relativity requires consistency of physical laws across inertial frames. It does not make every observation, measurement, or guess equally correct. Einstein made measurement and transformation rules explicit so that physical relationships could remain open to shared tests. [Einstein's 1905 paper](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys225/sp2026/Einstein1905.pdf)

I want to take the question further. Across physics, chemistry, biology, cognition, and society, can we learn to distinguish differences in objects themselves from differences in circumstances, and both from differences in the way objects are described?

This does not require every field to use the same equations. Choosing a reference value for an energy calculation in chemistry is not the same kind of relativity as an organism's ability to survive in a particular environment. The former may involve a representational convention; the latter concerns a change in actual outcomes when conditions change. A trait that helps in one environment may hinder in another. That is more than a change of coordinates: the conditions of survival have changed. Connections across fields become useful when these distinctions remain visible.

### What foundations should social science share?

Social science and natural science should rest on the same underlying foundations, rather than invoke additional rules or supernatural elements—a God-given consciousness or soul standing outside the universe's evolutionary processes. Our bodies, sensations, memories, language, and cooperation do not inhabit a separate world outside the universe. When our subject becomes human, we should not turn the very phenomena that need explanation into exceptions we cannot investigate.

The research stance of this book is to explain phenomena through mechanisms that can be tested, while allowing new evidence to change those explanations. It does not require settling everyone's religious beliefs in advance. Nor does it mean that a physical equation can replace psychology, economics, or institutional research. As relationships become more complex at higher levels of organization, we need objects, variables, boundaries, and methods of measurement suited to those levels.

Some foundational disputes in social science remind me of natural philosophy before Newton, with an urgent need for a common basis of fundamental concepts and mathematical principles. That basis should extend the foundations of natural science. There is abundant experience and there are many explanations, yet profound disagreements remain over what concepts mean, how explanations connect, and how conclusions can be assessed together. I hope to find foundations that make these bodies of inquiry more connected.

The analogy must not erase existing achievements. Social scientists already use statistics, game theory, causal analysis, and network models, and have established many testable findings. The task is not to start from nothing by declaring previous knowledge worthless. It is to help separate bodies of knowledge state their assumptions, scales, and limits more clearly, so they can contribute to better social arrangements.

We might understand this project as a search for coordinates and reference frames in social science. How do we describe a person's circumstances, compare different judgments, identify shared facts, and trace changes brought about by interaction? The inspiration comes from the investigative spirit of Cartesian coordinates, Newtonian frames, and Einstein's relativity. It is not a claim to have discovered equations that transfer physical relativity directly into society.

### What semantic spaces make possible

AI has enabled computing devices built from silicon to perform a growing range of tasks once performed by human intelligence. This gives concrete support to an important possibility: language, knowledge processing, and some forms of reasoning can be realized through mechanisms we can study and construct. Problems in human society can likewise be approached through further mathematical modeling and scientific experiments.

Large language models, in particular, allow semantic relationships in extensive bodies of language to be represented computationally in high-dimensional spaces. We can compare different expressions, identify similarities and differences, and follow changes in a concept across contexts. Those representations may help us construct and revise social models. This is an important technical condition for the study of cognition that I have in mind.

Two distinctions matter here. First, mathematical models of language and cognition did not begin with today's large models. Research on latent semantic analysis in 1997 already used high-dimensional representations to study words, texts, and knowledge acquisition. Second, representing relationships among language samples is not the same as fully measuring a person's consciousness. Unspoken experience, bodily sensation, emotion, and motivation do not become fully known merely because a model produces a vector. [Landauer and Dumais on latent semantic analysis](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~cshalizi/350/2008/readings/Landauer-Dumais.pdf)

The cognitive space I propose should therefore begin with material we can work with: statements, explanations, choices, and relevant circumstances that people voluntarily provide. We can record and analyze them to build approximate models of particular cognitive relationships. We must test whether the dimensions mean what we think they mean, whether measurements are repeatable, and whether conclusions survive a change of model or language. An internal axis of one model cannot simply be declared humanity's universal axis of justice or civilizational advancement.

### How cognitive frames take shape

People's worldviews, theories of knowledge, political leanings, moral beliefs, and ways of understanding occupy different positions and follow different tendencies across dimensions of thought and action. These differences have histories: personal experience, environment, access to information, and the influences to which a person has been exposed. A view does not arise from nowhere. Nor does it become absolutely correct merely because it is mine.

In this sense, I think cognition that begins with one's own experience can be examined within a broader evolutionary picture. A system capable of sensing and acting needs to distinguish what matters to it and what affects its continued existence, then extend its understanding through interaction. Whether some version of this is a basic feature of every system capable of evolution and emergence remains a question to investigate case by case. Human experience alone cannot establish a law for the entire universe.

If we regard present language models as high-dimensional representations of human linguistic knowledge, we can picture language and action within this cognitive space by analogy with movement through physical spacetime. This is an image of movement and change, not an assumption that the two obey the same equations. Different cognitive frames can create distance, but explanation can also build trust. Interactions among people with different frames then generate more complex structures and properties within the wider frame of society and civilization. Those structures, in turn, affect individuals by shaping the information they encounter and the habits and judgments they develop.

Two things are happening at once: we change our descriptions of the world, and we change the world through action. Someone who understands another group as an enemy may refuse communication. That refusal makes misunderstandings harder to correct and changes the actual possibilities for cooperation. A social model therefore needs more than static coordinates for opinions. It must track changes in information, action, resources, and rules over time. This is why the book later moves from semantic objects to a civilization considered as a running system.

### Relativity does not abolish facts or responsibility

If understanding begins from different positions, does that mean nothing can be right or wrong, good or bad?

What I oppose is the habit of treating one's own cognitive frame as an absolute standard, declaring everything inconsistent with it mistaken, and then turning other people into inherent enemies or demons. But rejecting the substitution of one's own position for a universal standard does not imply that all factual judgments are equally reliable or all actions equally acceptable.

We can understand the circumstances in which someone developed a fear while correcting the factual judgment that fear produced. We can explain why someone harmed another person while still stopping the harm, providing redress, and establishing responsibility. Explaining how an action arose and allowing it to continue are different questions.

Consider a dispute over a public facility. One person sees easier transport; another faces noise, displacement, or higher living costs. Calling the first progressive and the second selfish obscures the problem. Calling the first exploitative and the second righteous can do the same. Record who gains convenience, who bears the costs, and what alternatives exist. The disagreement can then move from opposing identities toward questions that can be investigated.

Even with adequate information, however, real conflicts of interest may remain. Translating two people's words cannot make a limited resource satisfy every demand at once. We still need shared rules, comparison of alternatives, protection of basic rights, and arrangements for compensation. Relativity can help reveal the structure of disagreement. It does not solve allocation by itself.

### Bringing moral judgment back to evidence and shared rules

Human thought and behavior should be open to observation, investigation, recording, and analysis, just as natural phenomena are. Where measurement permits, we should also attempt quantification and modeling. Mystification, sanctification, demonization, and emotional labels should not close off further inquiry.

Poetry and fiction can give us a gentle breeze, yielding grass, sacred light, or demonic darkness. Such language has literary and emotional value. But a scientific account of light cannot replace an explanation of its propagation with the claim that darkness is evil. We should bring the same discipline to social inquiry. Declaring a person or institution sacred cannot exempt it from evidence; declaring an opponent evil cannot remove the need to understand how that opponent came to act as they do.

This does not require people to have no emotions, or public speech to contain no rhetoric. Fear, anger, compassion, and hope are themselves real phenomena worth understanding. What we must avoid is treating them as untested causal conclusions, or as grounds for denying someone the chance to explain, challenge, or participate. Political action affects real people. One group's sense of sacred purpose cannot stand in for another group's consent and safety.

We can still uphold basic commitments to preventing harm, equal participation, and personal autonomy. But we should state their scope, explain how conflicts among them will be handled, identify who interprets them, and allow revision. The identity of the rule-maker cannot be the final proof. Someone who rejects much of my worldview can still test a fact with me or honor a cooperative agreement. That offers a more practicable foundation than requiring everyone to inhabit the same inner world first.

### Turning mathematical terms into usable structures

Vectors, tensors, objects and morphisms, duality, symmetry, fixed points, maximization, and minimization can all supply tools for inquiry. Listing them together will not create a new science. Whenever we use one, we need to say what it refers to, what operations are permitted, what relationships those operations preserve, and how we would discover that the concept does not fit.

|Mathematical concept|A possible use in social inquiry|Questions that must be answered|
|---|---|---|
|Dimensions and vectors|Describe needs, resources, judgments, or risks through several quantities in a specified task|How is each quantity measured, in what units, and with what omissions? A vector is not a whole person.|
|Tensors|Represent multidimensional relationships with specified transformation properties after defining the relevant spaces|Not every multidimensional table has the mathematical properties implied by calling it a tensor.|
|Objects, morphisms, and categories|Treat knowledge objects, states, or protocols as objects, and permitted transformations as morphisms|Can transformations be composed? Are there identity transformations? Does composition satisfy the stated rules?|
|Symmetry and relativity|Test whether relevant judgments remain consistent when names, units, or representations change|Which changes merely redescribe something, and which alter circumstances, rights, or facts?|
|Duality and complementary aspects|Study correspondences between structured descriptions, or connections between knowledge and intelligence, data and programs|A mathematical duality needs a definition. Two related concepts do not automatically establish a duality theorem.|
|Fixed points, equilibria, and extrema|Describe an update that leaves a state unchanged, strategies that respond to one another, or an objective reaching an extremum under constraints|What are the update, payoffs, and constraints? Stability, effectiveness, fairness, and optimality are different properties.|

For example, suppose two groups want help clarifying a disagreement. We can first specify a task, denote their voluntarily supplied accounts and circumstances by $h_i,c_i$, and use an explicitly versioned representation method $E_i$ to obtain $x_i=E_i(h_i,c_i)$. Here $x_i$ is a representation for this task. It neither defines the person nor measures the coordinates of a soul.

We can then try to construct an interpretive relation $T_{ij}$ that restates one group's account in language the other can understand. The tests can be straightforward. Does the first group recognize its meaning? Can the second now restate it more accurately? Can both propose the same checks for factual claims? If the correspondence is incomplete, record what is missing. If the restatement changes the meaning, retain that failure. Writing down $T_{ij}$ does not make it necessarily invertible, lossless, or endowed with all the properties of a geometric transformation.

Next comes a further question: did better understanding improve actual cooperation? Sometimes clearing up a misunderstanding makes a revised plan possible. Sometimes both sides understand each other perfectly and still lack enough resources to meet their wishes. That calls for negotiation and allocation. Adding model parameters while pretending every conflict is semantic will not solve it.

### From cognitive frames to shared construction

I hope to build a mechanism through which different understandings can be interpreted, checked together, and developed further. It should not be a center that assigns everyone the correct coordinates. A Merkle-DAG can preserve original statements, sources, conditions, versions, and revisions, so that a model's interpretation cannot silently overwrite what someone actually said. Models and interpretations may change while earlier statements and disagreements remain recoverable.

Shared records do not require every private experience to be made public. If an authorized summary, local computation, or limited disclosure can solve a problem, there is no need to demand a whole life history. As cognitive models become more capable, we need greater clarity about who may use them, for what purpose, and how the people represented can correct them or withdraw.

This leads to a more concrete question about cooperation, conflict, and social structure. Can people with different knowledge, needs, and preferences retain those differences while building rules for action they can trust together? Relativity helps us abandon the habit of placing ourselves at the absolute center. Shared tests keep us from becoming trapped in worlds that cannot communicate. Institutional construction must then turn understanding into durable cooperation. Connecting those steps is what I seek from a mathematical foundation for social science.
