### Keeping relativity open to tests

Extending the idea of reference frames into cognition and society can generate useful questions. It has not yet produced an empirically established social theory of relativity. We must examine separately whether we are redescribing the same fact, changing environmental conditions, investigating how people understand the world, or proposing rules for living together. These questions connect, but no single answer settles them all.

Semantic vectors are representations shaped by materials, training methods, and tasks. They are not complete measurements of consciousness. A feasible small experiment could ask willing participants to explain the same disagreement independently, then compare ordinary restatement with model-assisted restatement. Does misunderstanding decrease? Is the original meaning preserved? Do the results hold on material not used to construct the method? The record must also include cases where the model is unhelpful, mistaken, or obscures a real conflict of interest. A shorter vector distance does not itself demonstrate greater fairness, trust, or safety.

Likewise, calling cognitive differences curvature, trust parallel transport, or a change of perspective a functor provides, at most, suggestive metaphors unless the objects, relationships, and testable structures are defined. Mathematical terminology cannot supply a proof for a model that has not been constructed. The aim should be to make relationships among descriptions open to examination, rather than place people in coordinates that look precise but cannot be challenged.

### Evidence for stable alignment and for continuing correction

Representing alignment as a fixed point of an update operator is a research proposal. It needs a state space, permitted updates, environmental assumptions, participants' rights, and criteria for success. Existence, convergence, recovery after disturbance, and compliance with shared constraints each require their own proof or experimental assessment. A durable structure of domination is a counterexample to the inference that stability means goodness.

A starting point could be cooperation over resources at a small scale. Compare agents optimizing their own local scores with agents operating under shared limits on harm, public commitments, and correction procedures. Test both under imperfect information, unequal capabilities, and changing resources. Record the distribution of gains, incidents of loss, time to repair, effectiveness of withdrawal, and total costs. Retain counterexamples involving refusal to cooperate or collusion. Simulations can help choose further experiments; they cannot establish that a mechanism can safely be applied to the entire world.

Coordination among people can help clarify AI's objectives and authority, but it cannot replace technical safety tests. Ecological constraints likewise cannot be represented solely by scores that current human participants happen to endorse. The common foundation sought by Nova Principia should make these gaps clearer, failures easier to expose, and correction easier to continue. Giving the problems a shared name is not grounds for declaring them solved.
